Critics often cite *Sūrat al-Nisāʾ* 4:34—especially the word ***waḍribūhunna***—as evidence that Islam permits domestic abuse. Yet the disputed term has more than one reading, the verse sets out a graduated response to serious marital conflict, and even jurists who understood the verb physically prohibited harm.

## The verse in question

> <div dir="rtl" lang="ar" class="quran-arabic">﴿ٱلرِّجَالُ قَوَّٰمُونَ عَلَى ٱلنِّسَآءِ بِمَا فَضَّلَ ٱللَّهُ بَعْضَهُمْ عَلَىٰ بَعْضٍ وَبِمَآ أَنفَقُوا۟ مِنْ أَمْوَٰلِهِمْ ۚ فَٱلصَّـٰلِحَـٰتُ قَـٰنِتَـٰتٌ حَـٰفِظَـٰتٌ لِّلْغَيْبِ بِمَا حَفِظَ ٱللَّهُ ۚ وَٱلَّـٰتِى تَخَافُونَ نُشُوزَهُنَّ فَعِظُوهُنَّ وَٱهْجُرُوهُنَّ فِى ٱلْمَضَاجِعِ وَٱضْرِبُوهُنَّ ۖ فَإِنْ أَطَعْنَكُمْ فَلَا تَبْغُوا۟ عَلَيْهِنَّ سَبِيلًا ۗ إِنَّ ٱللَّهَ كَانَ عَلِيًّا كَبِيرًا ۝٣٤﴾ <span class="ayah-ref">سُورَةُ النِّسَاءِ</span></div>
{: .quote }

## 1. What does *waḍribūhunna* mean?

The Arabic verb ***ḍaraba*** has a broad range of meanings. It can mean *to strike*, but also *to travel, separate, set forth,* or *distance*. Early scholars—including al-Tabari—recognized more than one possible reading, and several interpreted ***waḍribūhunna*** as *“separate from them”* or *“part ways.”*

Other scholars understood the verb physically, but still did not permit harm. They restricted the act to a symbolic, non-injurious form.

## 2. A graduated response to serious marital conflict

Qur'an 4:34 gives a three-step sequence for responding to serious marital discord:

1. ***Faʿiẓūhunna*** (<span dir="rtl" lang="ar">فَعِظُوهُنَّ</span>) — counsel and advise.
2. ***Wahjurūhunna fī'l-maḍājiʿ*** (<span dir="rtl" lang="ar">وَٱهْجُرُوهُنَّ فِى ٱلْمَضَاجِعِ</span>) — withdraw from the marital bed, signaling disapproval without hostility.
3. ***Waḍribūhunna*** (<span dir="rtl" lang="ar">وَٱضْرِبُوهُنَّ</span>) — understood either as separation or, at most, a symbolic act devoid of harm.

The sequence moves from counsel to withdrawal and then to separation or a harmless symbolic act, progressively containing conflict rather than causing injury.

If the marriage approaches breakdown, the immediately following verse calls for arbitration from both families:

> *“Appoint an arbiter from his people and an arbiter from hers.”* (**Sūrat al-Nisāʾ 4:35**)
>
> <div dir="rtl" lang="ar" class="quran-arabic">﴿فَٱبْعَثُوا۟ حَكَمًا مِّنْ أَهْلِهِۦ وَحَكَمًا مِّنْ أَهْلِهَآ﴾ <span class="ayah-ref">[سُورَةُ النِّسَاءِ: ٣٥]</span></div>
{: .quote }

## 3. The Prophet's example

The Qur'an presents the Prophet ﷺ as an example for believers: *“Indeed, in the Messenger of God you have an excellent example”* (**Sūrat al-Aḥzāb 33:21**). He never struck a woman or servant.

> *“The Messenger of God never struck anything with his hand, neither a woman nor a servant…”* (Sahih Muslim).
{: .quote }

He instructed Muslims to treat women well and, when women complained that their husbands had beaten them, said of those husbands: *“Those are not the best among you.”* (Abu Dawud, Ibn Majah).

## 4. How jurists restricted the physical reading

Classical jurists who understood ***ḍaraba*** physically imposed severe restrictions. The act had to be symbolic and non-harmful: it could not cause pain, leave a mark, touch the face or another sensitive area, or become habitual. Many jurists regarded it as ***makrūh***—morally repugnant, though technically allowed.

Even the physical reading did not amount to an unrestricted authorization to strike or abuse a wife.

## 5. The Qur'an and Sunnah prohibit marital harm

The Qur'an commands, *“Live with them in kindness”* (**Sūrat al-Nisāʾ 4:19**), forbids retaining women in marriage in order to harm them (**Sūrat al-Baqarah 2:231**), and describes marriage in terms of tranquility, love, mercy, and solemn commitment (**Sūrat al-Rūm 30:21**).

> <div dir="rtl" lang="ar" class="quran-arabic">﴿وَعَاشِرُوهُنَّ بِٱلْمَعْرُوفِ﴾ <span class="ayah-ref">[سُورَةُ النِّسَاءِ: ١٩]</span></div>
{: .quote }

> <div dir="rtl" lang="ar" class="quran-arabic">﴿وَلَا تُمْسِكُوهُنَّ ضِرَارًا لِّتَعْتَدُوا۟﴾ <span class="ayah-ref">[سُورَةُ البَقَرَةِ: ٢٣١]</span></div>
{: .quote }

> <div dir="rtl" lang="ar" class="quran-arabic">﴿وَمِنْ ءَايَـٰتِهِۦٓ أَنْ خَلَقَ لَكُم مِّنْ أَنفُسِكُمْ أَزْوَٰجًا لِّتَسْكُنُوٓا۟ إِلَيْهَا وَجَعَلَ بَيْنَكُم مَّوَدَّةً وَرَحْمَةً﴾ <span class="ayah-ref">[سُورَةُ الرُّومِ: ٢١]</span></div>
{: .quote }

A general legal-ethical maxim states: <span dir="rtl" lang="ar">لا ضرر ولا ضرار</span> — *“No harm and no reciprocating harm.”* Abuse is treated as ***ḍarar*** (harm) and ***ẓulm*** (oppression), which the Qur'an and Sunnah repeatedly condemn as a grave sin.

Mercy, compassion, and love recur throughout the Qur'an. Every chapter except one begins by invoking God as *the Most Merciful, the Most Compassionate*; the Prophet's mission is described as *“a mercy to the worlds”* (**Sūrat al-Anbiyāʾ 21:107**); God's mercy *“encompasses all things”* (**Sūrat al-Aʿrāf 7:156**); and the Qur'an says that God loves those who rely on Him (**Sūrat Āl ʿImrān 3:159**) and those who do good (**Sūrat al-Baqarah 2:195**). Cruelty, abuse, and oppression conflict with these principles.

## 6. Legal remedies and misuse

Islamic law recognizes harm (***ḍarar***), abandonment, or cruelty as grounds for legal remedies, including ***tafrīq*** (court-ordered separation) or ***khulʿ***; an aggressor may also face damages or discipline. Conditions in a marriage contract (***shurūṭ***) can further protect rights and permit dissolution when those conditions are breached. A husband who harms his wife is answerable before God and the courts.

Using Qur'an 4:34 to justify abuse misuses the verse, just as laws and scriptures have been misused in other traditions to excuse wrongdoing. Islamic law provides remedies for such harm.

## 7. Ibn Ashur's modern reading

Ibn Ashur (d. 1973) understood the verse as a form of deterrence rather than permission to cause harm and held that modern circumstances call for separation. The disputed expression has not been understood only in the single, obvious sense often attributed to it.

## Conclusion

Qur'an 4:34 does not authorize domestic abuse. ***Ḍaraba*** admits more than one reading; the verse presents a graduated response to serious marital discord; the following verse turns to arbitration; and even jurists who accepted a physical reading prohibited harm.

The Prophet ﷺ never struck a woman. The Qur'an and Islamic law require kindness, condemn harm, and provide remedies when harm occurs. Qur'an 4:34 concerns the restrained handling of serious marital conflict, not permission to abuse.
