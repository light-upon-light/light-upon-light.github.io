Classical Islamic law prescribes death for apostasy — a Muslim leaving Islam.
**This is severe, it is in the sources, and an article that begins by minimising
it is not worth reading.**

What follows sets out what the Qur'an actually prescribes, what the hadith
actually say, and where Muslim scholars have disagreed — because they have
disagreed sharply, from the second century of Islam to the present, and
presenting the death penalty as a settled consensus misrepresents the tradition
as badly as any hostile account does.

The severe corporal and capital punishments for theft, adultery and other
offences — the *hudud* — are a related but distinct question, treated at
[The Hudud Punishments in Islam](/punishments).

**A note on terms.** *Ridda* is apostasy: a Muslim leaving Islam. *Hiraba* is
armed robbery or brigandage — violent predation on travellers and communities —
and matters below because one case of apostates actually executed in the
Prophet's ﷺ lifetime involved it. A *hadith* is a report of what the Prophet ﷺ
said or did, transmitted with a named chain and graded for reliability; *Sahih
al-Bukhari* and *Sahih Muslim* are the most rigorously screened collections, and
reports graded *da'if* are weak and cannot carry a legal ruling on their own.

## The objection

1. Islam kills people for changing their minds about God, which is
   incompatible with freedom of conscience at the most basic level.
2. This is not a fringe reading. It is in the canonical hadith collections and
   in all four schools of law.
3. Muslim-majority states enforce it today, so this is a live harm and not a
   historical curiosity.

Point 2 is where the objection is strongest and also where it is least
accurate. Point 3 is largely true and is dealt with at the end.

## What the Qur'an prescribes

The Qur'an refers to apostasy repeatedly. **In not one of those places does it
prescribe a worldly punishment.** The consequence it names is always in the next
life.

- The main passage assigns the loss of one's deeds and the punishment of the
  hereafter, and stops there:

    > "And whoever of you reverts from his religion and dies while he is a
    > disbeliever — for those, their deeds have become worthless in this world
    > and the Hereafter" (**Sūrat al-Baqarah 2:217**), Saheeh International
    >
    > <div dir="rtl" lang="ar" class="quran-arabic">﴿وَمَن يَرْتَدِدْ مِنكُمْ عَن دِينِهِۦ فَيَمُتْ وَهُوَ كَافِرٌۭ فَأُو۟لَٰٓئِكَ حَبِطَتْ أَعْمَٰلُهُمْ فِى ٱلدُّنْيَا وَٱلْءَاخِرَةِ﴾ <span class="ayah-ref">[سُورَةُ البَقَرَةِ: ٢١٧]</span></div>
    {: .quote }

- The verse on coerced denial protects the person who recants **under duress**,
  revealed concerning Ammar ibn Yasir, who denied his faith with his tongue
  under torture while his heart held to it:

    > "except for one who is forced [to renounce his religion] while his heart is
    > secure in faith" (**Sūrat al-Naḥl 16:106**), Saheeh International
    >
    > <div dir="rtl" lang="ar" class="quran-arabic">﴿إِلَّا مَنْ أُكْرِهَ وَقَلْبُهُۥ مُطْمَئِنٌّۢ بِٱلْإِيمَٰنِ﴾ <span class="ayah-ref">[سُورَةُ النَّحۡلِ: ١٠٦]</span></div>
    {: .quote }

- And one verse describes a person **apostatising, returning, apostatising
  again, and returning again**:

    > "Indeed, those who have believed then disbelieved, then believed, then
    > disbelieved, and then increased in disbelief — never will Allah forgive
    > them, nor will He guide them to a way." (**Sūrat al-Nisāʾ 4:137**),
    > Saheeh International
    >
    > <div dir="rtl" lang="ar" class="quran-arabic">﴿إِنَّ ٱلَّذِينَ ءَامَنُوا۟ ثُمَّ كَفَرُوا۟ ثُمَّ ءَامَنُوا۟ ثُمَّ كَفَرُوا۟ ثُمَّ ٱزْدَادُوا۟ كُفْرًۭا لَّمْ يَكُنِ ٱللَّهُ لِيَغْفِرَ لَهُمْ وَلَا لِيَهْدِيَهُمْ سَبِيلًۢا ۝١٣٧﴾ <span class="ayah-ref">سُورَةُ النِّسَاءِ</span></div>
    {: .quote }

    > This verse is the hardest single fact for the death-penalty reading to
    > absorb. **A man executed on his first apostasy cannot commit a second
    > one.** The Qur'an describes a cycle it evidently expects to be possible,
    > and assigns to it a consequence in the hereafter and none here.
    {: .gloss }

- And the general principle stands over all of it:

    > "Let there be no compulsion in religion" (**Sūrat al-Baqarah 2:256**),
    > Dr. Mustafa Khattab, *The Clear Quran*
    >
    > <div dir="rtl" lang="ar" class="quran-arabic">﴿لَآ إِكْرَاهَ فِى ٱلدِّينِ﴾ <span class="ayah-ref">[سُورَةُ البَقَرَةِ: ٢٥٦]</span></div>
    {: .quote }

## What the hadith say

The classical position rests on hadith rather than on the Qur'an. Two reports
carry it, and they do not say quite the same thing.

- The short and most-quoted text comes through Ibn Abbas, in a report about
  Ali, the Prophet's ﷺ cousin and son-in-law, burning a group of *zanadiqa* —
  heretics or subversives:

    > Ibn Abbas said: "Had I been in his place I would not have burnt them, as
    > the Prophet ﷺ said, 'Do not punish anybody with Allah's punishment.' I
    > would have killed them, for the Prophet ﷺ said: **'Whoever changes his
    > religion, kill him.'**"
    > — *Sahih al-Bukhari* 3017, with fuller wording at 6922[^1]
    {: .src }

- The other report is a list, and its third item contains a clause that the
  short version does not:

    > "The blood of a Muslim who testifies that none has the right to be
    > worshipped but Allah and that I am His Messenger cannot be shed except in
    > three cases: in retaliation for murder; a married person who commits
    > adultery; and **the one who reverts from Islam and leaves the community**."
    > — *Sahih al-Bukhari* 6878[^2]
    {: .src }

    > The Arabic of that third clause is *al-tarik li-dinihi al-mufariq
    > li'l-jamaʿa* — "the one who abandons his religion, **separating from the
    > community**." The separation is not a gloss added by later apologists. It
    > is in the text of the most rigorously screened hadith collection there is,
    > and it describes a **two-part** offence: leaving the faith *and* defecting
    > from the polity.
    {: .gloss }

- The context of the first report points the same way. Ali was dealing with
  *zanadiqa*, and Ibn Abbas is discussing the manner of their execution, not
  establishing a rule for private unbelief.

## Apostasy as political defection

In a seventh-century polity there was no distinction between religious
affiliation and political allegiance. Leaving the Muslim community was not a
change of private opinion; it was **switching sides in a conflict where the
sides were defined religiously**. Three lines of evidence support reading the
reports that way.

- **The wars of the ridda were about state obligations, not belief.** When the
  Prophet ﷺ died in 632, many Arabian tribes renounced their allegiance to
  Medina and stopped paying *zakat*; Abu Bakr, the first caliph, fought a
  series of campaigns — the *ridda* wars — to hold the polity together. Umar,
  who would become the second caliph two years later, objected to fighting
  them on the grounds that they still professed the testimony of faith. Abu
  Bakr's answer was not that they had left Islam in their hearts:

    > "I swear by God that I will certainly fight those who make a distinction
    > between prayer and *zakat*, for *zakat* is what is due from property."
    > — *Sahih Muslim* 124; also *Sunan Abi Dawud* 1556[^3]
    {: .src }

    > The argument turns on **withholding a tax owed to the state**. Umar's
    > objection assumes that professing belief protects a person's life, and Abu
    > Bakr does not deny it — he identifies a separate, material breach. This is
    > a dispute about secession and revenue, conducted in the generation that
    > knew the Prophet ﷺ best.
    {: .gloss }

- **The one case of apostates executed in his lifetime involved murder and
  robbery.** The men of Urayna were given hospitality, then killed the
  Prophet's ﷺ herdsman and drove off the camels, and apostatised. The verse
  revealed about them is the verse on *hiraba*, brigandage — not a verse about
  belief:

    > "Indeed, the penalty for those who wage war against Allah and His Messenger
    > and strive upon earth to cause corruption is none but that they be killed
    > or crucified, or that their hands and feet be cut off from opposite sides,
    > or that they be exiled from the land." (**Sūrat al-Māʾidah 5:33**),
    > Saheeh International
    >
    > <div dir="rtl" lang="ar" class="quran-arabic">﴿إِنَّمَا جَزَٰٓؤُا۟ ٱلَّذِينَ يُحَارِبُونَ ٱللَّهَ وَرَسُولَهُۥ وَيَسْعَوْنَ فِى ٱلْأَرْضِ فَسَادًا أَن يُقَتَّلُوٓا۟ أَوْ يُصَلَّبُوٓا۟ أَوْ تُقَطَّعَ أَيْدِيهِمْ وَأَرْجُلُهُم مِّنْ خِلَٰفٍ أَوْ يُنفَوْا۟ مِنَ ٱلْأَرْضِ ۚ ذَٰلِكَ لَهُمْ خِزْىٌۭ فِى ٱلدُّنْيَا ۖ وَلَهُمْ فِى ٱلْءَاخِرَةِ عَذَابٌ عَظِيمٌ ۝٣٣﴾ <span class="ayah-ref">سُورَةُ المَائـِدَةِ</span></div>
    {: .quote }

    > *Sunan Abi Dawud* 4369 states directly that "the verse regarding fighting
    > against Allah and His Prophet ﷺ was then revealed" about these men; the
    > incident is also in *Sahih Muslim* 4353 and *Sahih al-Bukhari*.[^4] The
    > offence being punished is identified by the source itself, and it is armed
    > predation.
    {: .gloss }

- **He signed away his own side's converts to secure a truce.** At Hudaybiyya,
  the 628 truce between the Prophet ﷺ and the Quraysh of Mecca, Suhayl ibn Amr
  insisted on a clause that anyone coming from Mecca to the
  Prophet ﷺ be returned to the Quraysh **"even if he embraced your religion."**
  The Muslims objected bitterly; the Prophet ﷺ accepted the term and honoured it,
  sending back Abu Jandal, who had arrived in fetters.[^5]

    > This is the opposite of the disposition the objection attributes to him.
    > A man who regarded departure from Islam as a capital matter does not agree
    > in writing to hand converts back to the community they escaped from, over
    > his own followers' protests, in exchange for a truce.
    {: .gloss }

## Where Muslim scholars have actually disagreed

Presenting the death penalty as unanimous is inaccurate. The disagreement is old,
it is internal, and it is recorded in the standard works.

- **The Hanafi school does not execute women apostates.** They are imprisoned
  until they return, not killed.[^6]

    > This exception is more revealing than it looks. If the offence were
    > **unbelief**, a woman's unbelief is the same as a man's and the penalty
    > should follow. The stated Hanafi reasoning is that women are not
    > combatants — which concedes that **the operative ground is fighting, not
    > believing**. Once that is admitted for women, the same logic is available
    > for any non-combatant.
    {: .gloss }

- **Ibrahim al-Nakhaʿi (d. 715) and Sufyan al-Thawri (d. 778)** — both major
  early figures, al-Thawri the founder of a school that did not survive —
  rejected the death penalty and held that the apostate should be invited to
  repent **indefinitely**.[^7]
- **Al-Sarakhsi**, one of the most authoritative Hanafi jurists, wrote of
  apostasy and unbelief that they are grave offences "but they are between the
  human being and his Lord," with the punishment falling in the hereafter.[^7]
- **Modern scholarship** has developed this considerably. Taha Jabir al-Alwani
  argued in a book-length study that neither the Qur'an nor the Sunnah supports
  a capital penalty for *ridda* as such, and that the reports concern rebellion
  against the polity.[^8] Ali Gomaa, then Grand Mufti of Egypt, stated publicly
  in 2007 that a Muslim who merely rejects the faith faces **no worldly
  punishment**, the matter being between the person and God.[^9] Abdullah bin
  Bayyah has argued that applying an apostasy penalty today is
  counterproductive, driving people from religion rather than to it.[^10]

**This is not a claim that the tradition has changed its mind.** The classical
majority position was the death penalty, and it is still held. What is false is
the claim that no alternative existed until modern embarrassment produced one.
The alternatives are as old as the eighth century.

## Where the criticism does land

The third point in the objection is largely correct and should not be answered
with more theory.

- **States apply the death penalty without the disagreement that has always
  accompanied it.** The distinction between private unbelief and political
  defection, the disagreement among early and modern jurists, and the option of
  repentance are the substance that made the classical position more contested —
  and in practice more humane — than "kill the apostate" by itself suggests. A
  state that keeps the penalty and drops the disagreement is not applying a
  settled tradition; it is selecting the harshest reading available and
  presenting it as the only one.
- **Blasphemy and apostasy statutes are used politically**, against religious
  minorities, dissenters and personal enemies, in ways that have nothing to do
  with the *mufariq li'l-jamaʿa* of the hadith and everything to do with local
  power.
- **The disagreement described above is often suppressed** in jurisdictions
  where the majority position has been codified, so that positions held by
  al-Nakhaʿi, al-Thawri and modern muftis are presented as though they were
  outside Islam.

None of this is a defence of those states, and the Islamic case against them is
available from within the sources rather than only from outside.

## Conclusion

The Qur'an prescribes no worldly penalty for apostasy, and describes a person
apostatising and returning more than once — which the death penalty would make
impossible. The hadith that carry the classical position include, in the most
reliable collection, a clause about **separating from the community**, and the
episodes where apostates were actually executed involved murder, robbery, or
armed secession. Within the tradition, the Hanafis exempted women on reasoning
that concedes the ground is combat rather than belief, and jurists from
al-Nakhaʿi in the eighth century to muftis in the twenty-first have denied the
penalty applies to belief as such. **The majority position was the death penalty.
The unanimity claimed for it does not exist.**

## Qur'an verses cited

- 2:217 — apostasy, with the consequence assigned to this world and the hereafter and no penalty specified
- 2:256 — no compulsion in religion
- 4:137 — belief, disbelief, belief and disbelief again
- 5:33 — the penalty for *hiraba*, the offence in the one lifetime case of apostates executed
- 16:106 — the exemption for one who denies under duress
