Umm Umara — Nusayba bint Kaʿb — came to the Prophet ﷺ with a complaint. **"I do
not see but that everything is for the men, and I do not see anything being
mentioned for the women."** She would later defend him with her own body at
the Battle of Uhud, when the Muslim line broke and the fighting reached the
Prophet ﷺ himself. Her complaint
was answered by a passage of the Qur'an that names men and women separately in
ten consecutive pairs and promises them the same reward.[^1]

The exchange introduces the central question directly. A woman said that women
were being left out, and her objection was answered. This page examines what
Islam gave women, what it required of men, and how the verses and reports most
often cited against it read in full.

**The difficult cases are addressed directly.** Five have separate pages —
[the verse about striking](/wadribuhunna), [plural marriage](/polygyny), [the
marriage to Aisha](/aisha), [apostasy](/ridda), and the general question of
[severe punishments](/punishments) — while the others are discussed below.

**A note on terms.** A *hadith* is a report about what the Prophet ﷺ said or did.
Each report is transmitted through a named chain of narrators and graded for
reliability. *Sahih al-Bukhari* and *Sahih Muslim* are the most rigorously
screened collections, while a report graded *da'if* is weak. *Mahr* is the
marriage gift a husband pays directly to his wife. *Khulʿ* is divorce initiated
by the wife. The *Companions* are the generation who knew the Prophet ﷺ
personally.

## The objection

The criticism is strongest when its separate claims are presented together:

1. A woman's testimony counts as half a man's.
2. She inherits half what her brother inherits.
3. Her husband may take three more wives; she may not take another husband.
4. A verse permits him to strike her.
5. She must cover herself, and he need not.
6. The Prophet ﷺ called women deficient in intellect and religion, and said a
   people who put a woman in charge will not succeed.
7. Muslim-majority societies today are, on most measures, among the worst places
   in the world to be a woman.

Points 1 through 6 refer to genuine texts, and each is considered below. Point
7 concerns the conduct of Muslims rather than the teachings of Islam. That
distinction is central to the discussion.

## What Islam replaced

An assessment of these teachings should begin with the practices they changed.
The historical setting does not excuse injustice, but it shows what the new
rules altered.

- **Infant daughters were buried alive.** The Qur'an condemns the practice by
  presenting the murdered child as the one questioned on the Day of Judgment:

    > "and when baby girls, buried alive, are asked for what crime they were put
    > to death" (**Sūrat al-Takwīr 81:8–9**), Dr. Mustafa Khattab,
    > *The Clear Quran*
    >
    > <div dir="rtl" lang="ar" class="quran-arabic">﴿وَإِذَا ٱلْمَوْءُۥدَةُ سُئِلَتْ ۝٨ بِأَىِّ ذَنۢبٍۢ قُتِلَتْ ۝٩﴾ <span class="ayah-ref">سُورَةُ التَّكۡوِيرِ</span></div>
    {: .quote }

    > The verse does not question the father who killed her. It questions
    > **the child**: "for what crime" was she put to death? The answer is none.
    > By giving the murdered daughter a voice, the Qur'an turns her death into
    > an accusation against the society that accepted it.
    {: .gloss }

- **Widows were inherited along with the property.** A dead man's wife could
  pass to his heirs as part of his estate. The Qur'an prohibits this in the same
  verse that commands good treatment in marriage: *"it is not lawful for you to
  inherit women by compulsion"* (4:19).
- **A wife's property was often treated as her husband's.** The Qur'an recognizes
  her earnings and possessions as her own.

## Standing before God

The Qur'an states the moral standing of women repeatedly and in several
different contexts.

- **One origin.** Men and women share the same human origin, and the Qur'an
  does not present a woman as the cause of the fall:

    > "Who created you from a single soul" (**Sūrat al-Nisāʾ 4:1**),
    > Dr. Mustafa Khattab, *The Clear Quran*
    >
    > <div dir="rtl" lang="ar" class="quran-arabic">﴿خَلَقَكُم مِّن نَّفْسٍۢ وَٰحِدَةٍۢ﴾ <span class="ayah-ref">[سُورَةُ النِّسَاءِ: ١]</span></div>
    {: .quote }

- **One measure of worth**, based on righteousness rather than sex, lineage,
  or tribe:

    > "Surely the most noble of you in the sight of Allah is the most righteous
    > among you" (**Sūrat al-Ḥujurāt 49:13**), Dr. Mustafa Khattab,
    > *The Clear Quran*
    >
    > <div dir="rtl" lang="ar" class="quran-arabic">﴿إِنَّ أَكْرَمَكُمْ عِندَ ٱللَّهِ أَتْقَىٰكُمْ﴾ <span class="ayah-ref">[سُورَةُ الحُجُرَاتِ: ١٣]</span></div>
    {: .quote }

- **The same reward for the same work**, stated explicitly for both men and
  women:

    > "Whoever does good, whether male or female, and is a believer, We will
    > surely bless them with a good life, and We will certainly reward them
    > according to the best of their deeds" (**Sūrat al-Naḥl 16:97**),
    > Dr. Mustafa Khattab, *The Clear Quran*
    >
    > <div dir="rtl" lang="ar" class="quran-arabic">﴿مَنْ عَمِلَ صَٰلِحًۭا مِّن ذَكَرٍ أَوْ أُنثَىٰ وَهُوَ مُؤْمِنٌۭ فَلَنُحْيِيَنَّهُۥ حَيَوٰةًۭ طَيِّبَةًۭ ۖ وَلَنَجْزِيَنَّهُمْ أَجْرَهُم بِأَحْسَنِ مَا كَانُوا۟ يَعْمَلُونَ ۝٩٧﴾ <span class="ayah-ref">سُورَةُ النَّحۡلِ</span></div>
    {: .quote }

- **The passage revealed after Umm Umara's question** lists ten qualities,
  naming each one separately for men and women:

    > "Indeed, the Muslim men and Muslim women, the believing men and believing
    > women, the obedient men and obedient women, the truthful men and truthful
    > women, the patient men and patient women, the humble men and humble women,
    > the charitable men and charitable women, the fasting men and fasting women,
    > the men who guard their private parts and the women who do so, and the men
    > who remember Allah often and the women who do so — for them Allah has
    > prepared forgiveness and a great reward." (**Sūrat al-Aḥzāb 33:35**),
    > Saheeh International
    >
    > <div dir="rtl" lang="ar" class="quran-arabic">﴿إِنَّ ٱلْمُسْلِمِينَ وَٱلْمُسْلِمَٰتِ وَٱلْمُؤْمِنِينَ وَٱلْمُؤْمِنَٰتِ وَٱلْقَٰنِتِينَ وَٱلْقَٰنِتَٰتِ وَٱلصَّٰدِقِينَ وَٱلصَّٰدِقَٰتِ وَٱلصَّٰبِرِينَ وَٱلصَّٰبِرَٰتِ وَٱلْخَٰشِعِينَ وَٱلْخَٰشِعَٰتِ وَٱلْمُتَصَدِّقِينَ وَٱلْمُتَصَدِّقَٰتِ وَٱلصَّٰٓئِمِينَ وَٱلصَّٰٓئِمَٰتِ وَٱلْحَٰفِظِينَ فُرُوجَهُمْ وَٱلْحَٰفِظَٰتِ وَٱلذَّٰكِرِينَ ٱللَّهَ كَثِيرًۭا وَٱلذَّٰكِرَٰتِ أَعَدَّ ٱللَّهُ لَهُم مَّغْفِرَةًۭ وَأَجْرًا عَظِيمًۭا ۝٣٥﴾ <span class="ayah-ref">سُورَةُ الأَحۡزَابِ</span></div>
    {: .quote }

    > The passage could have answered in general terms that women were
    > included. Instead, it repeats all ten qualities in both masculine and
    > feminine forms. **Umm Umara complained that women were not being named,
    > and the answer named them category by category.**
    {: .gloss }

- **Mutual responsibility, expressed in the same term.** Believing men and
  women are each other's *awliyaʾ* — protectors, allies, and people responsible
  for one another's affairs. Both are also charged with commanding what is right
  and forbidding what is wrong:

    > "The believing men and believing women are allies of one another"
    > (**Sūrat al-Tawbah 9:71**), Saheeh International
    >
    > <div dir="rtl" lang="ar" class="quran-arabic">﴿وَٱلْمُؤْمِنُونَ وَٱلْمُؤْمِنَٰتُ بَعْضُهُمْ أَوْلِيَآءُ بَعْضٍۢ﴾ <span class="ayah-ref">[سُورَةُ التَّوۡبَةِ: ٧١]</span></div>
    {: .quote }

## Standing before the law

The Qur'an also gives women enforceable legal and financial rights. These are
not only statements about spiritual standing.

- **Her earnings and property are her own.** The verse assigns each sex a share
  of what it acquires:

    > "For men is a share of what they have earned, and for women is a share of
    > what they have earned" (**Sūrat al-Nisāʾ 4:32**), Saheeh International
    >
    > <div dir="rtl" lang="ar" class="quran-arabic">﴿لِّلرِّجَالِ نَصِيبٌۭ مِّمَّا ٱكْتَسَبُوا۟ ۖ وَلِلنِّسَآءِ نَصِيبٌۭ مِّمَّا ٱكْتَسَبْنَ﴾ <span class="ayah-ref">[سُورَةُ النِّسَاءِ: ٣٢]</span></div>
    {: .quote }

- **The marriage gift is paid to her, not to her father.** The *mahr* belongs
  to the wife, and the verse presents it as a freely given gift rather than a
  purchase price:

    > "Give women ˹you wed˺ their due dowries graciously" (**Sūrat al-Nisāʾ
    > 4:4**), Dr. Mustafa Khattab, *The Clear Quran*
    >
    > <div dir="rtl" lang="ar" class="quran-arabic">﴿وَءَاتُوا۟ ٱلنِّسَآءَ صَدُقَٰتِهِنَّ نِحْلَةًۭ﴾ <span class="ayah-ref">[سُورَةُ النِّسَاءِ: ٤]</span></div>
    {: .quote }

- **She keeps it, and she keeps her own income, whether or not she is married.**
  Her husband's duty to support the household does not give him a claim to her
  wealth. The financial obligation runs from him to the household, not from her
  property to him.
- **These rights were reflected in practice.** The Prophet's ﷺ first wife,
  **Khadija bint Khuwaylid**, operated a trading business, employed him before
  their marriage, and initiated the marriage proposal. She supported his mission,
  was the first person to believe him, and remained his only wife until her
  death.

    > Khadija's life shows the legal capacity these rules protected. She owned
    > a trading business, employed the man she later married, and took the
    > initiative in arranging the marriage. The Qur'anic property rules
    > recognized and secured her ability to act in her own right.
    {: .gloss }
- **She inherits by fixed entitlement.** Many women had previously received no
  inheritance. The Qur'an assigns them defined shares (4:7, 4:11–12) that cannot
  be removed by a will.

## Consent, and what happened when it was ignored

Khansa bint Khidham's father arranged a marriage that she did not want. She
brought the matter to the Prophet ﷺ.

> "Her father gave her in marriage when she was a matron and she disliked that
> marriage. So she went to the Messenger of Allah ﷺ and **he declared that
> marriage invalid**."
> — *Sahih al-Bukhari* 5138 and 6945; *Sunan Abi Dawud* 2101 and *Sunan
> an-Nasa'i* 3268, both graded *sahih*[^2]
{: .src }

- The ruling was not limited to Khansa's case. It became **a precedent that
  other women invoked**. A woman from Jaʿfar's family feared that her guardian
  would marry her against her will. She sent for two elders of the Ansar, who
  told her: *"Don't be afraid — Khansa bint Khidham was given by her father in
  marriage against her will, and the Prophet ﷺ declared it invalid."*[^3]

    > The ruling did not remain an abstract principle. Another woman facing
    > the same danger was reassured by people who knew Khansa's case and could
    > invoke it as precedent. The annulment had become an operative legal right.
    {: .gloss }

## Getting out of a marriage

- **A wife could end her marriage without alleging fault.** The wife of Thabit
  ibn Qays told the Prophet ﷺ that she had no complaint about her husband's
  character or religion, but could not continue living with him. The Prophet ﷺ
  asked whether she would return the garden Thabit had given her as *mahr*. She
  agreed, and he ordered Thabit to divorce her.[^4]

    > This is *khulʿ*. A wife does not need to accuse her husband of
    > misconduct, produce witnesses, or prove that he harmed her. **Her statement
    > that she cannot continue is sufficient.** She returns the marriage gift,
    > and the marriage ends.
    {: .gloss }

- **Where there was harm, she did not have to return anything.** Thabit ibn
  Qays struck another wife, Jamila bint Abdullah ibn Ubayy, and broke her arm.
  Her brother brought the complaint to the Prophet ﷺ, who summoned Thabit and
  said: **"Take what she owes you and let her go."**[^5]

## The mosque, and knowledge

- **Women could not be prevented from attending the mosque**, and the
  Companions applied this instruction within their own families. When Ibn Umar
  reported the Prophet's ﷺ words, his son replied that the women of his household
  would still be prevented:

    > "Do not prevent the maid-servants of Allah from going to the mosque." …
    > Bilal ibn Abdullah said: "By Allah, we shall certainly prevent them."
    > **Ibn Umar turned on him and rebuked him so harshly that the narrator had
    > never heard the like from him.**
    > — *Sahih Muslim* 989, 990 and 992; *Sunan Ibn Majah* 16, graded *sahih*[^6]
    {: .src }

- **Aisha became a primary legal authority.** She narrated a large body of
  hadith and corrected senior Companions often enough for al-Zarkashi to compile
  an entire book on her corrections.[^7] Her scholarship is discussed in
  [The Prophet Muhammad's ﷺ Marriage to Aisha](/aisha).
- **Women requested their own teaching sessions and received them.** Women
  continued to transmit religious knowledge for centuries. Muhammad Akram
  Nadwi's biographical dictionary of women hadith scholars extends to dozens of
  volumes and records thousands of women.[^8]

## A woman argued with the Prophet ﷺ and the Qur'an took her side

Khawla bint Thaʿlaba's husband repudiated her using a pre-Islamic formula that
left her neither fully married nor free to remarry. She brought her case to the
Prophet ﷺ and continued to press it when he had no ruling to give. The chapter
revealed afterward opens by stating that God heard her:

> "Indeed, Allah has heard the argument of the woman who pleaded with you ˹O
> Prophet˺ concerning her husband, and appealed to Allah. Allah has heard your
> exchange." (**Sūrat al-Mujādilah 58:1**), Dr. Mustafa Khattab,
> *The Clear Quran*
>
> <div dir="rtl" lang="ar" class="quran-arabic">﴿قَدْ سَمِعَ ٱللَّهُ قَوْلَ ٱلَّتِى تُجَٰدِلُكَ فِى زَوْجِهَا وَتَشْتَكِىٓ إِلَى ٱللَّهِ﴾ <span class="ayah-ref">[سُورَةُ المُجَادلَةِ: ١]</span></div>
{: .quote }

The surah is named *al-Mujadila* — "the woman who argues."

## The measure the Prophet ﷺ set

- He made a man's treatment of his wife a measure of his character and applied
  the same standard to himself:

    > "The best of you is the best to his wives, and I am the best of you to my
    > wives."
    > — *Jami' at-Tirmidhi* 3895, graded *sahih*[^10]
    {: .src }

- In the Farewell Sermon, delivered before the largest audience he addressed,
  he stated that marriage carries obligations in both directions: *"There are
  rights for you upon your women, and rights for your women upon you,"* and
  instructed men to provide women with good clothing and food.[^11]

    > **The sentence about striking appears in this same sermon**, qualified
    > as "a beating that is not painful." It is not being passed over here. Its
    > wording, the term behind it, and the limits Muslim jurists placed on it are
    > examined in [The Misreading of Qur'an 4:34](/wadribuhunna).
    {: .gloss }

## The hard cases

### The verse about striking (4:34)

This is the verse most often cited against Islam. Its verb *ḍaraba* has many
meanings in Arabic; the Prophet ﷺ never struck a woman; and jurists who
understood it physically imposed restrictions that reduced the act to something
symbolic. The full discussion appears at
[The Misreading of Qur'an 4:34](/wadribuhunna).

### Four wives

The rule limits an unrestricted pre-Islamic practice, appears in a passage
about orphans, and makes plural marriage conditional on justice that the same
chapter says cannot be fully achieved. The issue is discussed at
[Four Wives](/polygyny).

### The marriage to Aisha

This is discussed at
[The Prophet Muhammad's ﷺ Marriage to Aisha](/aisha).

### "Her testimony is half"

- Verse 2:282 concerns **written commercial debt contracts**. It asks for two
  men, or one man and two women, so that one woman can remind the other if she
  errs. The rule governs documentation for a particular transaction; it is not a
  general rule for all testimony.
- **Classical law did not apply this ratio to every kind of evidence.** In matters
  primarily known by women — including birth, nursing, and women's physical
  conditions — one woman's testimony could be accepted by itself. In *liʿan*,
  the mutual-oath procedure used when a husband accuses his wife of adultery, her
  oath answers his on equal terms.
- The Qur'an's general rule for accusations imposes a severe burden on the
  accuser. A man who accuses a woman of adultery without producing four witnesses
  is **flogged eighty lashes and his testimony is rejected for life** (24:4).
  The rule protects a woman's reputation rather than treating her word as
  inherently unreliable.

### "She inherits half"

- The half-share applies in **specific family arrangements**, most commonly
  when a son and daughter inherit together. It is not a universal ratio. In other
  cases, a woman may inherit an equal share, a larger share, or inherit when a
  male relative does not.
- The rule belongs to a financial system in which **a man's share carries
  obligations that a woman's share does not**. He owes *mahr*, must maintain his
  wife and children, and may also be responsible for other relatives. He has no
  corresponding claim to her property or earnings, and she may keep her entire
  share.
- Muslim jurists have debated whether this arrangement remains fair when men no
  longer meet those financial duties. The narrower point here is that **the
  ratio is not a statement about worth**; it is part of a wider allocation of
  rights and obligations.

### "Deficient in intellect and religion"

- The phrase appears in a report in which the Prophet ﷺ addresses women at the
  Eid prayer (*Sahih al-Bukhari* 304, 1462). The English wording is harsh, and
  that should not be disguised.
- **The report explains both deficiencies by reference to particular legal
  circumstances rather than innate qualities.** The "deficiency in religion" is
  a menstruating woman's exemption from prayer and fasting, not a moral fault.
  The "deficiency" in testimony refers to the documentation rule in 2:282
  discussed above.
- The word *naqṣ* denotes a measurable shortfall, such as a debt or quantity that
  is incomplete. It does not describe an essential defect in a person's nature.
- The Prophet's ﷺ conduct also limits how the report can be read. **He left Aisha
  as an authority on some of the community's most intimate legal questions.**
  The Companions sought her rulings and accepted her corrections. That practice
  is inconsistent with treating women's minds as generally unreliable.

### "A people who put a woman in charge will not succeed"

- The Prophet ﷺ made this statement after hearing that the Persians had placed
  **Khosrau's daughter** on the throne after his death (*Jami' at-Tirmidhi* 2262,
  graded *sahih*). The immediate context was a particular dynastic succession in
  an empire already in decline.
- The reading adopted on this site understands the sentence as **addressed to the
  men, not as a judgment about women**. In this reading, leadership is a burden
  for which men were made responsible before God. A society in which men transfer
  that responsibility to women has failed to meet an obligation placed on them.
- **This is one interpretation, and it is presented as such.** Scholars have
  disagreed over whether the report establishes a general legal rule or comments
  on a particular succession, whether it applies only to supreme leadership, and
  how it relates to modern states. Muslim jurists have supported women serving in
  judicial and ministerial positions.
- The same tradition also records the Prophet ﷺ accepting political advice from
  his wife **Umm Salama** at Hudaybiyya, the 628 truce with the Quraysh of Mecca
  and the worst crisis of his leadership. After the treaty, he ordered the
  Companions three times to slaughter their
  sacrifices and shave their heads, but **not one of them moved.** He told Umm
  Salama what had happened. She advised him to go out, say nothing, and perform
  the rites himself. He followed her advice, and the entire camp then followed
  him.[^12]

    > This is not a marginal report. It appears in the tradition's most
    > rigorously screened collection and describes a serious crisis of
    > leadership. The Prophet ﷺ resolved it by accepting and acting on his
    > wife's political advice.
    {: .gloss }

### Covering

- The Qur'an addresses **men first**. Verse 24:30 tells believing men to lower
  their gaze and guard their modesty; 24:31 then addresses women. Men are
  therefore instructed to restrain their gaze before women are given their own
  dress and modesty requirements.
- The stated purpose is dignity and recognition, not removal from public life.
  Women in the Prophet's ﷺ community traded, farmed, questioned him publicly,
  prayed in the mosque, transmitted law, and fought at Uhud.
- **A religious dress obligation is distinct from a rule imposed by state force
  or a man's violence.** The Qur'anic principle that there is no compulsion in
  religion (2:256) is directly relevant to that distinction.

## Why these particular verses

The Qur'an distinguishes between two kinds of verse and warns about the misuse
of those that require interpretation:

> "in it are verses that are precise — they are the foundation of the Book — and
> others unspecific. As for those in whose hearts is deviation, they will follow
> that of it which is unspecific, seeking discord and seeking an interpretation
> suitable to them." (**Sūrat Āl ʿImrān 3:7**), Saheeh International
>
> <div dir="rtl" lang="ar" class="quran-arabic">﴿مِنْهُ ءَايَٰتٌۭ مُّحْكَمَٰتٌ هُنَّ أُمُّ ٱلْكِتَٰبِ وَأُخَرُ مُتَشَٰبِهَٰتٌۭ﴾ <span class="ayah-ref">[سُورَةُ آلِ عِمۡرَانَ: ٧]</span></div>
{: .quote }

- The *muḥkamāt* are clear verses, including statements that God is one,
  justice is obligatory, and there is no compulsion in religion. Their basic
  meaning does not depend on specialist interpretation.
- The *mutashābihāt* require interpretation. That work depends on knowledge of
  the language, the occasions of revelation, the related texts, and the reports
  showing how the Prophet ﷺ applied them.
- **The warning concerns interpretive method.** A person trying to prove a
  predetermined case may isolate verses that require interpretation and assign
  them a meaning that conflicts with the clear verses. Critics do this when they
  quote 4:34 without the Prophet's ﷺ practice. Muslim men do the same when they
  quote it without the restrictions imposed by jurists. **In both cases, an
  ambiguous text is isolated from the sources that govern its interpretation.**

## Where the criticism does land

The seventh objection cannot be answered only by citing religious sources,
because it concerns the conduct of Muslim societies.

- **The treatment of women in many Muslim societies is poor and is not
  improving quickly.** Forced marriage, denial of legally assigned inheritance,
  so-called honour violence, restrictions on education, and silence about
  domestic abuse are real and widespread. They are also defended in religious
  language by people who should know the relevant rulings.
- **Most of these practices violate the rules described above.** Forced marriage
  was annulled; inheritance shares were fixed; *khulʿ* did not require proof of
  fault; and a husband who broke his wife's arm was ordered to release her.
  Custom (*ʿurf*) is often presented as religion, especially when doing so serves
  the interests of those with power.
- **Islamic law provides grounds for condemning these practices.** The Prophetic
  maxim *la darar wa la dirar* — **"there should be neither harming nor
  reciprocating harm"** — became a foundational legal principle from which
  jurists developed remedies.[^13] Harm within marriage is not merely
  discouraged. It is unlawful and can give a wife grounds to dissolve the
  marriage.
- **Communities often fail to enforce the rules they teach.** The remedies may
  exist in law while the woman bears the social cost of using them.

Islam should be assessed by its authoritative teachings rather than by every
practice carried out in its name. This distinction does not excuse Muslims. It
means that a community that mistreats women is violating its own religion, and
that the strongest internal criticism can be made from the tradition's own
sources.

## Conclusion

The Prophet ﷺ said **"I was sent to perfect good character,"**[^14] and made a
man's treatment of his wife a measure of his character. The tradition should be
judged by that standard, as should the conduct of Muslims.

The sources give women specific rights and forms of legal standing: **the buried
daughter is allowed to testify against the wrong done to her; a woman's earnings
belong to her; her marriage gift is paid to her rather than her father; her
consent is required for marriage; the annulment of a forced marriage became a
precedent another woman could invoke; she may leave a marriage through *khulʿ*;
her access to the mosque is protected; her complaint about being omitted is
answered by revelation; and a surah is named after a woman who argued her case.**

The difficult verses remain difficult, and they are addressed here and on the
linked pages rather than omitted. They do not, however, establish that Islam
regards women as lesser. **When Umm Umara complained that women had not been
mentioned, the Qur'an answered by naming men and women separately across ten
pairs and promising both the same reward.**
