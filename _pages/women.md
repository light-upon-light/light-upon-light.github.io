[< Back to **The Qur'an and the Messenger ﷺ**](/quran)

A woman came to the Prophet ﷺ and told him she had a complaint. **"I do not see
but that everything is for the men, and I do not see anything being mentioned for
the women."** Her name was Umm Umara — Nusayba bint Kaʿb, who would later defend
him with her own body at the Battle of Uhud, when the Muslim line broke and
fighting reached the Prophet ﷺ himself. The answer she got was a passage of the
Qur'an naming men and women separately in ten consecutive pairs and promising
them the same reward.[^1]

That exchange is the shape of the subject. A woman raised an objection, in
person, and the objection was answered rather than dismissed. This page sets out
what Islam gave women, what it required of men, and what the verses and reports
most often quoted against it say when they are read in full.

**The difficult cases are not skipped.** Five of them have their own pages —
[the verse about striking](/wadribuhunna), [plural marriage](/polygyny), [the
marriage to Aisha](/aisha), [apostasy](/ridda), and the general question of
[severe punishments](/punishments) — and the rest are set out below.

**A note on terms.** A *hadith* is a report of what the Prophet ﷺ said or did,
transmitted with a named chain of narrators and graded for reliability; *Sahih
al-Bukhari* and *Sahih Muslim* are the most rigorously screened collections, and
a report graded *da'if* is weak. *Mahr* is the marriage gift paid by the husband
to the wife herself. *Khulʿ* is divorce initiated by the wife. The *Companions*
are the generation who knew the Prophet ﷺ personally.

## The objection

Put at its strongest, the case against is not one claim but a list, and the list
is what gives it force:

1. A woman's testimony counts as half a man's.
2. She inherits half what her brother inherits.
3. Her husband may take three more wives; she may not take another husband.
4. A verse permits him to strike her.
5. She must cover herself, and he need not.
6. The Prophet ﷺ called women deficient in intellect and religion, and said a
   people who put a woman in charge will not succeed.
7. Muslim-majority societies today are, on most measures, among the worst places
   in the world to be a woman.

Points 1 through 6 are all real texts and are dealt with individually below.
Point 7 is a claim about Muslims rather than about Islam, and the difference
between those two things is where most of this argument actually lives.

## What Islam replaced

Any assessment has to start from what was there before, not because custom
excuses anything, but because it identifies what the change actually was.

- **Infant daughters were buried alive.** The Qur'an does not merely prohibit
  this. It puts the murdered girl herself in the courtroom on the Day of Judgment
  and has her asked the question, which leaves her killer with nothing to say:

    > "and when baby girls, buried alive, are asked for what crime they were put
    > to death" (**Sūrat al-Takwīr 81:8–9**), Dr. Mustafa Khattab,
    > *The Clear Quran*
    >
    > <div dir="rtl" lang="ar" class="quran-arabic">﴿وَإِذَا ٱلْمَوْءُۥدَةُ سُئِلَتْ ۝٨ بِأَىِّ ذَنۢبٍۢ قُتِلَتْ ۝٩﴾ <span class="ayah-ref">سُورَةُ التَّكۡوِيرِ</span></div>
    {: .quote }

    > Note who is questioned. Not the father, who is the guilty party — **the
    > child**. The verse gives her the standing to be asked, and by asking "for
    > what crime" it answers itself: there was none. A society that treated a
    > daughter as disposable is told that she will be a witness.
    {: .gloss }

- **Widows were inherited along with the property.** A dead man's wife passed to
  his heirs as part of the estate. The Qur'an forbids it in the same verse that
  commands kindness in marriage: *"it is not lawful for you to inherit women by
  compulsion"* (4:19).
- **A wife's property was her husband's in practice.** The Qur'an makes her
  earnings and holdings hers.

## Standing before God

The Qur'an's position on the moral standing of women is not incidental or
scattered. It is stated repeatedly and in different registers.

- **One origin.** Men and women are not made from different stuff, and there is
  no Qur'anic narrative of a woman causing the fall:

    > "Who created you from a single soul" (**Sūrat al-Nisāʾ 4:1**),
    > Dr. Mustafa Khattab, *The Clear Quran*
    >
    > <div dir="rtl" lang="ar" class="quran-arabic">﴿خَلَقَكُم مِّن نَّفْسٍۢ وَٰحِدَةٍۢ﴾ <span class="ayah-ref">[سُورَةُ النِّسَاءِ: ١]</span></div>
    {: .quote }

- **One measure of worth**, and it is not sex, lineage, or tribe:

    > "Surely the most noble of you in the sight of Allah is the most righteous
    > among you" (**Sūrat al-Ḥujurāt 49:13**), Dr. Mustafa Khattab,
    > *The Clear Quran*
    >
    > <div dir="rtl" lang="ar" class="quran-arabic">﴿إِنَّ أَكْرَمَكُمْ عِندَ ٱللَّهِ أَتْقَىٰكُمْ﴾ <span class="ayah-ref">[سُورَةُ الحُجُرَاتِ: ١٣]</span></div>
    {: .quote }

- **The same reward for the same work**, stated so as to remove the ambiguity:

    > "Whoever does good, whether male or female, and is a believer, We will
    > surely bless them with a good life, and We will certainly reward them
    > according to the best of their deeds" (**Sūrat al-Naḥl 16:97**),
    > Dr. Mustafa Khattab, *The Clear Quran*
    >
    > <div dir="rtl" lang="ar" class="quran-arabic">﴿مَنْ عَمِلَ صَٰلِحًۭا مِّن ذَكَرٍ أَوْ أُنثَىٰ وَهُوَ مُؤْمِنٌۭ فَلَنُحْيِيَنَّهُۥ حَيَوٰةًۭ طَيِّبَةًۭ ۖ وَلَنَجْزِيَنَّهُمْ أَجْرَهُم بِأَحْسَنِ مَا كَانُوا۟ يَعْمَلُونَ ۝٩٧﴾ <span class="ayah-ref">سُورَةُ النَّحۡلِ</span></div>
    {: .quote }

- **And the passage Umm Umara's question produced**, which answers by
  enumeration — ten qualities, each named twice, once for each sex:

    > "Indeed, the Muslim men and Muslim women, the believing men and believing
    > women, the obedient men and obedient women, the truthful men and truthful
    > women, the patient men and patient women, the humble men and humble women,
    > the charitable men and charitable women, the fasting men and fasting women,
    > the men who guard their private parts and the women who do so, and the men
    > who remember Allah often and the women who do so — for them Allah has
    > prepared forgiveness and a great reward." (**Sūrat al-Aḥzāb 33:35**),
    > Saheeh International
    >
    > <div dir="rtl" lang="ar" class="quran-arabic">﴿إِنَّ ٱلْمُسْلِمِينَ وَٱلْمُسْلِمَٰتِ وَٱلْمُؤْمِنِينَ وَٱلْمُؤْمِنَٰتِ وَٱلْقَٰنِتِينَ وَٱلْقَٰنِتَٰتِ وَٱلصَّٰدِقِينَ وَٱلصَّٰدِقَٰتِ وَٱلصَّٰبِرِينَ وَٱلصَّٰبِرَٰتِ وَٱلْخَٰشِعِينَ وَٱلْخَٰشِعَٰتِ وَٱلْمُتَصَدِّقِينَ وَٱلْمُتَصَدِّقَٰتِ وَٱلصَّٰٓئِمِينَ وَٱلصَّٰٓئِمَٰتِ وَٱلْحَٰفِظِينَ فُرُوجَهُمْ وَٱلْحَٰفِظَٰتِ وَٱلذَّٰكِرِينَ ٱللَّهَ كَثِيرًۭا وَٱلذَّٰكِرَٰتِ أَعَدَّ ٱللَّهُ لَهُم مَّغْفِرَةًۭ وَأَجْرًا عَظِيمًۭا ۝٣٥﴾ <span class="ayah-ref">سُورَةُ الأَحۡزَابِ</span></div>
    {: .quote }

    > The form of the answer is the answer. It would have been shorter to say
    > "this applies to women too." Instead every single term is repeated in the
    > feminine, ten times over, so that the point cannot be read past. **A woman
    > said she had been left out, and the response was to write her in, item by
    > item.**
    {: .gloss }

- **Mutual responsibility, in the same word.** Believing men and women are each
  other's *awliyaʾ* — protectors, allies, those charged with one another's
  affairs — and the duty to command right and forbid wrong is given to both:

    > "The believing men and believing women are allies of one another"
    > (**Sūrat al-Tawbah 9:71**), Saheeh International
    >
    > <div dir="rtl" lang="ar" class="quran-arabic">﴿وَٱلْمُؤْمِنُونَ وَٱلْمُؤْمِنَٰتُ بَعْضُهُمْ أَوْلِيَآءُ بَعْضٍۢ﴾ <span class="ayah-ref">[سُورَةُ التَّوۡبَةِ: ٧١]</span></div>
    {: .quote }

## Standing before the law

Spiritual equality that stops at the mosque door is worth little. These are
property rules, and they were enforceable.

- **Her earnings and property are her own.** The verse is explicit that each sex
  keeps what it acquires:

    > "For men is a share of what they have earned, and for women is a share of
    > what they have earned" (**Sūrat al-Nisāʾ 4:32**), Saheeh International
    >
    > <div dir="rtl" lang="ar" class="quran-arabic">﴿لِّلرِّجَالِ نَصِيبٌۭ مِّمَّا ٱكْتَسَبُوا۟ ۖ وَلِلنِّسَآءِ نَصِيبٌۭ مِّمَّا ٱكْتَسَبْنَ﴾ <span class="ayah-ref">[سُورَةُ النِّسَاءِ: ٣٢]</span></div>
    {: .quote }

- **The marriage gift is paid to her, not to her father.** The *mahr* is her
  property, and the verse describes it as a gift freely given rather than a price:

    > "Give women ˹you wed˺ their due dowries graciously" (**Sūrat al-Nisāʾ
    > 4:4**), Dr. Mustafa Khattab, *The Clear Quran*
    >
    > <div dir="rtl" lang="ar" class="quran-arabic">﴿وَءَاتُوا۟ ٱلنِّسَآءَ صَدُقَٰتِهِنَّ نِحْلَةًۭ﴾ <span class="ayah-ref">[سُورَةُ النِّسَاءِ: ٤]</span></div>
    {: .quote }

- **She keeps it, and she keeps her own income, whether or not she is married.**
  A husband's obligation to maintain the household does not attach to her wealth.
  The financial arrangement is one-directional by design.
- **This was not theoretical.** The Prophet's ﷺ first wife, **Khadija bint
  Khuwaylid**, ran a trading business, employed him before she married him, and
  it was she who sent the marriage proposal. She was his employer, then his
  financier, then the first person on earth to believe him — and he took no other
  wife while she lived.

    > A society in which a woman could own a caravan business, hire the man she
    > later married, and open negotiations herself is not one in which female
    > legal incapacity was the starting assumption. The property rules above were
    > written into a place where this was already possible, and they made it
    > secure.
    {: .gloss }
- **She inherits by fixed entitlement.** Before Islam many women inherited
  nothing at all. The Qur'an assigns defined shares (4:7, 4:11–12) that cannot be
  written out by a will.

## Consent, and what happened when it was ignored

Khansa bint Khidham was given in marriage by her father. She did not want the
marriage. She went to the Prophet ﷺ.

> "Her father gave her in marriage when she was a matron and she disliked that
> marriage. So she went to the Messenger of Allah ﷺ and **he declared that
> marriage invalid**."
> — *Sahih al-Bukhari* 5138 and 6945; *Sunan Abi Dawud* 2101 and *Sunan
> an-Nasa'i* 3268, both graded *sahih*[^2]
{: .src }

- The ruling was not a one-off favour. It became **precedent that women used**.
  A woman of Jaʿfar's family, afraid her guardian would marry her off against her
  will, sent for two elders of the Ansar, who told her: *"Don't be afraid — Khansa
  bint Khidham was given by her father in marriage against her will, and the
  Prophet ﷺ declared it invalid."*[^3]

    > A right that a frightened woman can invoke by name, years later, through
    > two neighbours who know the case, is a right that is actually operating.
    > That is a different thing from a principle recorded in a book.
    {: .gloss }

## Getting out of a marriage

- **A wife could end her marriage without alleging fault.** The wife of Thabit
  ibn Qays came to the Prophet ﷺ and said she had no complaint about his
  character or his religion — she simply could not live with him. He asked
  whether she would return the garden Thabit had given her as *mahr*. She said
  yes. He ordered Thabit to divorce her.[^4]

    > This is *khulʿ*. Note what is not required: no misconduct, no witnesses, no
    > proof of harm. **Her word that she cannot continue is the ground.** She
    > returns the marriage gift and the marriage ends.
    {: .gloss }

- **And where there was harm, she did not have to return anything.** Thabit ibn
  Qays hit another wife, Jamila bint Abdullah ibn Ubayy, and broke her arm. Her
  brother brought the complaint. The Prophet ﷺ sent for Thabit and said: **"Take
  what she owes you and let her go."**[^5]

## The mosque, and knowledge

- **Women could not be kept from the mosque**, and the Companions enforced this
  against their own families. When Ibn Umar reported the Prophet's ﷺ words, his
  own son said the women of his household would be prevented anyway:

    > "Do not prevent the maid-servants of Allah from going to the mosque." …
    > Bilal ibn Abdullah said: "By Allah, we shall certainly prevent them."
    > **Ibn Umar turned on him and rebuked him so harshly that the narrator had
    > never heard the like from him.**
    > — *Sahih Muslim* 989, 990 and 992; *Sunan Ibn Majah* 16, graded *sahih*[^6]
    {: .src }

- **Aisha became a primary legal authority**, narrating a large body of the
  hadith corpus and correcting senior Companions often enough that al-Zarkashi
  compiled a book consisting solely of her corrections of them.[^7] Her
  scholarship is treated in
  [The Prophet Muhammad's ﷺ Marriage to Aisha](/aisha).
- **Women asked for their own teaching sessions and were given them.** The
  transmission of religious knowledge by women continued for centuries: Muhammad
  Akram Nadwi's biographical dictionary of women hadith scholars runs to dozens
  of volumes and thousands of named women.[^8]

## A woman argued with the Prophet ﷺ and the Qur'an took her side

Khawla bint Thaʿlaba's husband had repudiated her by a pre-Islamic formula that
left her neither married nor free. She brought the case to the Prophet ﷺ and
pressed it when he had no ruling to give her. The chapter that followed opens by
recording that God heard her:

> "Indeed, Allah has heard the argument of the woman who pleaded with you ˹O
> Prophet˺ concerning her husband, and appealed to Allah. Allah has heard your
> exchange." (**Sūrat al-Mujādilah 58:1**), Dr. Mustafa Khattab,
> *The Clear Quran*
>
> <div dir="rtl" lang="ar" class="quran-arabic">﴿قَدْ سَمِعَ ٱللَّهُ قَوْلَ ٱلَّتِى تُجَٰدِلُكَ فِى زَوْجِهَا وَتَشْتَكِىٓ إِلَى ٱللَّهِ﴾ <span class="ayah-ref">[سُورَةُ المُجَادلَةِ: ١]</span></div>
{: .quote }

The surah is named *al-Mujadila* — "the woman who argues."

## Mothers

> A man asked: "Who among the people is most deserving of good treatment from
> me?" He said: **"Your mother."** He said: "Then who?" He said: **"Your
> mother."** He said: "Then who?" He said: **"Your mother."** He said: "Then
> who?" He said: "Then your father."
> — *Sahih Muslim* 6500 and 6501[^9]
{: .src }

## The measure the Prophet ﷺ set

- He made treatment of one's wife the test of a man's quality, and put himself
  under the same test:

    > "The best of you is the best to his wives, and I am the best of you to my
    > wives."
    > — *Jami' at-Tirmidhi* 3895, graded *sahih*[^10]
    {: .src }

- In the Farewell Sermon, delivered to the largest audience he ever addressed,
  he set obligations running in both directions: *"There are rights for you upon
  your women, and rights for your women upon you,"* and instructed the men to
  treat women well in what they clothe and feed them with.[^11]

    > **The same sermon contains the sentence about striking**, qualified there
    > as "a beating that is not painful." It is not quoted selectively here: the
    > clause, the word behind it, and what the jurists did with it are the whole
    > subject of [The Misreading of Qur'an 4:34](/wadribuhunna). A page that
    > cited the sermon's kindness and passed over its hardest line would deserve
    > the suspicion it got.
    {: .gloss }

## The hard cases

### The verse about striking (4:34)

The most cited verse against Islam. Its verb *ḍaraba* is among the most
polysemous in Arabic; the Prophet ﷺ never struck a woman; and the jurists who
read it physically hedged it to the point of symbolism. Set out in full at
[The Misreading of Qur'an 4:34](/wadribuhunna).

### Four wives

A cap placed on an unlimited pre-Islamic practice, introduced in a passage about
orphans and conditioned on a justice the same chapter says will never be
complete. Set out at [Four Wives](/polygyny).

### The marriage to Aisha

Set out at [The Prophet Muhammad's ﷺ Marriage to Aisha](/aisha).

### "Her testimony is half"

- The verse (2:282) concerns **written commercial debt contracts** and asks for
  two men, or one man and two women, so that if one errs the other can remind
  her. It is a documentation rule in a specific transaction, not a general law
  of evidence.
- **It is not applied generally in classical law.** In matters where women were
  the primary witnesses — birth, nursing, physical conditions of women — a
  woman's testimony was accepted alone. In *liʿan*, the mutual-oath procedure
  when a husband accuses his wife of adultery, her oath cancels his exactly.
- The general Qur'anic rule on accusation runs the other way entirely: a man who
  accuses a woman of adultery and cannot produce four witnesses is **flogged
  eighty lashes and his testimony is rejected for life** (24:4). The evidentiary
  law's overall shape is protective of women's reputations, not dismissive of
  their word.

### "She inherits half"

- The half-share applies in **specific configurations**, most familiarly a son
  and a daughter. It is not a universal ratio, and there are cases where a woman
  inherits equally, more, or where she inherits and a male relative does not.
- It sits inside a system where **the man's share carries obligations the
  woman's does not**: he owes *mahr*, maintenance of his wife and children, and
  often support of other relatives, with no claim on her property or earnings.
  Her share is hers to keep entirely.
- Whether that trade remains fair where men no longer bear those costs is a real
  question, and Muslim jurists have argued about it. The point here is narrower:
  **the ratio is not a statement about worth**, it is one term in a package.

### "Deficient in intellect and religion"

- The phrase is from a report where the Prophet ﷺ addresses a group of women at
  the Eid prayer (*Sahih al-Bukhari* 304, 1462). It reads harshly in English, and
  it is fair to say so.
- **The reasons the report itself gives are circumstantial, not innate.** The
  "deficiency in religion" is that a menstruating woman does not pray or fast —
  an exemption granted to her, not a failing in her. The "deficiency" in
  testimony refers to the 2:282 documentation rule already discussed.
- The word *naqṣ* means a shortfall in an amount, as a debt or a measure falls
  short. It is not the vocabulary of essence.
- And the conduct of the man who said it is the check on any reading. **He made
  Aisha the community's authority on its most intimate law**, and the Companions
  went to her for rulings and accepted her corrections of them. A man who thought
  women's minds unreliable does not leave one of them as a primary legal source
  for everyone who comes after him.

### "A people who put a woman in charge will not succeed"

- The report (*Jami' at-Tirmidhi* 2262, graded *sahih*) was said on hearing that
  the Persians had made **Khosrau's daughter** their ruler after his death — a
  specific dynastic succession in a collapsing empire.
- A reading held on this site takes the sentence as **addressed to the men, not
  about the women**: in the Islamic scheme, leadership is a burden and a
  liability before God rather than a prize, and the responsibility for it was
  placed on men. A community whose men offload their own obligations onto women
  is a community that has failed at something — and the sentence is a warning to
  them.
- **This is one reading and is offered as such.** Scholars have differed
  sharply — on whether the report is a general legal rule or a comment on a
  particular succession, on whether it concerns supreme leadership only, and on
  its scope in modern states. Women have held judicial and ministerial office in
  Muslim polities with juristic backing.
- What is not in dispute is that the same tradition records the Prophet ﷺ taking
  political advice from his wife **Umm Salama** at Hudaybiyya, the 628 truce
  with the Quraysh of Mecca and the worst moment of his leadership. He had
  ordered the Companions to slaughter their
  sacrifices and shave their heads; he gave the order three times and **not one
  of them moved.** He went to Umm Salama and told her. She advised him to say
  nothing to anybody, go out, and do it himself. He did, and the whole camp
  followed.[^12]

    > A community that held it improper for a woman to have authority does not
    > preserve, in its most rigorously screened collection, the story of its
    > founder breaking the gravest crisis of obedience in his career by doing
    > what his wife told him.
    {: .gloss }

### Covering

- The Qur'an addresses **men first**. 24:30 commands believing men to lower their
  gaze and guard their modesty; 24:31 then addresses women. The order is not
  decorative — the burden of restraint is placed on the one doing the looking
  before it is placed on the one being looked at.
- The stated purpose is dignity and recognition, not concealment from public
  life. Women in the Prophet's ﷺ community traded, farmed, questioned him in
  public, prayed in the mosque, transmitted law, and fought at Uhud.
- **A dress requirement enforced by the state or by a man's violence is a
  different thing from a religious obligation**, and the Qur'an's own rule that
  there is no compulsion in religion (2:256) bears on it directly.

## Why these particular verses

The Qur'an describes its own text as containing two kinds of verse, and predicts
what will be done with the second kind:

> "in it are verses that are precise — they are the foundation of the Book — and
> others unspecific. As for those in whose hearts is deviation, they will follow
> that of it which is unspecific, seeking discord and seeking an interpretation
> suitable to them." (**Sūrat Āl ʿImrān 3:7**), Saheeh International
>
> <div dir="rtl" lang="ar" class="quran-arabic">﴿مِنْهُ ءَايَٰتٌۭ مُّحْكَمَٰتٌ هُنَّ أُمُّ ٱلْكِتَٰبِ وَأُخَرُ مُتَشَٰبِهَٰتٌۭ﴾ <span class="ayah-ref">[سُورَةُ آلِ عِمۡرَانَ: ٧]</span></div>
{: .quote }

- The *muḥkamāt* are the clear verses — that God is one, that justice is
  obligatory, that there is no compulsion in religion. They need no specialist to
  read.
- The *mutashābihāt* require interpretation, and interpreting them is a
  discipline with prerequisites: the language, the occasions of revelation, the
  whole body of related texts, the reports of how the Prophet ﷺ applied them.
- **The verse's prediction is about method.** Someone building a case will reach
  for the verses that require interpretation and read them alone, because those
  are the ones that can be made to carry a meaning the clear verses would rule
  out. That is true of hostile critics quoting 4:34 without the Prophet's ﷺ
  practice, and equally true of Muslim men quoting it without the jurists'
  conditions. **The method is the same in both directions and the verse names
  it.**

## Where the criticism does land

The seventh point in the objection is the one that cannot be answered from the
sources, because it is not about the sources.

- **Practice in many Muslim societies is bad and is not improving fast.** Forced
  marriage, denial of inheritance shares that are legally hers, so-called honour
  violence, restricted education, and domestic abuse met with silence are real,
  common, and defended in religious language by people who should know better.
- **Almost every one of those is a violation of the rules set out above** — the
  annulment of a forced marriage, the fixed inheritance shares, the *khulʿ* that
  needs no fault, the wife whose husband broke her arm and was ordered to let her
  go. Custom (*ʿurf*) has repeatedly been mistaken for religion, and the
  mistaking is not accidental where it happens to suit the person making it.
- **The law supplies the ground to condemn them on.** The maxim *la darar wa la
  dirar* — **"there should be neither harming nor reciprocating harm"** — is a
  Prophetic ruling, and it is one of the foundations from which the jurists built
  the whole apparatus of remedy.[^13] Harm within a marriage is not merely
  discouraged; it is unlawful in itself, and it grounds a wife's right to have the
  marriage dissolved.
- **Communities do not enforce what they teach.** The remedies exist; the social
  cost of using them falls on the woman.

Islam should be assessed on its authoritative teachings rather than on
everything done in its name. Applied honestly, that principle does not let
Muslims off. It says that a community failing its women is failing its religion,
and that the case against the practice is available from inside the tradition —
which is where it has the best chance of being listened to.

## Conclusion

The Prophet ﷺ said **"I was sent to perfect good character,"**[^14] and made a
man's treatment of his wife the measure of him. That is the standard the
tradition sets for itself, and it is the standard by which its adherents should
be judged.

Against that standard the record is specific: **a buried daughter given standing
to accuse her killer; a woman's earnings made hers; her marriage gift paid to
her and not her father; her consent made a condition of marriage, with the
annulment quoted back years later by a frightened woman who needed it; her exit
from marriage available on her word alone; her access to the mosque protected
against her own family; her complaint about being left out answered by a passage
of revelation; and a surah named after a woman who argued.**

The hard verses are hard, and they are set out above and on the pages linked
from it rather than tidied away. What they do not support is the claim they are
usually recruited for — that Islam regards women as lesser. **The book says the
opposite in terms it takes ten pairs of nouns to make unmistakable, because a
woman asked it to.**
