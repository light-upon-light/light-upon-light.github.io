The record includes household observations, encounters with opponents, and accounts of changed convictions. These are distinguished below from later historical and theological appraisals, which illuminate reception without independently proving prophethood.

{% include glossary-key.html %}

## What the witness record can establish
{: #what-the-witness-record-can-establish }

- <span id="src-2973"></span>Conversion by itself proves little; false movements can also attract devoted followers. What makes this record more interesting is the range of people involved.


- <span id="src-2975"></span>They include a wife who knew Prophet Muhammad ﷺ before his mission, a servant who lived with him for a decade, an enemy questioned by a foreign ruler, a hostile chief held as a prisoner and then released, a political negotiator who had seen imperial courts, and former enemies who later described how their judgment of him changed.[28](/messenger/sources#ref-28){: .footnote}[29](/messenger/sources#ref-29){: .footnote}[37](/messenger/sources#ref-37){: .footnote}[38](/messenger/sources#ref-38){: .footnote}[39](/messenger/sources#ref-39){: .footnote}[41](/messenger/sources#ref-41){: .footnote}[42](/messenger/sources#ref-42){: .footnote}


- <span id="src-3037"></span>No one witness is decisive, but together they observe Prophet Muhammad ﷺ from unusually different positions. An enemy speaks to his prior honesty. Family members describe private conduct. A servant describes everyday temperament. A negotiator comments on leadership and loyalty. Former enemies describe the effect of direct encounter. This convergence says more than the bare fact that many people followed him.

    > "And you are truly ˹a man˺ of outstanding character." (**Sūrat al-Qalam 68:4**), Dr. Mustafa Khattab, *The Clear Quran*[1](/messenger/sources#ref-1){: .footnote}[11](/messenger/sources#ref-11){: .footnote}[12](/messenger/sources#ref-12){: .footnote}[36](/messenger/sources#ref-36){: .footnote}
    >
    > <div dir="rtl" lang="ar" class="quran-arabic">﴿وَإِنَّكَ لَعَلَىٰ خُلُقٍ عَظِيمٍ ۝٤﴾ <span class="ayah-ref">سُورَةُ القَلَمِ</span></div>
    {: .quote }

- <span id="src-3044"></span>Closer access did not produce a surviving record of private disillusionment, yet insiders also preserved uncomfortable details. Wives, servants, relatives, and lifelong companions remained deeply committed while transmitting reports of poverty, fatigue, household jealousy, grief, a mistaken agricultural judgment, Qur’anic corrections, and disagreements with him. They also preserved ordinary details of his food, sleep, speech, and domestic work. A later community creating an entirely frictionless saint could easily have omitted much of this material, which makes the witness record more substantial than simple praise literature.[37](/messenger/sources#ref-37){: .footnote}[38](/messenger/sources#ref-38){: .footnote}[39](/messenger/sources#ref-39){: .footnote}[41](/messenger/sources#ref-41){: .footnote}[42](/messenger/sources#ref-42){: .footnote}


## Family, household, and the first reception
{: #family-household-and-the-first-reception }

- <span id="src-2977"></span>**Khadija believed him** before there was any worldly success to share in. Her first response to the revelation rested on the character she had already observed as his wife.

    > "By God, God will never disgrace you." — *Sahih al-Bukhari* 3[2](/messenger/sources#ref-2){: .footnote}[13](/messenger/sources#ref-13){: .footnote}[43](/messenger/sources#ref-43){: .footnote}
    {: .quote }

- <span id="src-2982"></span>Waraqa ibn Nawfal, who was familiar with earlier scripture, understood Prophet Muhammad’s ﷺ first experience within the line of biblical prophecy. According to Bukhari, he identified the messenger of revelation with the one sent to Moses and immediately predicted that Prophet Muhammad ﷺ would face opposition.

    > "This is the bearer of divine secrets whom God sent to Moses. I wish I were young when your people drive you out." — *Sahih al-Bukhari* 3[2](/messenger/sources#ref-2){: .footnote}[13](/messenger/sources#ref-13){: .footnote}[43](/messenger/sources#ref-43){: .footnote}
    {: .quote }

- <span id="src-3027"></span>Anas’s decade of service provides a very different kind of testimony from a dramatic conversion. He was not merely impressed by public preaching. He remembered how Prophet Muhammad ﷺ treated him when chores were forgotten and there was no audience to persuade.

    > "He never said 'uff' [an expression of irritation] to me." — *Sahih al-Bukhari* 6038[2](/messenger/sources#ref-2){: .footnote}[13](/messenger/sources#ref-13){: .footnote}[43](/messenger/sources#ref-43){: .footnote}
    {: .quote }

- <span id="src-3032"></span>Aisha’s testimony comes from the still more intimate setting of marriage. She reports Prophet Muhammad’s ﷺ service at home, avoidance of personal revenge, gentleness, tears, humor, and refusal to strike women or servants.

    > "The Messenger of God ﷺ never struck a woman or a servant." — *Sahih Muslim* 2328a[3](/messenger/sources#ref-3){: .footnote}[14](/messenger/sources#ref-14){: .footnote}[43](/messenger/sources#ref-43){: .footnote}
    {: .quote }

## Opponents and political observers in the transmitted record
{: #opponents-and-political-observers-in-the-transmitted-record }

- <span id="src-2987"></span>**Abu Sufyan gave useful testimony** while he was still Prophet Muhammad’s ﷺ opponent. He rejected the prophetic claim, but when asked about specific matters of character he acknowledged that Prophet Muhammad ﷺ had not previously been accused of lying and was not known for breaking agreements.

    > Asked whether they had accused him of lying before, Abu Sufyan said, "No." — *Sahih al-Bukhari* 7[2](/messenger/sources#ref-2){: .footnote}[13](/messenger/sources#ref-13){: .footnote}[43](/messenger/sources#ref-43){: .footnote}
    {: .quote }

- <span id="src-2992"></span>**Heraclius’s reasoning** is relevant without treating the emperor as an authority on prophethood. He argued from ordinary human credibility: a man who had not been known to lie about people was, in his judgment, an unlikely person suddenly to invent lies about God.

    > Heraclius said in substance: "I knew that if he did not lie about people, he would not lie about God." — *Sahih al-Bukhari* 7[2](/messenger/sources#ref-2){: .footnote}[13](/messenger/sources#ref-13){: .footnote}[43](/messenger/sources#ref-43){: .footnote}
    {: .quote }

- <span id="src-2997"></span>Urwa ibn Masud observed Prophet Muhammad’s ﷺ community as a negotiator for the opposing side at Hudaybiyyah. Urwa had seen the courts of Caesar, Khosrau, and the Negus. He returned to Quraysh saying that he had never seen followers respect a ruler as Prophet Muhammad’s ﷺ companions respected him, and he urged Quraysh to accept the proposed agreement.

    > "I have visited kings... yet I have never seen any of them respected by his companions as Muhammad is respected by his companions." — *Sahih al-Bukhari* 2731-2732[2](/messenger/sources#ref-2){: .footnote}[13](/messenger/sources#ref-13){: .footnote}[43](/messenger/sources#ref-43){: .footnote}
    {: .quote }

## Changed judgments and former opponents
{: #changed-judgments-and-former-opponents }

- <span id="src-3002"></span>Thumama ibn Uthal changed sides after Prophet Muhammad ﷺ released him without extracting payment. Thumama had been captured and held in the mosque for several days, and he offered wealth if ransom was wanted. Prophet Muhammad ﷺ ultimately ordered him released. Thumama left, washed, returned of his own choice, and declared his faith.

    > "There was no face on earth more hateful to me than yours; now your face has become the most beloved face to me." — *Sahih al-Bukhari* 4372[2](/messenger/sources#ref-2){: .footnote}[13](/messenger/sources#ref-13){: .footnote}[43](/messenger/sources#ref-43){: .footnote}
    {: .quote }

- <span id="src-3007"></span>Thumama said that his feelings had changed toward the religion and the city as well as toward Prophet Muhammad ﷺ personally. This is not miraculous proof. It is testimony to the effect of what he observed while detained and then unexpectedly released.

    > "No religion was more hateful to me than yours; now it is the most beloved religion to me." — *Sahih al-Bukhari* 4372[2](/messenger/sources#ref-2){: .footnote}[13](/messenger/sources#ref-13){: .footnote}[43](/messenger/sources#ref-43){: .footnote}
    {: .quote }

- <span id="src-3012"></span>Amr ibn al-As remembered moving from wanting Prophet Muhammad ﷺ dead to loving and revering him deeply. Near his own death, Amr described three phases of his life. During the first, no one was more hated by him than Prophet Muhammad ﷺ; after his conversion, no one was dearer.

    > "No one became dearer to me than the Messenger of God ﷺ, and no one was more majestic in my eyes." — *Sahih Muslim* 121[3](/messenger/sources#ref-3){: .footnote}[14](/messenger/sources#ref-14){: .footnote}[43](/messenger/sources#ref-43){: .footnote}
    {: .quote }

- <span id="src-3017"></span>Safwan ibn Umayya likewise described his hostility turning to love through Prophet Muhammad’s ﷺ generosity. The report openly acknowledges that material gifts helped produce the reconciliation. That makes the account more human and gives concrete content to the way Prophet Muhammad ﷺ treated a defeated opponent.

    > "He kept giving to me until he became the dearest of people to me, though he had been the most hateful." — *Sahih Muslim* 2313[3](/messenger/sources#ref-3){: .footnote}[14](/messenger/sources#ref-14){: .footnote}[43](/messenger/sources#ref-43){: .footnote}
    {: .quote }

- <span id="src-3022"></span>Abdullah ibn Salam, remembered as a learned Jewish figure in Medina, accepted Prophet Muhammad’s ﷺ claim. Bukhari reports that members of his Jewish community initially described him as among their best and most learned, then disparaged him after he announced his belief in Prophet Muhammad ﷺ.

    > They first said of Abdullah, "He is the most learned among us and the best among us." He then came out and testified to Muhammad's prophethood. — *Sahih al-Bukhari* 3329[2](/messenger/sources#ref-2){: .footnote}[13](/messenger/sources#ref-13){: .footnote}[43](/messenger/sources#ref-43){: .footnote}
    {: .quote }

- <span id="src-3046"></span>Several people who had fiercely opposed Prophet Muhammad ﷺ later joined the Muslim community and became important participants in it. Umar is remembered as having set out against him before becoming one of his closest companions. Khalid ibn al-Walid commanded Meccan cavalry against the Muslims at Uhud before becoming a Muslim commander. Abu Sufyan led years of opposition before joining the community. Conversion does not prove prophethood, but repeated reversals among people who directly experienced both sides of the conflict belong in the cumulative human evidence.[9](/messenger/sources#ref-9){: .footnote}[20](/messenger/sources#ref-20){: .footnote}[25](/messenger/sources#ref-25){: .footnote}[28](/messenger/sources#ref-28){: .footnote}[29](/messenger/sources#ref-29){: .footnote}[37](/messenger/sources#ref-37){: .footnote}[41](/messenger/sources#ref-41){: .footnote}[42](/messenger/sources#ref-42){: .footnote}


## Later historical and theological appraisals
{: #later-historical-and-theological-appraisals }

- <span id="src-3105"></span>Praise from outsiders cannot prove prophethood. A historian may admire Prophet Muhammad ﷺ while rejecting revelation, and historical influence is not inherently good. Such judgments therefore belong at the edge of the cumulative case rather than at its center. They remain worth noting because they show how some readers outside Islam have assessed the man and his career.


- <span id="src-3107"></span>Prophet Muhammad’s ﷺ historical stature is recognized far beyond Muslim devotional writing. Michael H. Hart famously ranked him first in *The 100: A Ranking of the Most Influential Persons in History*, emphasizing his combined religious and secular influence. The United States Supreme Court’s courtroom frieze also includes Prophet Muhammad ﷺ among historical lawgivers. Neither fact establishes that he was a prophet, but both show how difficult it is to treat his career as marginal to world history.[74](/messenger/sources#ref-74){: .footnote}[75](/messenger/sources#ref-75){: .footnote}

    > Further context: Michael H. Hart, *The 100: A Ranking of the Most Influential Persons in History*[74](/messenger/sources#ref-74){: .footnote}; U.S. Supreme Court, "Courtroom Friezes: South and North Walls"[75](/messenger/sources#ref-75){: .footnote}
    >
    > [The 100: A Ranking of the Most Influential Persons in History](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_100:_A_Ranking_of_the_Most_Influential_Persons_in_History)
    >
    > [Prophet Muhammad Honored By the U.S. Supreme Court As One Of The Greatest Lawgivers Of The World In 1935](https://www.soundvision.com/article/prophet-muhammad-honored-by-the-us-supreme-court-as-one-of-the-greatest-lawgivers-of-the)
    >
    > [What Non-Muslims Say About Muhammad ﷺ](https://al-islam.org/articles/personalities-what-non-muslims-say-about-muhammad-prophet-islam)
    {: .quote }

- <span id="src-3118"></span>Some non-Muslim religious thinkers have found the simple deliberate-imposture explanation inadequate and have discussed Prophet Muhammad ﷺ in genuinely prophetic terms. The Christian theologian Keith Ward, Regius Professor of Divinity at Oxford, is one modern example: he has written that Christians can see Prophet Muhammad ﷺ "as truly inspired by God, as called to proclaim a strict monotheistic faith, and as chosen by God for that purpose," and that he can be accorded "the highest honor as a true prophet." Such judgments are not independent tests of revelation, and Ward stops short of the Muslim position that Prophet Muhammad ﷺ was the final and definitive voice of revelation, but they show that recognizing Prophet Muhammad’s ﷺ sincerity, or even some prophetic significance in him, is not logically confined to people who already identify as Muslim.[76](/messenger/sources#ref-76){: .footnote}

    > Further discussion: Keith Ward, interview on Muhammad and Islam[76](/messenger/sources#ref-76){: .footnote}
    {: .quote }

- <span id="src-3127"></span>W. Montgomery Watt, a major non-Muslim twentieth-century historian of early Islam, also rejected the simple impostor explanation as inadequate. His conclusion was not an acceptance of Islam, but a historian’s judgment that the theory of conscious fabrication creates more explanatory problems than it resolves.[41](/messenger/sources#ref-41){: .footnote}

    > "His readiness to undergo persecution for his beliefs, the high moral character of the men who believed in him and looked up to him as leader, and the greatness of his ultimate achievement — all argue his fundamental integrity. To suppose Muhammad an impostor raises more problems than it solves." — W. Montgomery Watt, *Muhammad at Mecca* (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1953), p. 52[41](/messenger/sources#ref-41){: .footnote}
    {: .quote }
