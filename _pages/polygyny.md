Islam permits a man to have up to four wives at one time, while a woman may have only one husband. The asymmetry is real. Qur'an 4:3, however, places polygyny in a passage about protecting orphans, limits an existing practice, makes justice a condition, and directs a man to marry one if he fears he cannot meet that condition.

**Terms.** *Polygyny* means one man having several wives; *polygamy* is the broader term for plural marriage; *polyandry* means one woman having several husbands. A *hadith* is a report about what the Prophet ﷺ said or did; *Sahih al-Bukhari* and *Sahih Muslim* are the two most rigorously screened hadith collections. *Mahr* means the marriage gift a husband pays directly to his wife.

## The objection

1. Islam allows a man four wives but a woman only one husband.
2. The asymmetry appears designed for male sexual interests.
3. Having to share a husband can itself be seen as placing a wife below him, whatever rules govern time or financial support.
4. The Prophet ﷺ had more than the four wives permitted to ordinary men.

The first and fourth points are not disputed here. The argument concerns whether the asymmetry exists chiefly for male desire and whether plural marriage necessarily places a wife beneath her husband.

## What Qur'an 4:3 actually permits

The verse begins with the rights of orphans, not with "two, three, or four":

> "If you fear you might fail to give orphan women their ˹due˺ rights ... then marry other women of your choice — two, three, or four. But if you are afraid you will fail to maintain justice, then ˹content yourselves with˺ one..."
> (**Sūrat al-Nisāʾ 4:3**), Dr. Mustafa Khattab, *The Clear Quran*
>
> <div dir="rtl" lang="ar" class="quran-arabic">﴿وَإِنْ خِفْتُمْ أَلَّا تُقْسِطُوا۟ فِى ٱلْيَتَـٰمَىٰ ... فَإِنْ خِفْتُمْ أَلَّا تَعْدِلُوا۟ فَوَٰحِدَةً﴾ <span class="ayah-ref">[سُورَةُ النِّسَاءِ: ٣]</span></div>
{: .quote }

The verse addresses guardians who could exploit orphaned women under their control. Marrying other women is presented as the alternative to that abuse.

The passage was revealed after Uhud, when the Muslim community had lost many fighting men and was left with widows and orphaned children lacking independent support. Plural marriage could bring them into households responsible for their care.

Pre-Islamic Arabia had no fixed ceiling on wives. Qur'an 4:3 set the maximum at four, and men who already had more were required to release those beyond the limit. The verse restricted an existing practice rather than creating an unlimited new privilege.

It also makes justice a condition. A man who fears he cannot be just is told to marry one. Four is a maximum, not a recommendation.

The verse also refers to women held in slavery, an institution the Qur'an addressed in its historical society. That separate subject is not examined on this page.

## Justice is a condition

Later in the same surah, the Qur'an states:

> "You will never be able to maintain ˹emotional˺ justice between your wives — no matter how keen you are. So do not totally incline towards one leaving the other in suspense."
> (**Sūrat al-Nisāʾ 4:129**), Dr. Mustafa Khattab, *The Clear Quran*
>
> <div dir="rtl" lang="ar" class="quran-arabic">﴿وَلَن تَسْتَطِيعُوٓا۟ أَن تَعْدِلُوا۟ بَيْنَ ٱلنِّسَآءِ وَلَوْ حَرَصْتُمْ﴾ <span class="ayah-ref">[سُورَةُ النِّسَاءِ: ١٢٩]</span></div>
{: .quote }

The classical explanation distinguishes feelings, which cannot be commanded, from treatment, which can. Verse 4:129 does not prohibit plural marriage; it forbids favoring one wife so heavily that another is left suspended.


> "When a man has two wives and he is inclined to one of them, he will come
> on the Day of Resurrection with a side hanging down."
> — *Sunan Abi Dawud* 2133; also *Jami' at-Tirmidhi* 1141, *Sunan an-Nasa'i*
> 3942, *Sunan Ibn Majah* 1969. Graded *sahih* in most assessments, with one
> dissenting *da'if*.[1](#ref-1){: .footnote}
{: .src }

Islamic law accordingly treated justice as measurable in matters such as nights, maintenance, housing, and treatment. A wife could take a husband who failed those duties to court. A hadith warns that a man who unjustly favors one of two wives will appear on the Day of Resurrection with one side hanging down.[1](#ref-1){: .footnote}

A second marriage therefore creates another set of enforceable obligations rather than simply expanding a man's marital options.

## Permission is not recommendation

The Qur'an never presents polygyny as better, as a reward, or as something men should seek. It permits it conditionally in a passage concerned with vulnerable dependents.

Elsewhere, marriage is described through "comfort," "compassion," and "mercy" (**Sūrat al-Rūm 30:21**), and husbands are told to "live with them in kindness" (**Sūrat al-Nisāʾ 4:19**). The command applies equally in plural households.

Plural marriage has historically remained a minority arrangement in Muslim societies. The cost of maintaining separate households has itself limited its prevalence.

## The Prophet's marriages—and a marriage he refused

The Prophet ﷺ exceeded the ordinary four-wife limit, but his own marital history was not one of lifelong plural marriage.

He was married to Khadija alone for twenty-five years, from about age twenty-five until her death when he was around fifty. This covered his early adulthood and the entire first phase of his mission. His plural marriages came later, mostly after the migration to Medina. Most were to widows, several were to women past youth, and several established alliances between clans. This pattern, together with the criticisms made of it, is examined in [The Prophet Muhammad's ﷺ Marriage to Aisha](/aisha).

The Qur'an gave him a dispensation from the ordinary limit, then prohibited him from marrying further. His wives could not remarry after his death and were offered a choice between the life of the world and remaining with him under the conditions he could provide.[2](#ref-2){: .footnote} The dispensation came with restrictions of its own.

Ali ibn Abi Talib, while married to the Prophet's ﷺ daughter Fatima, later sought to marry the daughter of Abu Jahl. Fatima went to her father, who said publicly:

> "I do not give permission, and will not give permission ... For Fatima is a part of me ... and what hurts her, hurts me."
> — *Sahih al-Bukhari* 5230; also 3729 and *Sahih Muslim* 6309–6310.[3](#ref-3){: .footnote}
{: .src }

Ali did not proceed.

The report does **not** establish a general prohibition of polygyny. Its reasoning refers to Fatima's particular status, and a parallel version says that the daughter of the Messenger of God and the daughter of the enemy of God should not be wives of the same man. In this case, however, the harm a second marriage would cause an existing wife was sufficient to prevent it publicly, despite Ali's standing.

## Can a wife require monogamy?

An Islamic marriage is a contract, and conditions in that contract can be enforceable. A woman may stipulate that her husband will not marry another wife.

The legal schools differ over the remedy. The Hanbali position is the most explicit: it treats such a condition as binding and gives the wife the right to dissolve the marriage if her husband breaks it.[4](#ref-4){: .footnote}

A woman can therefore secure monogamy before marriage, when her consent is still required and her bargaining position is strongest.

## Why is the permission one-sided?

None of the preceding removes the basic asymmetry: a man may have several wives, while a woman may not have several husbands. The traditional explanations do not erase that fact, but they place it within a broader legal structure.

**Lineage.** The classical explanation focused on paternity. Islamic law ties inheritance, maintenance, guardianship, and prohibited degrees of marriage to an identifiable father. Under polyandry, paternity could historically be uncertain. Genetic testing makes this argument less forceful today.

**Financial obligations.** The stronger explanation concerns the husband's legal burdens. He pays the *mahr*, owes his wife maintenance and housing, and supports the children. He acquires no claim over her property or earnings, which remain hers. Plural marriage therefore multiplies his liabilities without increasing his legal entitlement to his wives' wealth.

**Historical welfare function.** War could leave widows and children without independent income. Plural marriage could bring them into households against which they had enforceable claims rather than leave them destitute. This historical function does not determine how the permission should operate today.

The asymmetry remains. It operates, however, within a system of legal and financial responsibilities rather than as an isolated male entitlement. Describing polygyny simply as a concession to appetite omits the conditions and liabilities attached to it.

## Where the criticism is justified

Some men take second wives without the means to maintain two households, fail to divide their time fairly, or do so without informing the first wife. Such conduct violates the Qur'anic condition of justice.

Communities can also fail to enforce women's remedies, leaving women to bear the social cost of using rights that formally exist.

Qur'an 4:3 is also often quoted from the middle: "marry two, three, or four" is repeated without its opening concern for orphans or its closing instruction to marry one when justice is feared. That omission is not always innocent.

Tunisia prohibits plural marriage outright, while several other Muslim-majority jurisdictions require judicial permission, proof of financial means, or notification of an existing wife.[5](#ref-5){: .footnote} Such regulations are debated within Islamic legal frameworks because Qur'an 4:3 makes the permission conditional; a state may restrict it when it concludes that the condition is not being met.

## Conclusion

Qur'an 4:3 permits polygyny while limiting an existing practice to four wives, placing the rule in a passage concerned with vulnerable dependents, and directing a man to marry one if he fears he cannot be just. Islamic law attaches concrete financial and personal duties to any plural marriage.

The asymmetry with polyandry remains real, and the traditional explanations do not remove every reason for discomfort. They place the rule within a wider structure of lineage, financial responsibility, and protection of dependents rather than reducing it to a simple licence for male appetite.
