Classical Islamic law prescribes amputation for theft, stoning for adultery by a married person, and flogging for fornication, false accusation, and intoxication. These penalties are severe and plainly present in the sources.

They were not treated as automatic responses to an offence. Classical jurists attached demanding rules of proof, procedures designed to avoid fixed penalties where doubt remained, repentance provisions, and social conditions governing whether those penalties could be imposed.

These rules reflect an important distinction: Islamic law did not treat every sin as something authorities should uncover and prosecute. A person's wrongdoing and repentance before God are one question; whether a court may impose a fixed penalty is another. The *hudud* belonged to a much narrower legal sphere in which an offence had to be lawfully established under exacting rules.

The death penalty for apostasy is a related but separate issue, discussed at [Apostasy in Islam](/ridda).

*Terms:* the *hudud* (singular *hadd*, “limit”) are a small class of offences whose penalties are fixed in the sources; *taʿzir* is discretionary punishment set by a judge. *Hiraba* means armed robbery or brigandage. A *hadith* is a report of something the Prophet ﷺ said or did, transmitted through a named chain and assessed for reliability; *Sahih al-Bukhari* and *Sahih Muslim* are the most rigorously screened collections, while reports graded *da'if* are weak and cannot establish a legal ruling on their own.

## The objection

Critics object that Islamic law amputates the hands of thieves and stones adulterers; these punishments are cruel and disproportionate; they are not marginal interpretations but appear in the canonical hadith collections and all four schools of law; and some Muslim-majority states still enforce them.

The punishments are genuinely part of the classical tradition, and modern enforcement is not merely a historical concern. Describing only the maximum punishments, however, omits the legal structure that determined when they could actually be imposed.

## The fixed penalties were tightly constrained

The *hudud* form a short, closed list: theft, brigandage, fornication, false accusation of fornication, and intoxication. Their penalties are fixed in the sources rather than left to a judge’s discretion.

Fixing both the offence and the penalty limits rulers as well as offenders by restricting arbitrary punishment. Matters outside this list fall under *taʿzir*, where judges have discretion, and in practice most criminal cases were handled there.

A fixed penalty is not automatic. It is reached only if the legal requirements for imposing it are satisfied. The severity of the stated punishment therefore cannot be separated from the narrow legal route by which a case could reach it.

## The evidentiary bar

For fornication, the standard of proof is exceptionally high: four eyewitnesses must have seen the act itself and agree about what they saw. Circumstantial evidence is not enough.

An accuser who fails to produce the required witnesses is himself flogged eighty lashes, and his testimony is permanently rejected thereafter.[1](#ref-1){: .footnote} The rule makes unsupported accusation dangerous for the accuser and protects the accused from having a reputation destroyed by allegation.

Confession was also actively discouraged. When Maʿiz ibn Malik came to confess fornication, the Prophet ﷺ repeatedly turned him away and had his people asked whether anything was wrong with his mind.[2](#ref-2){: .footnote} When Maʿiz persisted, he was offered lesser explanations:

> “Perhaps you kissed, or squeezed, or looked?” He said: “No.”
> — *Sunan Abi Dawud* 4427; also *Sahih al-Bukhari* 6824[3](#ref-3){: .footnote}
{: .src }

The procedure sought a credible alternative that would avoid the fixed penalty rather than simply securing a conviction.

### Private wrongdoing was not to be hunted out

This fits a broader prohibition on *tajassus* — seeking out concealed faults. The Qur'an commands, “do not spy” (49:12). When a man was brought to Ibn Masʿud with wine dripping from his beard, Ibn Masʿud replied that they had been forbidden to seek out faults; if wrongdoing became manifest, they could act on it.[4](#ref-4){: .footnote}

The point is not that private wrongdoing ceased to be wrong. It is that moral guilt and legally punishable guilt were not identical. The law could condemn an act while limiting the authority of rulers and judges to search people's private lives in order to expose it.

### Doubt favoured the accused

Islamic criminal procedure also placed strong barriers in the way of imposing the most severe fixed punishments. Jurists developed the maxim *idra'u al-hudud bi'l-shubuhat* — roughly, “ward off fixed punishments wherever a legitimate doubt can be found” — and surrounded *hudud* with restrictive definitions and demanding proof.

This principle was operated across the legal schools of thought and helped make the *hudud* rare in practice. This was not a loophole around the law; avoiding the fixed punishment where a legally significant doubt remained became part of the law itself.

### Rare in practice

The constraints had practical effects. In his study of Ottoman criminal law, Rudolph Peters describes amputation for theft as rare and stoning as extremely rare; the only Ottoman stoning he found was a highly exceptional case in Istanbul in 1680.[6](#ref-6){: .footnote} Surviving records are incomplete and cannot establish a total for all Muslim societies, but they are consistent with a system in which the fixed penalties were difficult to reach.

Avoiding a *hadd* did not necessarily mean avoiding accountability. Conduct that failed the requirements for a fixed penalty could still, where the law allowed, be handled under *taʿzir*.

## The punishment was not automatic

### Repentance and forgiveness

The Qur’an places repentance alongside the punishment verses. This matters because worldly punishment and forgiveness before God are distinct questions: the sources do not present state punishment as the route by which a sinner earns God's forgiveness.

For *hiraba*:

> “Except for those who return [repenting] before you apprehend them. And know that God is Forgiving and Merciful.” (**Sūrat al-Māʾidah 5:34**), Saheeh International, with “Allah” rendered as “God”
>
> <div dir="rtl" lang="ar" class="quran-arabic">﴿إِلَّا ٱلَّذِينَ تَابُوا۟ مِن قَبْلِ أَن تَقْدِرُوا۟ عَلَيْهِمْ ۖ فَٱعْلَمُوٓا۟ أَنَّ ٱللَّهَ غَفُورٌ رَّحِيمٌ ۝٣٤﴾ <span class="ayah-ref">سُورَةُ المَائـِدَةِ</span></div>
{: .quote }

For theft, in the verse immediately after the amputation verse:

> “But whoever repents after his wrongdoing and reforms, indeed, God will turn to him in forgiveness.” (**Sūrat al-Māʾidah 5:39**), Saheeh International, with “Allah” rendered as “God”
>
> <div dir="rtl" lang="ar" class="quran-arabic">﴿فَمَن تَابَ مِنۢ بَعْدِ ظُلْمِهِۦ وَأَصْلَحَ فَإِنَّ ٱللَّهَ يَتُوبُ عَلَيْهِ ۗ إِنَّ ٱللَّهَ غَفُورٌ رَّحِيمٌ ۝٣٩﴾ <span class="ayah-ref">سُورَةُ المَائـِدَةِ</span></div>
{: .quote }

The legal effect of repentance differs by offence. Qur'an 5:34 expressly exempts those who repent from *hiraba* before capture, while 5:39 promises divine forgiveness after theft without itself cancelling the earthly penalty. The point is not that repentance automatically erases every *hadd*, but that a person's standing before God is not reducible to whether the state punishes them.

### Social conditions

Classical jurists did not regard the *hudud* as applicable without regard to social conditions.

As caliph, Umar ibn al-Khattab suspended the theft penalty during the famine year known as the *ʿam al-ramada*. His reasoning was that someone who steals food to survive is not the kind of thief contemplated by the penalty. The report appears in juristic and historical literature rather than the canonical hadith collections, and jurists cite it as a precedent for suspending a *hadd* when its social preconditions are absent.[7](#ref-7){: .footnote}

The state therefore could not treat necessity and its own social obligations as irrelevant and then impose the maximum penalty mechanically. The circumstances surrounding the offence formed part of determining whether the *hadd* was legally available at all.

## Modern enforcement

Some states apply these penalties without the preconditions that made them difficult to impose in classical law. If a state retains the punishment while discarding the evidentiary standards, the prohibition on prying into concealed faults, the doubt maxim, the repentance provisions, and the social conditions that constrained it, it is not applying the legal structure described above. It preserves the severity while abandoning the restraints.

Likewise, *zina* accusations have been used against women who report rape. That reverses the purpose of the four-witness rule, which makes unsupported accusation costly in order to protect the accused.

These practices can be criticized from within the Islamic sources, not only from outside them.

## Conclusion

The *hudud* penalties are severe and genuinely present in the sources. But classical Islamic law did not authorize rulers to seek out every sin and punish every occurrence they could discover. It distinguished moral wrongdoing from the much narrower category of wrongdoing that could be publicly and legally established for a fixed penalty.

That narrower category was constrained by demanding proof, a prohibition on prying into concealed faults, procedures that favoured avoiding the fixed penalty where doubt remained, repentance, and social conditions that the society itself had to take seriously. A concealed sin could remain a matter of repentance before God; a *hadd* required a legally established case.

An account that lists only the maximum punishments while omitting the rules that determined whether they could be imposed therefore gives a distorted picture of Islamic criminal law.
