Classical Islamic law prescribes amputation for theft, stoning for adultery by a married person, and flogging for fornication, false accusation, and intoxication. These penalties are severe, and they are plainly present in the sources. Any serious account should begin by acknowledging that.

But the penalties are only one part of the law. Classical jurists also attached demanding rules of proof, procedures designed to avoid fixed penalties where doubt remained, repentance provisions, and social conditions that governed whether those penalties could be imposed at all. This article explains that framework.

The death penalty for apostasy is a related but separate issue, discussed at [Apostasy in Islam](/ridda).

*A note on terms:* the *hudud* (singular *hadd*, “limit”) are a small class of offences whose penalties are fixed in the sources; *taʿzir* is discretionary punishment set by a judge. *Hiraba* means armed robbery or brigandage. A *hadith* is a report of something the Prophet ﷺ said or did, transmitted through a named chain and assessed for reliability; *Sahih al-Bukhari* and *Sahih Muslim* are the most rigorously screened collections, while reports graded *da'if* are weak and cannot establish a legal ruling on their own.

## What the objection gets right

The standard criticism is straightforward: Islamic law amputates the hands of thieves and stones adulterers; these punishments are cruel and disproportionate; they are not marginal interpretations but appear in the canonical hadith collections and all four schools of law; and some Muslim-majority states still enforce them.

The penalties are indeed part of the classical tradition, and modern enforcement is not merely a historical concern. What is misleading is to describe the maximum punishments without the legal structure that determined when they could actually be imposed.

## The fixed penalties were tightly constrained

The *hudud* form a short, closed list: theft, brigandage, fornication, false accusation of fornication, and intoxication. Their penalties are fixed in the sources rather than left to a judge’s discretion.

That fixed character limits the ruler as well as the offender. It defines both the offence and the maximum punishment instead of giving authorities an open-ended licence to punish. Matters outside this list fall under *taʿzir*, where judges have discretion, and in practice most criminal cases were handled there.

More importantly, a fixed penalty is not automatic. It is reached only if the legal requirements for imposing it are satisfied.

## The evidentiary bar

For fornication, the standard of proof is exceptionally high: four eyewitnesses must have seen the act itself and agree about what they saw. Circumstantial evidence is not enough.

An accuser who fails to produce the required witnesses is himself flogged eighty lashes, and his testimony is permanently rejected thereafter.[1](#ref-1){: .footnote} The rule makes unsupported accusation dangerous for the accuser and is meant to protect women from having their reputations destroyed by allegation.

Confession was also actively discouraged. When Maʿiz ibn Malik came to confess fornication, the Prophet ﷺ repeatedly turned him away and had his people asked whether anything was wrong with his mind.[2](#ref-2){: .footnote} When Maʿiz persisted, he was offered lesser explanations:

> “Perhaps you kissed, or squeezed, or looked?” He said: “No.”
> — *Sunan Abi Dawud* 4427; also *Sahih al-Bukhari* 6824[3](#ref-3){: .footnote}
{: .src }

The point is difficult to miss: the procedure was not designed to secure a conviction as quickly as possible. It deliberately looked for a credible alternative that would avoid the fixed penalty.

### Doubt favoured the accused

Jurists expressed this approach in the maxim *idraʾu al-hudud bi'l-shubuhat* — “avert the fixed penalties by doubts.” It operated across the schools and helped make the *hudud* rare in practice.

The hadith commonly cited for this maxim is weak, and that should be stated clearly. The wording attributed to the Prophet ﷺ is graded *da'if* in *Jami' at-Tirmidhi* 1424 and *da'if*, indeed “very *da'if*,” in *Sunan Ibn Majah* 2545.[4](#ref-4){: .footnote} The maxim’s legal authority therefore does not rest on a sound Prophetic chain. It comes from its acceptance by jurists, supported by the practice described above and by reports from the Companions.

## The punishment was not automatic

### Repentance

The Qur’an places repentance alongside the punishment verses, an unusual feature in a penal code.

For *hiraba*:

> “Except for those who return [repenting] before you apprehend them. And know that Allah is Forgiving and Merciful.” (**Sūrat al-Māʾidah 5:34**), Saheeh International
>

For theft, in the verse immediately after the amputation verse:

> “But whoever repents after his wrongdoing and reforms, indeed, Allah will turn to him in forgiveness.” (**Sūrat al-Māʾidah 5:39**), Saheeh International
>

### Social conditions

Classical jurists also did not treat the *hudud* as applicable without regard to the society in which an offence occurred.

As caliph, Umar ibn al-Khattab suspended the theft penalty during the famine year known as the *ʿam al-ramada*. His reasoning was that someone who steals food to survive is not the kind of thief contemplated by the penalty. The report appears in juristic and historical literature rather than the canonical hadith collections, and jurists cite it as a precedent for suspending a *hadd* when its social preconditions are absent.[5](#ref-5){: .footnote}

The broader principle is that a punishment for theft presupposes a society that has already met its obligations of zakat, maintenance, and public provision, so that nobody needs to steal to survive. Imposing the penalty while neglecting those obligations reverses the proper order.

## Modern enforcement

This framework does not answer every criticism by appealing to legal theory.

Some states apply these penalties without the preconditions that made them difficult to impose in classical law. If a state retains the punishment while discarding the evidentiary standards, the doubt maxim, the repentance provisions, and the social obligations that constrained it, it is not applying the legal structure described above. It preserves the severity while abandoning the restraints.

Likewise, *zina* accusations have been used against women who report rape. That reverses the purpose of the four-witness rule, which makes accusation costly in order to protect the accused.

This does not defend those states. The case against their conduct can be made from within the Islamic sources, not only from outside them.

## Conclusion

The *hudud* penalties are severe and genuinely present in the sources. But classical Islamic law did not treat them as automatic responses to an offence. Their application depended on demanding standards of proof, procedures that favoured avoiding the fixed penalty where doubt remained, provisions for repentance, and social preconditions that the society itself was expected to fulfil.

The penalties therefore cannot be understood apart from the procedures governing them. An account that lists only the maximum punishments while omitting the rules that determine whether they may be imposed gives a distorted picture of Islamic criminal law.
