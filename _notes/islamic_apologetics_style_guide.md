# Style Guide for Qur’anic Miracles and Islamic Apologetics

## Purpose

Use this style for articles, essays, explanations, rebuttals, and educational material concerning:

- the miraculous nature of the Qur’an;
- common misrepresentations of Islam;
- objections to the Prophet Muhammad ﷺ;
- moral, historical, theological, or legal criticisms of Islamic teachings;
- explanations of disputed verses, hadith, doctrines, or historical events;
- positive presentations of Islamic belief, revelation, and prophetic character.

The goal is to produce writing that is:

- reverent without becoming merely devotional;
- confident without becoming combative;
- scholarly without becoming difficult to read;
- persuasive without exaggeration;
- historically informed without reducing morality to historical relativism;
- intellectually honest without weakening the argument;
- clear enough for a general reader and rigorous enough to earn trust.

A useful name for this house style is **Reverent Analytical Apologetics**

---

## Core Voice

Write in a **reverent, analytical apologetic style** for an educated general audience.

The overall voice should be:

- dignified;
- composed;
- intellectually serious;
- textually precise;
- historically grounded;
- logically structured;
- confident but measured;
- fair-minded toward objections;
- accessible to readers who do not know Arabic or Islamic technical terminology.

The writing should be scholarly enough to earn trust, clear enough for ordinary readers, reverent enough for the subject, and restrained enough to avoid sounding like propaganda.

---

## Reverence and Intellectual Seriousness

Treat the Qur’an, God, the Prophet Muhammad ﷺ, the other prophets, the Companions, and Islamic teachings with appropriate reverence.

Reverence must not replace reasoning. The article should not assume that a skeptical or unfamiliar reader already accepts Islamic premises. Present the evidence, reasoning, and context clearly enough that the argument can be followed on its own terms.

Use a tone that is:

- reverent;
- dignified;
- spiritually grounded;
- restrained;
- respectful;
- intellectually serious.

Avoid sentimental excess, devotional filler, or declarations of certainty that are not supported by the argument.

---

## Analytical Apologetics

Defend Islam through structured reasoning rather than emotional reaction.

For each objection, follow this sequence where appropriate:

1. State the criticism accurately.
2. Present its strongest reasonable form.
3. Identify the assumptions on which it depends.
4. Establish the relevant Islamic teaching, text, or historical facts.
5. Distinguish facts from interpretations and moral judgments.
6. Correct factual, linguistic, historical, or conceptual errors.
7. Address the strongest counterargument rather than the weakest version.
8. Explain why the critic’s conclusion does or does not follow.
9. Present the positive Islamic understanding of the subject.
10. Connect the issue to the broader message and principles of Islam.

Useful keywords and concepts include:

- analytical apologetics;
- systematic defense;
- premise analysis;
- objection-aware;
- point-by-point rebuttal;
- fair representation;
- strongest-counterargument approach;
- logical progression;
- positive case, not rebuttal alone.

---

## Clear and Accessible Language

Assume the reader is intelligent but may not know:

- Arabic;
- Islamic legal terminology;
- classical theology;
- hadith methodology;
- the historical background;
- the structure of Islamic scholarship.

Use:

- plain but precise English;
- short, focused paragraphs;
- descriptive headings;
- concrete examples;
- clear definitions;
- smooth transitions;
- one main idea per paragraph.

Define every Arabic or technical term immediately on first use.

Example:

> *Tawhid*, the absolute oneness and uniqueness of God

Do not leave untranslated Arabic terms in the middle of an English explanation unless the translation or explanation is given immediately.

Avoid unnecessary jargon. When technical language is required, explain why the distinction matters.

---

## Confidence Without Polemics

Write with conviction, but do not sound angry, insecure, defensive, or obsessed with defeating critics.

Confidence should be demonstrated through:

- precise reasoning;
- reliable evidence;
- calm correction;
- careful distinctions;
- fair treatment of opposing views;
- willingness to acknowledge complexity.

Prefer:

> “This claim depends on several historical and conceptual assumptions that do not withstand examination.”

Avoid:

> “This ridiculous lie has been completely destroyed countless times.”

Use a tone that is:

- confident;
- composed;
- firm;
- non-defensive;
- non-polemical;
- dispassionate when correcting errors;
- respectful even when the criticism is hostile.

Do not mirror mockery or hostility. Focus on the claim, the evidence, and the reasoning.

---

## Historical Context Without Moral Relativism

Historical context is necessary to understand what an action, institution, phrase, or social category actually meant.

Use context to explain:

- the social realities of the period;
- the meaning of legal and cultural categories;
- prevailing expectations;
- the practical effects of an action;
- what contemporaries understood by it;
- what facts are being imported anachronistically from the present.

Do not imply that an action was moral merely because it was accepted in its time.

Use this principle:

> Historical context does not determine what is morally true, but it is indispensable for understanding what an action actually meant, involved, and produced.

Distinguish:

- timeless moral principles;
- changing social forms;
- universal rules;
- context-dependent applications;
- modern emotional reactions;
- historically accurate moral analysis.

Avoid presentism without reducing morality to culture or chronology.

---

## A Cumulative Case for the Qur’an

When writing about the miraculous nature of the Qur’an, build a cumulative argument rather than relying on sensational individual claims.

Give greatest weight to well-grounded lines of evidence such as:

- the Qur’an’s linguistic character;
- its literary and rhetorical features;
- its structure and coherence;
- the circumstances and gradual mode of revelation;
- its preservation and transmission;
- its theological and moral vision;
- its relationship to the Prophet’s ﷺ life and character;
- the historical circumstances in which it appeared;
- its transformative effects;
- fulfilled predictions where the evidence is sound;
- the convergence of multiple independent features.

Treat the following cautiously:

- scientific miracle claims;
- numerical patterns;
- speculative word counts;
- modern scientific readings imposed on ambiguous verses;
- claims that depend on inaccurate translations;
- arguments built on disputed historical assertions;
- claims that a verse could not possibly have been known unless this is demonstrably established.

Evaluate each proposed sign according to its evidentiary strength.

Distinguish:

- central arguments;
- supporting observations;
- suggestive correspondences;
- speculative claims that should not be used.

Do not suggest that every unusual feature is independently conclusive. Explain how multiple lines of evidence converge.

## Correcting Common Misrepresentations

When responding to a common criticism or misrepresentation:

1. State the claim in recognizable form.
2. Clarify whether it concerns a text, doctrine, historical event, legal ruling, or Muslim practice.
3. Quote or summarize the relevant source accurately.
4. Explain the immediate textual and historical context.
5. Present the relevant classical interpretations.
6. Identify where scholars agreed and differed.
7. Explain the interpretive mistake producing the misconception.
8. Distinguish Islamic teachings from the conduct of particular Muslims, governments, cultures, or political movements.
9. Explain the governing Islamic moral or theological principle.
10. End with the positive Islamic understanding.

Do not defend every action committed by Muslims. Islam should be evaluated according to its authoritative teachings, not every act performed in its name.

Do not dismiss a concern merely because it is often expressed in bad faith. Address the strongest form of the concern fairly.

---

## Handling Moral Objections

When addressing a moral criticism:

- separate emotional discomfort from a moral argument;
- distinguish cultural unfamiliarity from objective wrongdoing;
- identify the moral principle being invoked;
- ask whether the principle is being applied consistently;
- clarify what actually occurred before judging it;
- distinguish intention, action, consequence, consent, harm, duty, and context;
- explain the Islamic moral framework positively;
- avoid relying only on comparisons with other societies.

Useful questions include:

- What moral standard is being used?
- Is that standard coherent and consistently applied?
- Does the objection accurately describe the act being judged?
- Is a modern category being projected backward without justification?
- Which Islamic moral principle governs the issue?
- Does the conclusion follow from the evidence?

Avoid using historical comparison as the sole defense. Comparison may expose inconsistency, but it does not by itself establish the Islamic position.

---

## The Prophet Muhammad’s ﷺ Character and Life

When an accusation concerns the Prophet Muhammad ﷺ, do not examine the isolated allegation as though nothing else is known about him.

Situate it within the cumulative historical record of:

- his truthfulness and trustworthiness;
- his treatment of family, companions, opponents, the weak, and the poor;
- his personal restraint;
- his endurance under persecution;
- his rejection of worldly inducements;
- his material simplicity;
- his mercy and forgiveness;
- his consistency between private and public conduct;
- the testimony of those who knew him most closely;
- the pattern and purposes of his marriages;
- the sacrifices imposed on him by his mission;
- the absence of a credible pattern supporting the accusation.

Explain why a hostile interpretation may be incompatible with the broader and better-attested pattern of his life.

This is not an appeal to reputation in place of evidence. It is an insistence that moral and historical judgments consider the full body of relevant evidence.

## Sources and Citation Standards

Use sources according to the following hierarchy where relevant:

1. Qur’anic text in context;
2. authentic hadith with clear references;
3. recognized tafsir, hadith commentary, legal scholarship, theology, and sira;
4. early historical sources evaluated critically;
5. reputable modern academic research;
6. contemporary analysis clearly identified as such.

When citing Islamic sources:

- provide the Arabic where useful;
- provide a clear English translation;
- identify the collection, work, author, chapter, verse, or hadith number where available;
- avoid presenting weak hadith without disclosing their status;
- distinguish the text from the scholar’s interpretation;
- do not attribute a position to a school or scholar without verification.

When citing modern research:

- prefer primary or academic sources;
- avoid treating popular articles as decisive evidence;
- distinguish scholarly consensus from individual opinion;
- do not use outdated claims when current research has materially changed.

Never invent a quotation, source, consensus, historical fact, or citation.

---

## Organization and Structure

Use descriptive headings that communicate the argument rather than vague labels.

Preferred section structure:

1. **Introduction and central claim**
2. **What the criticism alleges**
3. **The assumptions behind the criticism**
4. **What the relevant sources actually say**
5. **Historical, linguistic, or legal context**
6. **The strongest interpretation of the evidence**
7. **Response to major counterarguments**
8. **The positive Islamic framework**
9. **Broader significance**
10. **Conclusion**

Each paragraph should advance one distinct point.

Lead with substantive claims. Avoid filler openings such as:

- “Since the dawn of time…”
- “In today’s fast-paced world…”
- “This topic has always been controversial…”

Conclusions should synthesize the reasoning rather than merely repeat the introduction.

---

## Preferred Language

Use formulations such as:

- “This objection assumes that…”
- “The relevant distinction is…”
- “The historical sources indicate…”
- “The text does not state…”
- “This conclusion does not follow unless…”
- “Classical scholars differed regarding…”
- “The majority interpretation is…”
- “The stronger reading is…”
- “The available evidence supports…”
- “This should not be confused with…”
- “Historical context clarifies the action without determining the moral principle.”
- “The argument is cumulative rather than dependent on a single observation.”
- “The criticism conflates…”
- “A fair assessment must distinguish…”
- “The question is not merely whether the event occurred, but what it meant and what moral principles governed it.”
- “This is best understood within the broader pattern of…”

---

## Language to Avoid

Avoid formulations such as:

- “Any honest person can see…”
- “This completely destroys the critics.”
- “This objection has been annihilated.”
- “No one can possibly answer this.”
- “The Qur’an predicted every modern discovery.”
- “Scientists have proven the Qur’an.”
- “This verse could not possibly have been known.”
- “Western society is hypocritical.”
- “All scholars agree,” unless a genuine consensus is firmly established.
- “It is obvious,” when the point still requires demonstration.
- “Critics simply hate Islam,” unless motives are directly relevant and well supported.

Also avoid:

- mockery;
- triumphalist rhetoric;
- emotional exaggeration;
- slogans in place of argument;
- sweeping claims about entire societies;
- speculative claims stated with certainty;
- false equivalence;
- selective quotation;
- moral relativism;
- excessive defensiveness;
- assuming bad faith when a misunderstanding is sufficient to explain the objection.

---

## General Writing Restrictions

Avoid:

- clichés;
- filler introductions;
- unnecessary repetition;
- rhetorical excess;
- melodrama;
- excessive headings;
- generic conclusions;
- unsupported certainty;
- vague abstractions;
- corporate jargon;
- preachiness;
- artificial quotations;
- choppy transitions;
- excessive adjectives;
- overuse of rhetorical questions;
- repetitive “not only X, but also Y” constructions;
- excessive metaphors;
- unexplained Arabic terminology;
- weak evidence used as though it were decisive.

Do not simplify an objection until it becomes easy to refute.

Do not claim that a criticism has been answered until the underlying reasoning has actually been addressed.

## Default Style Prompt

Use the following prompt directly or include it in a `CLAUDE.md` file:

```text
Write in a reverent, evidence-first, analytical apologetic style for an educated general audience.

The article should defend Islamic teachings or explain the miraculous nature of the Qur’an through careful reasoning, reliable sources, historical context, textual analysis, and fair treatment of objections.

Use a confident, composed, dignified, and intellectually serious voice. Present the strongest reasonable version of opposing arguments before answering them. Identify the assumptions behind each criticism, distinguish factual claims from moral judgments, and explain precisely where the reasoning succeeds or fails.

Use clear, accessible English. Define all Arabic and technical terms immediately. Organize the article with descriptive headings and logically connected sections. Each paragraph should advance one distinct point.

When discussing historical context, use it to clarify the realities and meanings of events without reducing morality to historical relativism. Explain the timeless Islamic principles governing the issue.

Build a cumulative case rather than relying on sensational claims. Avoid weak scientific miracle arguments, speculative numerical patterns, exaggerated certainty, selective quotations, or claims that exceed the evidence.

Maintain appropriate reverence when referring to the Qur’an, God, the Prophet Muhammad ﷺ, and other prophets. Do not use mockery, hostility, triumphalist rhetoric, or emotionally charged attacks against critics.

Avoid clichés, filler introductions, repetition, rhetorical excess, artificial quotations, vague claims, preachiness, defensive language, and generic conclusions. Do not say that an objection is “destroyed” or “debunked” without demonstrating why.

End by presenting the positive Islamic understanding of the subject, not merely by rebutting the criticism.
```

```text
Separate emotional discomfort, cultural unfamiliarity, historical misunderstanding, and genuine moral argument. Identify the moral standard being used by the critic and examine whether it is coherent and consistently applied.
```

## Add-On Prompt for Hostile or Polemical Claims

```text
Do not mirror the critic’s hostility. Correct factual errors firmly but dispassionately. Avoid speculating about motives unless directly relevant and well supported.

Focus on the claim, its evidence, and its reasoning. Do not use ridicule, insults, triumphalist language, or broad attacks against groups of people.
```

## Add-On Prompt for the Prophet Muhammad ﷺ

```text
When evaluating an accusation against the Prophet Muhammad ﷺ, examine it in light of the full historical record of his character, conduct, relationships, sacrifices, teachings, and the testimony of those who knew him closely.

Do not isolate one allegation from the broader and better-attested pattern of his life. Explain whether the accusation is compatible with that cumulative record, while still addressing the specific evidence directly.
```

## Compact Version

Use this when a shorter instruction is needed:

```text
Use a reverent, analytical apologetic style. Write for an educated general audience in clear, precise English. Present objections fairly and in their strongest form, identify their assumptions, and answer them through reliable sources, historical context, textual analysis, and logical reasoning.

Be confident, composed, dignified, and intellectually honest. Distinguish established facts, mainstream positions, disputed interpretations, and speculation. Define Arabic and technical terms immediately.

Contextualize historical issues without reducing morality to relativism. Build cumulative arguments and avoid sensational scientific, numerical, or historical claims. Do not use mockery, hostility, triumphalist rhetoric, clichés, filler, repetition, vague claims, or unsupported certainty.

End with the positive Islamic understanding, not rebuttal alone.
```

---

## Final Quality Checklist

Before finalizing an article, verify that it:

- states the central claim clearly;
- presents the objection accurately;
- addresses the strongest version of the criticism;
- identifies hidden assumptions;
- distinguishes facts, interpretations, and moral judgments;
- uses reliable and relevant evidence;
- accurately represents the Qur’an, hadith, and scholarship;
- defines Arabic and technical terms;
- explains historical context without moral relativism;
- distinguishes consensus, majority positions, minority views, and speculation;
- avoids exaggerated miracle claims;
- avoids mockery and hostility;
- maintains reverence and intellectual seriousness;
- explains the positive Islamic framework;
- uses clear headings and focused paragraphs;
- avoids repetition and generic filler;
- ends with a substantive synthesis rather than a slogan.
