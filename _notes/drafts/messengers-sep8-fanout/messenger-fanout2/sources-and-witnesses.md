> Questions about later legend are questions about evidence before they are alternative explanations of the prophetic claim. This page therefore separates **what the sources are**, **how transmitted reports were evaluated**, and **what kinds of witnesses appear in the record**.

## Framing the evidence

Many people first encounter Prophet Muhammad ﷺ as a name, a historical founder, or the subject of debate. The Islamic sources preserve a much fuller portrait: a man who worshipped through the night and helped at home; who led a state yet died with his armor held as security for food; who wept over his child, played with his family, forgave personal injuries, restrained soldiers, protected treaty partners, listened to women and children, and repeatedly directed attention away from himself and toward God.[2](/messenger/sources#ref-2){: .footnote}[3](/messenger/sources#ref-3){: .footnote}[13](/messenger/sources#ref-13){: .footnote}[14](/messenger/sources#ref-14){: .footnote}[37](/messenger/sources#ref-37){: .footnote}

The Qur’an was recited publicly by **one man**, described in the Islamic sources as unable to read or write, in portions over roughly twenty-three years in seventh-century Arabia. His first audience had known him for decades before he claimed revelation, and his later life was observed in unusual detail by family members, companions, servants, opponents, petitioners, and political rivals. For that reason, **the Qur’an and the life of the man who delivered it have to be considered together**.[1](/messenger/sources#ref-1){: .footnote}[36](/messenger/sources#ref-36){: .footnote}[37](/messenger/sources#ref-37){: .footnote}[40](/messenger/sources#ref-40){: .footnote}[41](/messenger/sources#ref-41){: .footnote}

The evidence library asks two related questions: **What did Prophet Muhammad ﷺ teach and how did he live?** And **what best explains his claim to prophethood?** These are not identical questions. At the broadest level, the claim can be compared with alternatives such as **deliberate invention, sincere error, or genuine prophethood**; because many details of his life are preserved through transmitted reports, the possibility of later legendary development also has to be evaluated source by source. Good character alone does not prove revelation, just as a reported miracle does not authenticate itself. The detailed teaching, character, and biography pages establish the moral and historical picture; the argument pages then examine the prophetic claim more directly and bring the strands together.

His life is nevertheless directly relevant to that claim. The same person was known before revelation, endured years of marginalization and persecution afterward, was observed closely in private, and eventually acquired political and military authority. Any explanation of his mission has to account for the whole trajectory: conduct before the claim, under pressure, inside the home, and after gaining power.[8](/messenger/sources#ref-8){: .footnote}[28](/messenger/sources#ref-28){: .footnote}[37](/messenger/sources#ref-37){: .footnote}[38](/messenger/sources#ref-38){: .footnote}[41](/messenger/sources#ref-41){: .footnote}[42](/messenger/sources#ref-42){: .footnote}

The central question is therefore not whether every episode proves prophethood by itself. It is which explanation best accounts for the **overall pattern**—the teachings, character, sacrifices, conduct under changing circumstances, the Qur’an he delivered, the predictions and reported signs associated with his mission, and the responses of people who knew him. Readers primarily interested in the direct case can begin with **[the main argument](/messenger)** or **[the alternative-explanations page](/messenger/alternative-explanations)**, then follow the links to the moral, personal, and biographical evidence they want to inspect in depth.

## How the sources are used

- **The Qur’an** is the primary text Prophet Muhammad ﷺ publicly recited as revelation.[1](/messenger/sources#ref-1){: .footnote}[36](/messenger/sources#ref-36){: .footnote}
- **Hadith** are transmitted reports of his words, actions, and approvals. This evidence library gives priority to *Sahih al-Bukhari* and *Sahih Muslim*, the two most highly regarded Sunni hadith collections. In hadith terminology, *sahih* [sound/authentic] is the standard term for a report judged to meet the highest ordinary grade of authenticity. Other collections are identified explicitly when used.[2](/messenger/sources#ref-2){: .footnote}[3](/messenger/sources#ref-3){: .footnote}[22](/messenger/sources#ref-22){: .footnote}[23](/messenger/sources#ref-23){: .footnote}[33](/messenger/sources#ref-33){: .footnote}[43](/messenger/sources#ref-43){: .footnote}[44](/messenger/sources#ref-44){: .footnote}
- **Early biography, often called *sira* [prophetic biography],** preserves important narrative material that is not always found in the major hadith collections. Its reports vary in the strength of their transmission, so biographical material is identified as such rather than treated as equivalent to a rigorously authenticated hadith.[8](/messenger/sources#ref-8){: .footnote}[19](/messenger/sources#ref-19){: .footnote}[28](/messenger/sources#ref-28){: .footnote}[29](/messenger/sources#ref-29){: .footnote}[37](/messenger/sources#ref-37){: .footnote}[38](/messenger/sources#ref-38){: .footnote}[39](/messenger/sources#ref-39){: .footnote}
- Quotations from the Qur’an across these pages are **verbatim**, drawn from Dr. Mustafa Khattab's *The Clear Quran* or Saheeh International, whichever reads more clearly in English for the point being made; each is attributed to its translator. Hadith quotations are cited by collection and report number.
- The symbol **ﷺ** means “may God’s peace and blessings be upon him.”

## How hadith were preserved and evaluated

Much of the evidence across this library about Prophet Muhammad’s ﷺ character, conduct, predictions, and reported miracles comes through hadith. That record therefore has to be examined rather than taken for granted. The classical Muslim system for criticizing transmission is historically unusual in its scale, rigor, and detail, but it does not make every report automatically true.

Modern historians also debate how confidently later chains of transmission can recover events from Islam’s first century. Even with those cautions, the surviving material is not simply an undifferentiated collection of anonymous legends.[22](/messenger/sources#ref-22){: .footnote}[23](/messenger/sources#ref-23){: .footnote}[31](/messenger/sources#ref-31){: .footnote}[32](/messenger/sources#ref-32){: .footnote}[33](/messenger/sources#ref-33){: .footnote}[43](/messenger/sources#ref-43){: .footnote}[44](/messenger/sources#ref-44){: .footnote}[45](/messenger/sources#ref-45){: .footnote}[46](/messenger/sources#ref-46){: .footnote}

1. Hadith reports were normally accompanied by named *isnads* [chains of transmission]. The chain records who claimed to have received the report from whom, reaching back toward an earlier witness. This gave critics a stated history of transmission to investigate rather than leaving a saying as an anonymous maxim.

   > "The *isnad* [chain of transmission] is part of the religion. Were it not for the *isnad* [chain of transmission], whoever wished could say whatever he wished." — Abdullah ibn al-Mubarak, cited by Muslim in the introduction to *Sahih Muslim*[3](/messenger/sources#ref-3){: .footnote}[14](/messenger/sources#ref-14){: .footnote}[22](/messenger/sources#ref-22){: .footnote}[23](/messenger/sources#ref-23){: .footnote}[43](/messenger/sources#ref-43){: .footnote}
   {: .quote }

1. Evaluating those chains produced a vast biographical literature about the transmitters themselves. Hadith critics recorded dates, locations, teachers, students, memory, deterioration late in life, and reputations for honesty. Works such as al-Mizzi’s *Tahdhib al-Kamal*, Ibn Hajar’s later abridgments, and al-Dhahabi’s biographical writings preserve judgments on many thousands of transmitters.[24](/messenger/sources#ref-24){: .footnote}[25](/messenger/sources#ref-25){: .footnote}[26](/messenger/sources#ref-26){: .footnote}[33](/messenger/sources#ref-33){: .footnote}[43](/messenger/sources#ref-43){: .footnote}

1. The same critical standards could be applied even to teachers, relatives, and respected insiders. A well-known example is Ali ibn al-Madini, an important teacher of al-Bukhari, judging his own father too weak to serve as a reliable hadith authority. The purpose of *al-jarh wa-l-ta‘dil* [criticism and accreditation of transmitters] was precisely to keep affection or status from rescuing a defective chain.[22](/messenger/sources#ref-22){: .footnote}[24](/messenger/sources#ref-24){: .footnote}[26](/messenger/sources#ref-26){: .footnote}[33](/messenger/sources#ref-33){: .footnote}

1. Hadith critics did not treat every report as equally credible. They distinguished grades such as *sahih* [sound], *hasan* [good], and weak, and compiled works devoted to reports they considered fabricated. Ibn al-Jawzi’s *al-Mawdu‘at*, for example, catalogued sayings he judged forged rather than pretending that false attributions did not exist.[23](/messenger/sources#ref-23){: .footnote}[27](/messenger/sources#ref-27){: .footnote}[33](/messenger/sources#ref-33){: .footnote}[43](/messenger/sources#ref-43){: .footnote}

1. Written transmission existed alongside oral transmission well before the major ninth-century compilations. The *Sahifa* [written collection] associated with Hammam ibn Munabbih, a student of the Companion Abu Hurayra, represents an early written hadith collection transmitted into later manuscripts. Many of its reports have parallels in Bukhari and Muslim through their own chains. The surviving manuscripts are not Hammam’s autograph, so the evidence should not be overstated, but they weigh against the idea that hadith first began to be written only in al-Bukhari’s time.[31](/messenger/sources#ref-31){: .footnote}[32](/messenger/sources#ref-32){: .footnote}[43](/messenger/sources#ref-43){: .footnote}[44](/messenger/sources#ref-44){: .footnote}[45](/messenger/sources#ref-45){: .footnote}[46](/messenger/sources#ref-46){: .footnote}

1. Scholars sometimes traveled great distances to hear a report from a nearer source or investigate its transmission. The practice became known as *al-rihla fi talab al-hadith* [travel in search of hadith]. It reflects a scholarly culture in which verifying or shortening a chain could be considered worth months of travel.[22](/messenger/sources#ref-22){: .footnote}[23](/messenger/sources#ref-23){: .footnote}[31](/messenger/sources#ref-31){: .footnote}[43](/messenger/sources#ref-43){: .footnote}

1. The canonical collections were filtered selections, not archives of everything then in circulation. Al-Bukhari knew far more reports and transmission routes than he included in his *Sahih*. Rejected material was therefore not an accidental embarrassment outside the method. Distinguishing stronger reports from weaker ones was part of the method’s purpose.[23](/messenger/sources#ref-23){: .footnote}[43](/messenger/sources#ref-43){: .footnote}

1. Classical scholars distinguished ordinary reports from *mutawatir* [mass-transmitted] reports. The latter category was reserved for material transmitted through enough independent routes that deliberate coordinated fabrication was judged implausible. Most individual hadith are not *mutawatir*. That is precisely why “found in a hadith book” and “mass-transmitted” should not be treated as equivalent claims.[23](/messenger/sources#ref-23){: .footnote}[33](/messenger/sources#ref-33){: .footnote}[43](/messenger/sources#ref-43){: .footnote}

This method explains how Muslim scholarship can accept some reports, weaken others, and identify statements falsely attributed to Prophet Muhammad ﷺ. It also explains why the date of a source still matters here. The major canonical collections were compiled generations after the Prophet, so a prediction fulfilled before compilation remains open to the skeptical possibility that its wording was shaped after the event. A prediction whose canonical written source demonstrably predates the event, such as the report later connected with the 1256 Hijaz eruption, has a different evidentiary advantage.

For an academic introduction to the discipline and its modern criticisms, see Jonathan A. C. Brown, *Hadith: Muhammad’s Legacy in the Medieval and Modern World* (Oneworld, 2009; later editions).

## Witnesses from different vantage points

Conversion by itself proves little; false movements can also attract devoted followers. What makes this record more interesting is the range of people involved.

They include a wife who knew Prophet Muhammad ﷺ before his mission, a servant who lived with him for a decade, an enemy questioned by a foreign ruler, a hostile chief held as a prisoner and then released, a political negotiator who had seen imperial courts, and former enemies who later described how their judgment of him changed.[28](/messenger/sources#ref-28){: .footnote}[29](/messenger/sources#ref-29){: .footnote}[37](/messenger/sources#ref-37){: .footnote}[38](/messenger/sources#ref-38){: .footnote}[39](/messenger/sources#ref-39){: .footnote}[41](/messenger/sources#ref-41){: .footnote}[42](/messenger/sources#ref-42){: .footnote}

1. Khadija believed him before there was any worldly success to share in. Her first response to the revelation rested on the character she had already observed as his wife.

   > "By God, God will never disgrace you." — *Sahih al-Bukhari* 3[2](/messenger/sources#ref-2){: .footnote}[13](/messenger/sources#ref-13){: .footnote}[43](/messenger/sources#ref-43){: .footnote}
   {: .quote }

1. Waraqa ibn Nawfal, who was familiar with earlier scripture, understood Prophet Muhammad’s ﷺ first experience within the line of biblical prophecy. According to Bukhari, he identified the messenger of revelation with the one sent to Moses and immediately predicted that Prophet Muhammad ﷺ would face opposition.

   > "This is the bearer of divine secrets whom God sent to Moses. I wish I were young when your people drive you out." — *Sahih al-Bukhari* 3[2](/messenger/sources#ref-2){: .footnote}[13](/messenger/sources#ref-13){: .footnote}[43](/messenger/sources#ref-43){: .footnote}
   {: .quote }

1. Abu Sufyan gave useful testimony while he was still Prophet Muhammad’s ﷺ opponent. He rejected the prophetic claim, but when asked about specific matters of character he acknowledged that Prophet Muhammad ﷺ had not previously been accused of lying and was not known for breaking agreements.

   > Asked whether they had accused him of lying before, Abu Sufyan said, "No." — *Sahih al-Bukhari* 7[2](/messenger/sources#ref-2){: .footnote}[13](/messenger/sources#ref-13){: .footnote}[43](/messenger/sources#ref-43){: .footnote}
   {: .quote }

1. Heraclius’s reasoning is relevant without treating the emperor as an authority on prophethood. He argued from ordinary human credibility: a man who had not been known to lie about people was, in his judgment, an unlikely person suddenly to invent lies about God.

   > Heraclius said in substance: "I knew that if he did not lie about people, he would not lie about God." — *Sahih al-Bukhari* 7[2](/messenger/sources#ref-2){: .footnote}[13](/messenger/sources#ref-13){: .footnote}[43](/messenger/sources#ref-43){: .footnote}
   {: .quote }

1. Urwa ibn Masud observed Prophet Muhammad’s ﷺ community as a negotiator for the opposing side at Hudaybiyyah. Urwa had seen the courts of Caesar, Khosrau, and the Negus. He returned to Quraysh saying that he had never seen followers respect a ruler as Prophet Muhammad’s ﷺ companions respected him, and he urged Quraysh to accept the proposed agreement.

   > "I have visited kings... yet I have never seen any of them respected by his companions as Muhammad is respected by his companions." — *Sahih al-Bukhari* 2731-2732[2](/messenger/sources#ref-2){: .footnote}[13](/messenger/sources#ref-13){: .footnote}[43](/messenger/sources#ref-43){: .footnote}
   {: .quote }

1. Thumama ibn Uthal changed sides after Prophet Muhammad ﷺ released him without extracting payment. Thumama had been captured and held in the mosque for several days, and he offered wealth if ransom was wanted. Prophet Muhammad ﷺ ultimately ordered him released. Thumama left, washed, returned of his own choice, and declared his faith.

   > "There was no face on earth more hateful to me than yours; now your face has become the most beloved face to me." — *Sahih al-Bukhari* 4372[2](/messenger/sources#ref-2){: .footnote}[13](/messenger/sources#ref-13){: .footnote}[43](/messenger/sources#ref-43){: .footnote}
   {: .quote }

1. Thumama said that his feelings had changed toward the religion and the city as well as toward Prophet Muhammad ﷺ personally. This is not miraculous proof. It is testimony to the effect of what he observed while detained and then unexpectedly released.

   > "No religion was more hateful to me than yours; now it is the most beloved religion to me." — *Sahih al-Bukhari* 4372[2](/messenger/sources#ref-2){: .footnote}[13](/messenger/sources#ref-13){: .footnote}[43](/messenger/sources#ref-43){: .footnote}
   {: .quote }

1. Amr ibn al-As remembered moving from wanting Prophet Muhammad ﷺ dead to loving and revering him deeply. Near his own death, Amr described three phases of his life. During the first, no one was more hated by him than Prophet Muhammad ﷺ; after his conversion, no one was dearer.

   > "No one became dearer to me than the Messenger of God ﷺ, and no one was more majestic in my eyes." — *Sahih Muslim* 121[3](/messenger/sources#ref-3){: .footnote}[14](/messenger/sources#ref-14){: .footnote}[43](/messenger/sources#ref-43){: .footnote}
   {: .quote }

1. Safwan ibn Umayya likewise described his hostility turning to love through Prophet Muhammad’s ﷺ generosity. The report openly acknowledges that material gifts helped produce the reconciliation. That makes the account more human and gives concrete content to the way Prophet Muhammad ﷺ treated a defeated opponent.

   > "He kept giving to me until he became the dearest of people to me, though he had been the most hateful." — *Sahih Muslim* 2313[3](/messenger/sources#ref-3){: .footnote}[14](/messenger/sources#ref-14){: .footnote}[43](/messenger/sources#ref-43){: .footnote}
   {: .quote }

1. Abdullah ibn Salam, remembered as a learned Jewish figure in Medina, accepted Prophet Muhammad’s ﷺ claim. Bukhari reports that members of his Jewish community initially described him as among their best and most learned, then disparaged him after he announced his belief in Prophet Muhammad ﷺ.

   > They first said of Abdullah, "He is the most learned among us and the best among us." He then came out and testified to Muhammad's prophethood. — *Sahih al-Bukhari* 3329[2](/messenger/sources#ref-2){: .footnote}[13](/messenger/sources#ref-13){: .footnote}[43](/messenger/sources#ref-43){: .footnote}
   {: .quote }

1. Anas’s decade of service provides a very different kind of testimony from a dramatic conversion. He was not merely impressed by public preaching. He remembered how Prophet Muhammad ﷺ treated him when chores were forgotten and there was no audience to persuade.

   > "He never said 'uff' [an expression of irritation] to me." — *Sahih al-Bukhari* 6038[2](/messenger/sources#ref-2){: .footnote}[13](/messenger/sources#ref-13){: .footnote}[43](/messenger/sources#ref-43){: .footnote}
   {: .quote }

1. Aisha’s testimony comes from the still more intimate setting of marriage. She reports Prophet Muhammad’s ﷺ service at home, avoidance of personal revenge, gentleness, tears, humor, and refusal to strike women or servants.

   > "The Messenger of God ﷺ never struck a woman or a servant." — *Sahih Muslim* 2328a[3](/messenger/sources#ref-3){: .footnote}[14](/messenger/sources#ref-14){: .footnote}[43](/messenger/sources#ref-43){: .footnote}
   {: .quote }

1. No one witness is decisive, but together they observe Prophet Muhammad ﷺ from unusually different positions. An enemy speaks to his prior honesty. Family members describe private conduct. A servant describes everyday temperament. A negotiator comments on leadership and loyalty. Former enemies describe the effect of direct encounter. This convergence says more than the bare fact that many people followed him.

   > "And you are truly ˹a man˺ of outstanding character." (**Sūrat al-Qalam 68:4**), Dr. Mustafa Khattab, *The Clear Quran*[1](/messenger/sources#ref-1){: .footnote}[11](/messenger/sources#ref-11){: .footnote}[12](/messenger/sources#ref-12){: .footnote}[36](/messenger/sources#ref-36){: .footnote}
   >
   > <div dir="rtl" lang="ar" class="quran-arabic">﴿وَإِنَّكَ لَعَلَىٰ خُلُقٍ عَظِيمٍ ۝٤﴾ <span class="ayah-ref">سُورَةُ القَلَمِ</span></div>
   {: .quote }

1. Closer access did not produce a surviving record of private disillusionment, yet insiders also preserved uncomfortable details. Wives, servants, relatives, and lifelong companions remained deeply committed while transmitting reports of poverty, fatigue, household jealousy, grief, a mistaken agricultural judgment, Qur’anic corrections, and disagreements with him. They also preserved ordinary details of his food, sleep, speech, and domestic work. A later community creating an entirely frictionless saint could easily have omitted much of this material, which makes the witness record more substantial than simple praise literature.[37](/messenger/sources#ref-37){: .footnote}[38](/messenger/sources#ref-38){: .footnote}[39](/messenger/sources#ref-39){: .footnote}[41](/messenger/sources#ref-41){: .footnote}[42](/messenger/sources#ref-42){: .footnote}

1. Several people who had fiercely opposed Prophet Muhammad ﷺ later joined the Muslim community and became important participants in it. Umar is remembered as having set out against him before becoming one of his closest companions. Khalid ibn al-Walid commanded Meccan cavalry against the Muslims at Uhud before becoming a Muslim commander. Abu Sufyan led years of opposition before joining the community. Conversion does not prove prophethood, but repeated reversals among people who directly experienced both sides of the conflict belong in the cumulative human evidence.[9](/messenger/sources#ref-9){: .footnote}[20](/messenger/sources#ref-20){: .footnote}[25](/messenger/sources#ref-25){: .footnote}[28](/messenger/sources#ref-28){: .footnote}[29](/messenger/sources#ref-29){: .footnote}[37](/messenger/sources#ref-37){: .footnote}[41](/messenger/sources#ref-41){: .footnote}[42](/messenger/sources#ref-42){: .footnote}
