# Muhammad: His Life, Character, and Teachings

*An evidence-based portrait of the Prophet of Islam*

## Introduction

Muhammad (peace and blessings be upon him) is not presented in Islam merely as a teacher of doctrines. The Qur’an presents him as **a lived example**: a man whose worship, family life, leadership, restraint, courage, mercy, and judgment were all open to examination. His life was preserved in unusual detail. His followers recorded how he prayed, governed, joked, grieved, ate, forgave, listened, fought, made peace, treated children, and behaved when no public performance was required.

The portrait below is cumulative. No single incident explains why his companions loved him so deeply or why later Muslims have continued to love him. The force lies in the consistency: the same man who preached mercy when weak practiced restraint when powerful; the same man who demanded justice accepted no exemption for his own family; the same man who led a state continued serving in his home and dying without accumulated wealth.

### A note on sources

The strongest evidence here comes first from the **Qur’an**, then from rigorously authenticated hadith reports, especially *Sahih al-Bukhari* and *Sahih Muslim*. Reports from the sira [Prophetic biography] are used more cautiously. Quotations are rendered in plain English, sometimes in a concise translation rather than copied from one published version. References are provided so that each report can be checked.

## I. The Formation of His Character Before Prophethood

1. **He began life without the protections that normally produce worldly confidence.** His father died before he was born, his mother died when he was young, and he was then raised by his grandfather and uncle. The Qur’an later reminded him that God had sheltered him as an orphan, making care for vulnerable children a personal memory before it became a public teaching.

   > “Did He not find you an orphan and give you shelter?”  
   > — Qur’an 93:6

2. **He did not build his claim on inherited kingship.** He came from a respected clan, but neither his father nor his grandfather had been a king. Even an enemy, Abu Sufyan, admitted this when questioned by the Byzantine emperor Heraclius. That mattered because it weakened the theory that Muhammad was using religion to recover a lost throne.

   > Heraclius asked, “Was any of his forefathers a king?” Abu Sufyan answered, “No.”  
   > — *Sahih al-Bukhari* 7

3. **His truthfulness was established before he announced prophethood.** When Heraclius asked Abu Sufyan—then still Muhammad’s enemy—whether Muhammad had ever been accused of lying before his claim, Abu Sufyan had to answer no. A man known not to lie about ordinary people was now claiming revelation from God.

   > “Have you ever accused him of lying before he said what he now says?” He answered, “No.”  
   > — *Sahih al-Bukhari* 7

4. **His closest observer described a life already organized around service.** After the first revelation frightened him, his wife Khadijah did not reassure him with vague affection. She cited his established conduct: maintaining family ties, helping the poor, honoring guests, speaking truthfully, and assisting people struck by calamity.

   > “God will never disgrace you. You maintain family ties, speak the truth, support the weak, honor guests, and help those afflicted by hardship.”  
   > — *Sahih al-Bukhari* 3

5. **He worked rather than living as an idle spiritual aristocrat.** He tended sheep, as other prophets had done, and later worked in trade. His teaching would continue to honor lawful work and earning by one’s own effort rather than dependence on status.

   > “God did not send any prophet except that he tended sheep.”  
   > — *Sahih al-Bukhari* 2262

6. **His marriage to Khadijah was marked by loyalty, not opportunism.** She was his only wife during their long marriage. She supported him at the beginning of revelation, shared the years of persecution, and remained so deeply loved that he continued honoring her friends long after her death.

   > “The Messenger of God did not marry another woman until Khadijah died.”  
   > — *Sahih Muslim* 2436; see also *Sahih al-Bukhari* 3818

7. **He sought solitude before he sought authority.** Before revelation, he withdrew to the cave of Hira for worship and reflection. Nothing in this beginning resembles the preparation of a tribal conqueror. The public mission emerged from an experience he found overwhelming, not from a staged announcement of ambition.

   > “He used to withdraw to the cave of Hira and worship there for a number of nights.”  
   > — *Sahih al-Bukhari* 3

8. **The first command he received centered knowledge, not domination.** The opening revelation began with reading, creation, and the generosity of God who teaches. The first movement of his mission was intellectual and spiritual: human beings must know their Creator and recognize that knowledge itself is a gift.

   > “Read in the name of your Lord who created… Read, and your Lord is most generous, who taught by the pen.”  
   > — Qur’an 96:1–4

9. **His first reaction was fear, not self-congratulation.** He returned trembling and asked to be covered. This human response is striking. He did not emerge claiming instant mastery over the unseen; he feared what had happened until Khadijah steadied him and took him to someone knowledgeable about earlier revelation.

   > “He returned with his heart trembling and said, ‘Cover me, cover me.’”  
   > — *Sahih al-Bukhari* 3

10. **The Qur’an openly denied that he had engineered his own prophetic career.** He had lived among his people for decades before reciting the Qur’an. The text itself appealed to this shared history as evidence: his public life before revelation contained no apprenticeship in composing scripture or founding a religious movement.

   > “I lived among you for a lifetime before this. Will you not reason?”  
   > — Qur’an 10:16

## II. The Meccan Mission: Truth Without Power

11. **His central message was the worship of God alone.** He did not ask people to worship him, his family, his tribe, or an image. He called them away from inherited idols and toward the one Creator, linking worship to moral accountability.

   > “Say: He is God, One; God, the One on whom all depend.”  
   > — Qur’an 112:1–2

12. **He presented himself as a human messenger, not a divine being.** The Qur’an repeatedly instructed him to deny possession of God’s powers. This protected the movement from turning him into an object of worship and placed his greatness in faithful servanthood rather than divinity.

   > “Say: I am only a human being like you, to whom it has been revealed that your God is one God.”  
   > — Qur’an 18:110

13. **He refused religious compromise when compromise would have ended persecution.** Meccan leaders wanted mutual accommodation between their gods and his message. The Qur’an answered with a clear boundary: peaceful coexistence did not require pretending contradictory forms of worship were the same.

   > “I do not worship what you worship… For you is your religion, and for me is mine.”  
   > — Qur’an 109:2, 6

14. **He continued preaching after ridicule, social pressure, and violence.** The early Muslims had little protection. Some were tortured; others were socially isolated. Muhammad did not answer by authorizing private revenge. The Meccan revelations repeatedly commanded patience and principled separation from abuse.

   > “Be patient over what they say, and leave them with a dignified withdrawal.”  
   > — Qur’an 73:10

15. **He cared about the weak before they could offer him political advantage.** Many early followers were poor, enslaved, young, or socially unprotected. His opponents complained that such people gathered around him. The Qur’an forbade him from pushing sincere but low-status believers aside to attract elites.

   > “Do not drive away those who call upon their Lord morning and evening, seeking His face.”  
   > — Qur’an 6:52

16. **He sent persecuted followers to a Christian king for protection.** Early biographical sources report that this migration to Abyssinia took place before Muslims had a state. Muhammad trusted that a just Christian ruler would not permit oppression, showing that justice could be recognized across religious boundaries.

   > “There is a king there under whom no one is wronged.”  
   > — Report of the first migration in the sira of Ibn Ishaq, preserved by Ibn Hisham

17. **He endured a clan boycott without abandoning his message.** His clan and followers were subjected to social and economic isolation. The hardship did not cause him to dilute monotheism or turn the movement into a campaign for personal revenge.

   > “Do people think they will be left to say, ‘We believe,’ without being tested?”  
   > — Qur’an 29:2

18. **The deaths of Khadijah and his uncle Abu Talib left him personally exposed.** He lost his closest emotional support and his main tribal protector in the same period. Yet the mission continued. His persistence after these losses is difficult to explain as a project sustained merely by comfort, family advantage, or easy success.

   > “So surely with hardship comes ease; surely with hardship comes ease.”  
   > — Qur’an 94:5–6

19. **At Ta’if, he chose hope for his attackers’ descendants over their destruction.** After being rejected and wounded, he was offered the destruction of the town between its mountains. He declined. He hoped future generations would worship God. This was mercy when retaliation appeared miraculously available, not mercy forced by weakness alone.

   > “No. I hope that God will bring from their descendants people who worship God alone.”  
   > — *Sahih al-Bukhari* 3231

20. **His night journey placed prayer at the center of spiritual life.** According to the Qur’an and authenticated hadith, he was taken by night from the Sacred Mosque to the farthest mosque and shown signs of God. The lasting communal gift associated with this event was not wealth or territory, but the daily prayer.

   > “Glory be to Him who took His servant by night from the Sacred Mosque to the farthest mosque.”  
   > — Qur’an 17:1

## III. Migration, Community, Leadership, and the Final Years

21. **He planned the migration carefully while trusting God.** The migration to Medina was not passive fatalism. Routes, supplies, intelligence, concealment, and a skilled guide were arranged. Yet in the cave, when danger came close, he reassured Abu Bakr with confidence in God.

   > “Do not grieve; God is with us.”  
   > — Qur’an 9:40

22. **He accepted skilled help from a non-Muslim guide.** The man hired to guide the migration route was trusted for his expertise despite not sharing Muhammad’s faith. This was practical recognition that professional trustworthiness and competence can cross religious boundaries.

   > “The Prophet and Abu Bakr hired a skilled guide… who followed the religion of Quraysh.”  
   > — *Sahih al-Bukhari* 2264

23. **His first major project in Medina was a shared place of worship and community.** He helped build the mosque rather than supervising from a distance. The mosque became a place of prayer, education, consultation, shelter, and public life.

   > “The Prophet began carrying bricks with them, saying, ‘There is no true life except the life of the Hereafter.’”  
   > — *Sahih al-Bukhari* 428

24. **He joined migrants and residents in bonds of mutual responsibility.** The local Muslims shared homes, wealth, and livelihoods with people who had fled Mecca. The Qur’an praised the residents of Medina for preferring others even while they themselves were in need.

   > “They give them preference over themselves, even though they are in need.”  
   > — Qur’an 59:9

25. **He established a plural civic order in Medina.** The early Medinan agreement recognized distinct religious communities while binding them to mutual defense and public obligations. It did not erase religious differences, but created a political framework in which different groups could belong to one protected society.

   > “The Jews have their religion and the Muslims have theirs… and between them is mutual help against whoever attacks the people of this document.”  
   > — Constitution of Medina, preserved in the sira of Ibn Ishaq

26. **Permission to fight came after expulsion and persecution.** The Qur’an’s earliest authorization for armed defense explicitly tied it to people being wronged and driven from their homes for saying that God was their Lord. War entered the mission as defense against persecution, not as its opening method.

   > “Permission is given to those who are fought because they have been wronged… those driven from their homes only for saying, ‘Our Lord is God.’”  
   > — Qur’an 22:39–40

27. **After the disaster at Uhud, he was commanded to forgive and consult the same followers whose mistakes had contributed to it.** The Qur’an did not tell him to become authoritarian after military failure. It told him to pardon them, pray for them, and keep consulting them.

   > “Pardon them, ask forgiveness for them, and consult them in affairs.”  
   > — Qur’an 3:159

28. **At the trench, he worked beside his companions.** During the confederate siege of Medina, he carried earth and endured hunger with the rest. Leadership did not exempt him from physical labor or danger.

   > “We saw the Prophet carrying earth until the dust covered his abdomen.”  
   > — *Sahih al-Bukhari* 4104

29. **At Hudaybiyyah, he accepted painful terms for the sake of peace.** He agreed to wording that omitted his prophetic title and accepted conditions many companions found humiliating. The Qur’an later called the treaty a clear victory because it opened space for peaceful contact and wider acceptance of Islam.

   > “We have granted you a clear victory.”  
   > — Qur’an 48:1; see *Sahih al-Bukhari* 2731–2732

30. **His response to final victory was worship and accountability, not self-glorification.** The city that had expelled him came under his authority with limited fighting. The Qur’an instructed him to answer victory by praising God and seeking forgiveness. His final public teaching stressed the sanctity of life, property, and honor rather than celebrating personal conquest.

   > “When God’s help and victory come… glorify your Lord with praise and seek His forgiveness.”  
   > — Qur’an 110:1–3

   > “Your blood, your property, and your honor are sacred to you.”  
   > — *Sahih al-Bukhari* 1739, 1741; *Sahih Muslim* 1679

## IV. His Relationship With God

31. **He understood greatness as servanthood.** Even at the highest point of his spiritual journey, the Qur’an called him God’s servant. He did not present submission to God as humiliation; he presented it as the highest human freedom—from idols, ego, fear of people, and the need to dominate others.

   > “Glory be to Him who took His servant by night…”  
   > — Qur’an 17:1

32. **His worship was driven by gratitude rather than insecurity.** He stood in night prayer until his feet swelled. When asked why he exerted himself despite God’s forgiveness, he did not answer that he feared losing status. He answered that he wanted to be a grateful servant.

   > “Should I not be a grateful servant?”  
   > — *Sahih al-Bukhari* 1130

33. **He taught that actions are judged by their inner purpose.** This principle placed moral value deeper than appearance. The same outward act could become worship, vanity, exploitation, or courage depending on intention.

   > “Actions are only by intentions, and each person will have only what he intended.”  
   > — *Sahih al-Bukhari* 1; *Sahih Muslim* 1907

34. **He prayed with concentration, tenderness, and awe.** Reports describe prolonged standing, bowing, and prostration. Yet he did not impose his private intensity on everyone else. His own worship was deep; his public leadership remained considerate.

   > “The closest a servant is to his Lord is while prostrating, so increase your supplication.”  
   > — *Sahih Muslim* 482

35. **He made prayer a repeated return to God, not a weekly performance.** Five daily prayers interrupt ordinary life with remembrance, repentance, gratitude, and moral reorientation. He compared them to washing in a river that removes accumulated dirt.

   > “If a river were at the door of one of you and he bathed in it five times a day, would any dirt remain?”  
   > — *Sahih al-Bukhari* 528; *Sahih Muslim* 667

36. **He connected fasting to character, not hunger alone.** A person who abstains from food while continuing to lie and harm others has missed the point. Fasting trains restraint over speech, anger, appetite, and retaliation.

   > “Whoever does not leave false speech and acting upon it, God has no need of his leaving food and drink.”  
   > — *Sahih al-Bukhari* 1903, 6057

37. **He taught constant repentance without despair.** Human beings were not divided into a pure religious class and a permanently ruined class. The door back to God remained open while life remained.

   > “God is more joyful at the repentance of His servant than one who finds his lost camel in a barren land.”  
   > — *Sahih Muslim* 2747

38. **He himself asked God for forgiveness repeatedly.** Prophethood did not produce spiritual complacency. He taught repentance by practicing it, showing that turning to God is not only for notorious sinners but part of an honest relationship with God.

   > “By God, I seek God’s forgiveness and repent to Him more than seventy times a day.”  
   > — *Sahih al-Bukhari* 6307

39. **He combined reliance on God with responsible action.** His migrations, treaties, military preparations, appointments, and household decisions all involved planning. Reliance meant doing what was morally and practically required, then accepting that outcomes belong to God.

   > “When you have decided, then place your trust in God.”  
   > — Qur’an 3:159

40. **He rejected religious excess.** When some men proposed permanent fasting, nightly prayer without sleep, and avoidance of marriage, he corrected them. Spirituality was not meant to destroy the body, family, or ordinary human life.

   > “I fast and I break my fast; I pray and I sleep; and I marry. Whoever turns away from my way is not of me.”  
   > — *Sahih al-Bukhari* 5063; *Sahih Muslim* 1401

## V. His Personal Character

41. **The Qur’an described his character as immense.** This judgment appears in an early Meccan chapter, before political authority and military victory. His moral stature was not a retrospective product of success.

   > “Indeed, you are upon magnificent character.”  
   > — Qur’an 68:4

42. **His wife summarized his character as the Qur’an lived out.** When Aisha was asked about him, she did not offer a collection of slogans. She said his character was the Qur’an: its mercy, discipline, patience, justice, worship, and restraint made visible in a human life.

   > “His character was the Qur’an.”  
   > — *Sahih Muslim* 746

43. **He did not answer personal injury with personal revenge.** Aisha, who observed him privately and publicly, said he never took revenge for himself. He could enforce public justice, but wounded pride did not govern him.

   > “The Messenger of God never took revenge for himself.”  
   > — *Sahih al-Bukhari* 3560; *Sahih Muslim* 2328

44. **He preferred the easier lawful path.** His instinct was not to burden people unnecessarily. When two permissible options were available, he chose the easier, while remaining firm against wrongdoing.

   > “Whenever he was given a choice between two matters, he chose the easier one so long as it was not sinful.”  
   > — *Sahih al-Bukhari* 3560

45. **Gentleness was a governing principle, not a decorative virtue.** He taught that gentleness improves whatever it enters and that harshness disfigures whatever it enters. This applied to teaching, family life, correction, leadership, and religious invitation.

   > “Gentleness is not placed in anything except that it beautifies it.”  
   > — *Sahih Muslim* 2594

46. **He remained patient with crude treatment.** A Bedouin pulled his cloak so violently that it marked his shoulder, then demanded money. Muhammad turned, smiled, and ordered that he be given something. The incident shows composure under public disrespect from someone with no refined manners.

   > “The Prophet turned toward him, smiled, and ordered that he be given a gift.”  
   > — *Sahih al-Bukhari* 3149, 5809

47. **He was accessible enough that strangers could not identify him by a throne or special seat.** A visitor once entered the gathering and had to ask which man was Muhammad. He sat among his companions rather than arranging the room to magnify his rank.

   > “Which of you is Muhammad?”  
   > — *Sahih al-Bukhari* 63

48. **He disliked ceremonial self-exaltation.** He did not cultivate the manners of kings who required people to stand in reverence. His authority rested on trust, revelation, and service rather than court ritual.

   > “They would not stand when they saw him because they knew that he disliked that.”  
   > — *Jami al-Tirmidhi* 2754 (graded *hasan* [sound])

49. **His generosity was rapid and expansive.** Ibn Abbas described him as the most generous of people, especially in Ramadan, when his giving was compared to a wind bringing benefit everywhere.

   > “The Messenger of God was more generous in doing good than a freely blowing wind.”  
   > — *Sahih al-Bukhari* 3554; *Sahih Muslim* 2308

50. **He gave in ways that changed hostility into affection.** Safwan ibn Umayyah said Muhammad kept giving him after the conquest even though Muhammad had been the person he hated most; the generosity continued until Muhammad became the person he loved most.

   > “He kept giving me until he became the most beloved of people to me.”  
   > — *Sahih Muslim* 2313

## VI. Courage, Humility, Emotion, and Loyalty

51. **His courage was clearest when others were afraid.** At Hunayn, when part of the army scattered under surprise attack, he remained in place and identified himself openly rather than fleeing anonymously.

   > “I am the Prophet—this is no lie. I am the son of Abd al-Muttalib.”  
   > — *Sahih al-Bukhari* 2864, 4315; *Sahih Muslim* 1776

52. **He shared physical danger with his followers.** At the trench he dug beside them; at Uhud he was wounded; on journeys he accepted hunger and fatigue. He did not ask others to bear risks from which he was institutionally protected.

   > “On that day, the Prophet’s face was wounded and one of his teeth was broken.”  
   > — *Sahih al-Bukhari* 2911; *Sahih Muslim* 1790

53. **He taught that real strength is mastery of the self.** In a culture that admired physical dominance, he redefined strength as control during anger. This moved heroism from defeating another person to defeating one’s own impulse to harm.

   > “The strong person is not the one who overpowers others, but the one who controls himself when angry.”  
   > — *Sahih al-Bukhari* 6114; *Sahih Muslim* 2609

54. **He was deeply modest.** Companions described him as more shy than a secluded young woman. When he disliked something, they often recognized it from his face rather than from humiliating speech.

   > “The Prophet was more modest than a virgin in her private room.”  
   > — *Sahih al-Bukhari* 3562, 6102; *Sahih Muslim* 2320

55. **He smiled frequently without becoming frivolous.** His companions remembered an open, welcoming expression. Jarir ibn Abdullah said that after accepting Islam, he was never denied access and was met with a smile.

   > “He never saw me after I accepted Islam except that he smiled at me.”  
   > — *Sahih al-Bukhari* 3035; *Sahih Muslim* 2475

56. **His humor remained truthful.** He joked with companions and children, but refused to make falsehood entertaining. This preserved warmth without normalizing deceit.

   > “I do not say anything except the truth.”  
   > — *Jami al-Tirmidhi* 1990 (graded *hasan* [sound])

57. **He allowed himself to grieve openly.** When his infant son Ibrahim died, he wept. He did not present tears as weakness or grief as a failure of faith. He distinguished compassion from words of protest against God.

   > “The eyes shed tears and the heart grieves, but we say only what pleases our Lord.”  
   > — *Sahih al-Bukhari* 1303

58. **He remembered goodness for years.** Long after Khadijah’s death, he spoke of her, sent gifts to her friends, and showed visible emotion when someone connected to her visited. Loyalty did not expire when a relationship could no longer benefit him.

   > “When he slaughtered a sheep, he would say, ‘Send some to Khadijah’s friends.’”  
   > — *Sahih Muslim* 2435; see also *Sahih al-Bukhari* 3818

59. **He did not criticize ordinary food.** If he liked what was served, he ate; if not, he left it. The home was not made anxious by constant fault-finding over meals.

   > “The Prophet never criticized food. If he desired it, he ate; if he disliked it, he left it.”  
   > — *Sahih al-Bukhari* 5409; *Sahih Muslim* 2064

60. **His private life confirms his public gentleness.** Public figures can perform patience before crowds. The more difficult evidence is the testimony of spouses, servants, children, and household members. Their reports consistently describe service, restraint, affection, and the absence of habitual contempt.

   > “I served him for ten years, and he never once said to me, ‘Uff’ [an expression of impatience].”  
   > — *Sahih al-Bukhari* 6038; *Sahih Muslim* 2309

## VII. His Treatment of Family and Women

61. **He made treatment of one’s family a test of moral quality.** Public religiosity could not compensate for cruelty at home. He explicitly located human excellence in the place where hypocrisy is hardest to maintain: daily family life.

   > “The best of you are those who are best to their families, and I am the best of you to my family.”  
   > — *Jami al-Tirmidhi* 3895; *Sunan Ibn Majah* 1977

62. **He served in his own home.** Aisha was asked what he did privately. She answered that he worked for his family, then left for prayer when the call was made. Leadership of a community did not turn domestic service into work beneath him.

   > “He used to work for his family.”  
   > — *Sahih al-Bukhari* 5363

63. **He never struck a wife or servant.** Aisha’s statement is categorical about his personal practice. His lived example within the household was restraint and nonviolence.

   > “The Messenger of God never struck anything with his hand—neither a woman nor a servant.”  
   > — *Sahih Muslim* 2328

64. **He expressed affection openly.** He drank from the same place on a cup where Aisha had drunk, rested with her, spoke with her at length, and did not treat tenderness as incompatible with masculinity or religious seriousness.

   > “I would drink while menstruating, then hand it to the Prophet, and he would place his mouth where mine had been.”  
   > — *Sahih Muslim* 300

65. **He made room for play in marriage.** Aisha reported racing with him on journeys. Years later they raced again, and he joked about the changed result. This was companionship, not merely a legal household arrangement.

   > “He said, ‘This makes up for that earlier race.’”  
   > — *Sunan Abi Dawud* 2578

66. **He listened to his wife’s political judgment.** At Hudaybiyyah, when the companions were too distressed to act immediately on his instruction, Umm Salama advised him to begin the ritual himself without further debate. He followed her counsel, and the crisis ended.

   > “Go out, speak to no one, sacrifice your animal, and shave your head.”  
   > — Report of Umm Salama’s counsel within *Sahih al-Bukhari* 2731–2732

67. **He honored his daughter Fatimah visibly.** When she entered, he welcomed her, stood for her, kissed her, and seated her where he had been sitting. A daughter was not treated as a lesser child whose arrival carried no public honor.

   > “When she entered upon him, he stood, took her hand, kissed her, and seated her in his place.”  
   > — *Sunan Abi Dawud* 5217; *Jami al-Tirmidhi* 3872

68. **He elevated the mother’s claim to companionship.** When a man asked who most deserved his good company, Muhammad named the mother three times before naming the father. Care work, pregnancy, birth, and early nurture were given moral weight.

   > “Your mother.” He asked, “Then who?” He said, “Your mother.” He asked, “Then who?” He said, “Your mother.”  
   > — *Sahih al-Bukhari* 5971; *Sahih Muslim* 2548

69. **He required a woman’s consent to marriage.** He invalidated the assumption that guardians could dispose of women without regard to their will. A previously married woman had an explicit voice, and a virgin’s permission had to be sought.

   > “A previously married woman has more right concerning herself than her guardian, and a virgin’s permission must be sought.”  
   > — *Sahih Muslim* 1421; see also *Sahih al-Bukhari* 5136

70. **He gave women direct access to religious learning.** When women said men were taking most of his teaching time, he set aside a day for them. They questioned him directly and were not told that serious knowledge belonged only to men.

   > “The women said, ‘The men have taken all your time, so appoint a day for us.’ He appointed a day and taught them.”  
   > — *Sahih al-Bukhari* 101

## VIII. Children, Servants, the Poor, and the Socially Vulnerable

71. **He kissed and embraced children.** When a man boasted that he had ten children and had never kissed any of them, Muhammad treated this not as toughness but as a deficiency of mercy.

   > “Whoever does not show mercy will not be shown mercy.”  
   > — *Sahih al-Bukhari* 5997; *Sahih Muslim* 2318

72. **He adjusted communal worship for a crying child.** He sometimes intended to lengthen the prayer, then shortened it when he heard a child crying because he knew the mother would be distressed. The congregation’s ritual experience did not outweigh a parent’s anguish.

   > “I hear a child crying, so I shorten the prayer because I know how deeply his crying troubles his mother.”  
   > — *Sahih al-Bukhari* 707, 709

73. **He carried his granddaughter while leading prayer.** He prayed while holding Umamah, putting her down when he bowed and lifting her when he stood. A child’s presence was not treated as contamination of sacred space.

   > “The Messenger of God prayed while carrying Umamah… when he stood, he carried her; when he prostrated, he put her down.”  
   > — *Sahih al-Bukhari* 516, 5996; *Sahih Muslim* 543

74. **He entered a child’s world.** He noticed a small boy’s grief over a pet bird and addressed him with a playful rhyme. The report survived because the companions saw significance in a prophet making time for a child’s small sorrow.

   > “O Abu Umayr, what happened to the little bird?”  
   > — *Sahih al-Bukhari* 6129; *Sahih Muslim* 2150

75. **He placed the caregiver of an orphan beside himself in Paradise.** Orphan care was not framed as optional sentiment. It was one of the works nearest to him in the next life.

   > “I and the one who cares for an orphan will be in Paradise like this,” and he joined two fingers.  
   > — *Sahih al-Bukhari* 6005, 5304

76. **He treated sustained care for widows and the poor as a form of heroic worship.** Quiet economic support was compared to striving in God’s cause and to continuous fasting and night prayer.

   > “The one who works for a widow or a poor person is like one who strives in God’s cause.”  
   > — *Sahih al-Bukhari* 6006; *Sahih Muslim* 2982

77. **His servant Anas experienced ten years without contempt.** Anas was a child when placed in his service. His later testimony is unusually revealing: no disgusted expression of impatience, no routine blame, and no demand for explanations designed to shame him.

   > “He never said, ‘Why did you do that?’ or, ‘Why did you not do that?’”  
   > — *Sahih al-Bukhari* 6038

78. **He confronted class prejudice among his own companions.** When Abu Dharr insulted another man by referring to his mother’s ethnicity, Muhammad told him that ignorance remained in him. Spiritual rank did not excuse racial contempt.

   > “You are a man in whom there is still ignorance.”  
   > — *Sahih al-Bukhari* 30; *Sahih Muslim* 1661

79. **He required humane treatment of enslaved and dependent workers.** He ordered that they be fed from what their households ate, clothed from what they wore, not burdened beyond capacity, and helped when given difficult work.

   > “Feed them from what you eat, clothe them from what you wear, and do not burden them with what overwhelms them.”  
   > — *Sahih al-Bukhari* 30; *Sahih Muslim* 1661

80. **He made himself available to people without status.** Anas reported that a woman whose mental condition might have led others to dismiss her could ask him to walk aside with her so he could address her need. He did not protect his schedule by treating her as an inconvenience.

   > “Choose whichever road you wish, and I will go with you until I meet your need.”  
   > — *Sahih Muslim* 2326; see also *Sahih al-Bukhari* 6072

---

## IX. Justice, Equality, and Public Leadership

81. **He rejected legal privilege for powerful families.** When prominent people sought intercession for a well-connected woman convicted of theft, he warned that earlier societies were destroyed by punishing the weak while exempting the elite.

   > “By God, if Fatimah the daughter of Muhammad stole, I would apply the law to her.”  
   > — *Sahih al-Bukhari* 3475, 6788; *Sahih Muslim* 1688

82. **He taught judges to remain aware of their limits.** He acknowledged that a persuasive speaker might win a judgment while being wrong. A legal verdict based on available evidence could not turn stolen rights into moral property.

   > “I am only human. You bring disputes to me, and perhaps one of you is more eloquent in presenting his case.”  
   > — *Sahih al-Bukhari* 2680; *Sahih Muslim* 1713

83. **He condemned gifts tied to public office.** When a tax collector returned saying part of the wealth was public revenue and part was a personal gift, Muhammad exposed the conflict of interest: would the gift have arrived if the official had remained in his parents’ home?

   > “Why did he not sit in the house of his father and mother and see whether gifts would come to him?”  
   > — *Sahih al-Bukhari* 7174; *Sahih Muslim* 1832

84. **He was commanded to consult rather than govern by ego.** Consultation in the Qur’an appears alongside forgiveness and trust in God. He sought advice in military, civic, and family matters, even when he possessed prophetic authority.

   > “Consult them in affairs.”  
   > — Qur’an 3:159

85. **He distinguished revelation from technical expertise.** After offering an opinion about date-palm pollination, he accepted that farmers knew their craft better when the crop suffered. This preserved an important boundary between religious guidance and ordinary empirical knowledge.

   > “You are more knowledgeable about the affairs of your worldly life.”  
   > — *Sahih Muslim* 2363

86. **The Qur’an publicly corrected him.** It reproved him for turning from a blind seeker while addressing elites, for forbidding himself something lawful to please family members, and in other decisions. A fabricated scripture designed to magnify its author would not naturally preserve such corrections.

   > “He frowned and turned away because the blind man came to him.”  
   > — Qur’an 80:1–2

87. **He taught justice even against one’s own interest.** Loyalty to family, wealth, group, or emotion could not override truthful testimony. The Qur’an ordered believers to stand firmly for justice even when the result harmed themselves or relatives.

   > “Stand firmly for justice, as witnesses for God, even against yourselves, parents, or close relatives.”  
   > — Qur’an 4:135

88. **He forbade hatred from distorting justice.** Moral standards did not end at the boundary of friendship. Even hostility toward another people could not justify unfairness.

   > “Do not let the hatred of a people cause you to depart from justice. Be just; that is nearer to reverence.”  
   > — Qur’an 5:8

89. **He protected the life of non-Muslims under covenant.** He issued one of his severest warnings against murdering a protected non-Muslim, placing such a crime between the killer and even the scent of Paradise.

   > “Whoever kills a person under covenant will not smell the fragrance of Paradise.”  
   > — *Sahih al-Bukhari* 3166

90. **He lived without converting public authority into personal fortune.** He died while his armor was mortgaged to a Jewish merchant for food. This is difficult to reconcile with the claim that his mission was a scheme for wealth.

   > “The Prophet died while his armor was mortgaged to a Jew for thirty measures of barley.”  
   > — *Sahih al-Bukhari* 2916, 4467

---
## X. His Moral and Social Teachings

91. **He connected faith to the safety of the community.** Religious identity was not defined only by ritual. A Muslim was someone from whose tongue and hand other Muslims were safe.

   > “The Muslim is the one from whose tongue and hand the Muslims are safe.”  
   > — *Sahih al-Bukhari* 10; *Sahih Muslim* 40

92. **He made love of good for others part of faith.** He opposed a spirituality built on private salvation while remaining indifferent to everyone else.

   > “None of you truly believes until he loves for his brother what he loves for himself.”  
   > — *Sahih al-Bukhari* 13; *Sahih Muslim* 45

93. **He taught people to stop an oppressor rather than support him out of tribal loyalty.** When he said to help both the oppressed and the oppressor, he explained that helping the oppressor means physically or morally preventing the oppression.

   > “Help him by restraining him from oppression.”  
   > — *Sahih al-Bukhari* 2444

94. **He joined belief to care for neighbors, guests, and speech.** A person’s theology was tested in ordinary social friction: whether neighbors were harmed, guests honored, and words controlled.

   > “Whoever believes in God and the Last Day should not harm his neighbor, should honor his guest, and should speak good or remain silent.”  
   > — *Sahih al-Bukhari* 6018; *Sahih Muslim* 47

95. **He made reconciliation better than prolonged estrangement.** He discouraged believers from severing ordinary relations for more than three days and praised the one who begins the greeting.

   > “The better of the two is the one who begins with peace.”  
   > — *Sahih al-Bukhari* 6077; *Sahih Muslim* 2560

96. **He prohibited suspicion, spying, and searching for faults.** Community could not be built by moral surveillance and rumor. The Qur’an he recited protected private dignity from suspicion, surveillance, and backbiting.

   > “Avoid much suspicion… do not spy, and do not backbite one another.”  
   > — Qur’an 49:12; see also *Sahih al-Bukhari* 6064 and *Sahih Muslim* 2563

97. **He treated control of speech and sexual conduct as paths to salvation.** He warned that unrestrained words and appetites can destroy other people’s dignity and the speaker’s own soul.

   > “Whoever guarantees for me what is between his jaws and what is between his legs, I guarantee Paradise for him.”  
   > — *Sahih al-Bukhari* 6474

98. **He taught that religion should guide without crushing people.** He repeatedly instructed teachers and governors to make matters manageable, give hope, and avoid driving people away.

   > “Make things easy and do not make them difficult; give glad tidings and do not repel people.”  
   > — *Sahih al-Bukhari* 69, 6125; *Sahih Muslim* 1734

99. **He rejected unsustainable perfectionism.** Religion was described as easy in its structure, and people were told to aim rightly, come close when they could not be perfect, and continue steadily.

   > “This religion is ease. No one makes it harsh upon himself except that it overwhelms him.”  
   > — *Sahih al-Bukhari* 39

100. **He valued consistent good more than impressive bursts.** Spiritual life was not a cycle of public intensity followed by collapse. Small deeds retained over time were more beloved than unsustainable excess.

   > “The most beloved deeds to God are those done consistently, even if small.”  
   > — *Sahih al-Bukhari* 6465; *Sahih Muslim* 783

---

## XI. Knowledge, Work, Charity, and Public Welfare

101. **He made the pursuit of understanding a sign of divine good.** Knowledge in his teaching was not merely information; it was insight that produces responsible worship and sound judgment.

   > “When God intends good for someone, He gives him understanding of the religion.”  
   > — *Sahih al-Bukhari* 71; *Sahih Muslim* 1037

102. **He taught in ways designed for human attention.** He did not lecture constantly until people became numb. Ibn Masud said the Prophet selected appropriate times for teaching because he disliked boring people.

   > “He used to choose suitable times to teach us, fearing that we would become bored.”  
   > — *Sahih al-Bukhari* 68, 70

103. **He taught through questions and made room for inquiry.** The hadith collections preserve direct questions about faith, intimacy, grief, doubt, family conflict, worship, money, and death. In the famous hadith of Gabriel, the essentials of religion are taught through a sequence of clear questions and answers.

   > “Tell me about Islam… Tell me about faith… Tell me about excellence.”  
   > — The hadith of Gabriel, *Sahih Muslim* 8

104. **He honored work and self-sufficiency.** He taught that food earned by one’s own labor is better than dependence, and he reminded people that the prophet David ate from the work of his hands.

   > “No one has eaten better food than what he earned with his own hands.”  
   > — *Sahih al-Bukhari* 2072

105. **He condemned commercial deception.** When he found wet grain hidden beneath dry grain in a market, he exposed it and rejected cheating as incompatible with his community.

   > “Whoever cheats us is not one of us.”  
   > — *Sahih Muslim* 102

106. **He insisted that workers be paid and contracts honored.** In a sacred report, God declares Himself the opponent of the person who hires a worker, takes the full benefit of the labor, and withholds the wage.

   > “I will be the opponent of… a man who hires a worker, receives his labor in full, and does not pay him.”  
   > — *Sahih al-Bukhari* 2270

107. **He encouraged relief for debtors.** Financial claims were real, but mercy toward someone genuinely unable to pay was spiritually weighty. He told of a creditor forgiven by God because he instructed his agents to overlook the struggling debtor.

   > “Be lenient with the one in difficulty, so perhaps God will be lenient with us.”  
   > — *Sahih al-Bukhari* 2078; *Sahih Muslim* 1562

108. **He turned wealth into a social obligation.** Charity was not merely generosity by exceptional people. The required alms took wealth from the affluent and returned it to the poor, embedding redistribution into worship.

   > “It is taken from their rich and returned to their poor.”  
   > — *Sahih al-Bukhari* 1395; *Sahih Muslim* 19

109. **He encouraged lasting charitable institutions.** When Umar obtained valuable land, Muhammad advised him to preserve the asset and dedicate its yield to the poor, relatives, travelers, and public good. This became a foundation for charitable endowments.

   > “Retain the property itself and give its produce in charity.”  
   > — *Sahih al-Bukhari* 2737, 2764; *Sahih Muslim* 1632

110. **He joined spiritual life to cleanliness and public health.** Purity was described as half of faith. He taught washing, oral hygiene, sanitation, and a quarantine principle during plague: do not enter an affected land and do not flee from it.

   > “Purity is half of faith.”  
   > — *Sahih Muslim* 223

   > “If you hear of plague in a land, do not enter it; if it occurs where you are, do not leave fleeing from it.”  
   > — *Sahih al-Bukhari* 5728; *Sahih Muslim* 2219

## XII. War, Peace, Religious Difference, Creation, and His Final Legacy

111. **He forbade aggression even in war.** The Qur’an permitted fighting those who fought the Muslims but immediately prohibited transgression. Armed force was not morally self-justifying.

   > “Fight in God’s cause those who fight you, but do not transgress. God does not love transgressors.”  
   > — Qur’an 2:190

112. **He forbade the killing of women and children.** When a woman was found killed after a battle, he condemned it and prohibited killing noncombatants.

   > “The Messenger of God forbade the killing of women and children.”  
   > — *Sahih al-Bukhari* 3015; *Sahih Muslim* 1744

113. **He prohibited treachery and mutilation.** His commanders were instructed not to betray agreements, steal from spoils, mutilate bodies, or kill children. Ethical limits remained even when facing enemies.

   > “Do not act treacherously, do not mutilate, and do not kill a child.”  
   > — *Sahih Muslim* 1731

114. **He treated treaties as binding even when emotionally painful.** At Hudaybiyyah, Abu Jandal arrived in chains seeking protection, but the treaty had just required his return. Muhammad honored the agreement while assuring him that God would make a way out. Principle constrained immediate sympathy.

   > “Be patient and seek God’s reward. God will make for you and those with you a relief and a way out.”  
   > — Hudaybiyyah report in *Sahih al-Bukhari* 2731–2732 and the sira

115. **He offered protection even to an enemy seeking to hear the message.** The Qur’an required Muslims to grant asylum to a hostile polytheist who requested safety, allow him to hear, and then escort him to a secure place if he did not accept Islam.

   > “If any of the polytheists seeks your protection, grant it… then convey him to his place of safety.”  
   > — Qur’an 9:6

116. **He stood for the funeral of a Jewish man.** When told the deceased was Jewish, he answered by recalling shared humanity. Religious difference did not erase the gravity of death.

   > “Was he not a human soul?”  
   > — *Sahih al-Bukhari* 1312; *Sahih Muslim* 961

117. **He visited a sick Jewish boy.** The report shows ordinary human contact across religious lines. He did not limit concern to members of his own community.

   > “A Jewish boy used to serve the Prophet and became ill, so the Prophet went to visit him.”  
   > — *Sahih al-Bukhari* 1356

118. **He taught mercy toward animals as a matter of salvation.** A woman of sinful reputation was forgiven for giving water to a dying dog; another woman was condemned for imprisoning and starving a cat. Compassion and cruelty toward animals had eternal moral weight.

   > “God thanked her for it and forgave her.”  
   > — *Sahih al-Bukhari* 3321; see also 3318

119. **He made planting and removing harm acts of charity.** A tree that feeds a person, bird, or animal continues to generate reward. Even moving a harmful branch from a road could lead to forgiveness and Paradise.

   > “No Muslim plants a tree from which a person, animal, or bird eats except that it is charity for him.”  
   > — *Sahih Muslim* 1553; see also *Sahih al-Bukhari* 2320

120. **He died as he had lived: a servant-messenger concerned with God, prayer, justice, and people rather than dynastic grandeur.** He founded no hereditary dynasty and left no palace or accumulated treasury. His possessions were modest, his armor was mortgaged, and his message remained the trust. The Qur’an’s summary is therefore fitting: his mission was mercy, and his life was an example for those seeking God.

   > “We sent you only as a mercy to all peoples.”  
   > — Qur’an 21:107

## Conclusion: The Coherence of the Man

The case for admiring Muhammad does not rest on portraying him as soft in every situation or detached from the severe realities of seventh-century Arabia. He led a persecuted community, fought wars, judged disputes, enforced law, negotiated with enemies, managed family tensions, and carried political responsibility. The remarkable feature is the **moral coherence** visible across these roles.

When he lacked power, he preached without authorizing revenge. When he gained power, he remained personally unpretentious. He demanded justice but denied privilege to his family. He worshipped intensely but rejected religious excess for his followers. He could command armies yet stop a prayer early for a crying child. He received public revenue yet died with his armor pledged for food. He was obeyed by a growing civilization yet still worked in his home, listened to women, joked with children, sat among ordinary people, and accepted correction.

The Qur’an calls him an excellent example and a mercy to the worlds. The historical reports explain why those descriptions have carried such force for Muslims. They saw not only a preacher of mercy, truth, courage, patience, gratitude, and justice, but a man who practiced these qualities under pressure, grief, poverty, conflict, family life, victory, and approaching death.

> “In the Messenger of God you have an excellent example for whoever hopes in God and the Last Day and remembers God often.”  
> — Qur’an 33:21
