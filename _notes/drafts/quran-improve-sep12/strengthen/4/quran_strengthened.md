> This page presents evidence for the **miraculous nature** of the **Qur'an** and the **prophethood** of **Prophet Muhammad** ﷺ. Not every point is a miracle or proof on its own. Taken together, however, they form a cumulative case for the Qur'an's divine origin and the authenticity of the Prophet's mission.

The Qur'an and the life of Prophet Muhammad ﷺ together form a cumulative case for his prophethood.

The Qur'an was revealed orally over twenty-three years in seventh-century Arabia by a man who could neither read nor write. Its passages were revealed separately, often in response to unforeseen events and outside their final order, yet the Qur'an maintains unmatched eloquence, intricate structure, coherence, and depth throughout.

It openly explains its claim to revelation, invites scrutiny, and challenges its opponents to rival it. Its fulfilled prophecies, historical and linguistic details, early written transmission, and continuing recitation support its claim to divine origin. Its account of one transcendent yet near Creator gives unity to its treatment of repentance, human purpose, suffering, justice, and moral growth. It joins that vision to practical laws and habits of worship. The page examines these features together, addresses the difficult textual evidence, and compares the strongest alternative explanations of its authorship.

The life of Prophet Muhammad ﷺ further strengthens that case. He was known for exceptional integrity, mercy, courage, and humility. He embodied the justice and generosity he taught, endured years of persecution rather than abandon his message, and lived simply even after gaining authority. His public leadership and private conduct reflected the same devotion to God and responsibility toward others. His teachings and example transformed Arabia and shaped billions of lives across fourteen centuries, making him one of history's most influential figures.

The main alternative explanations fail to account for the evidence of his life and message. Deliberate fraud does not explain his personal sacrifices or his faithful transmission of revelations that openly corrected him. Sincere error does not explain decades of coherent teaching, sound judgment, and effective leadership. Evil inspiration is incompatible with a message that calls people to worship God, reject Satan and evil, live morally, and resist wrongdoing. The claim that his story was fabricated later is contradicted by early manuscript evidence and by the extensive record of his life carefully preserved by the first generations of Muslims.

The case is therefore cumulative: the Qur'an's extraordinary qualities, the Prophet Muhammad's ﷺ moral character and lasting influence, and the weakness of alternative explanations all reinforce one another. The Qur'an is presented as a lasting and preserved miracle from God, given to Prophet Muhammad ﷺ as evidence for his message to all humanity. Together with the carefully preserved record of his life, they provide every generation with tangible evidence by which to examine the Qur'an's claim to divine origin and assess Muhammad's ﷺ claim to prophethood.

## The Book

The Qur'an presents a remarkable convergence of signs pointing to its divine origin. Its mode of revelation, literary character, intricate composition, predictions, early preservation, depth of guidance, and relationship with the Messenger ﷺ are examined here as parts of one cumulative case.

**Remarkable and miraculous are different judgments.** Preservation alone does not establish divinity; human works can be well preserved. Eloquence, moral insight, influence, or a correct prediction also require further argument before being called miracles. The question is whether the strongest established features, taken together in their historical setting, are better explained by revelation than by the alternatives. Different lines of evidence carry different weight, and overlapping observations should not be counted as independent proofs.

*Translation note: quoted English translations use “God” for the Arabic divine name. New renderings are identified where used; the Arabic text is retained alongside close literary examples.*

### <span class="num-roman">I.</span> How the Qur'an Emerged
{: #mode-of-revelation }

1. The Qur'an was first proclaimed through oral recitation by Prophet Muhammad ﷺ, a man from seventh-century Mecca whom Islamic tradition describes as unable to read or write.
    > The Qur'an stated this openly before people who had known the Prophet ﷺ throughout his life. Had he been publicly known for reading and writing earlier scripture, his opponents would have had grounds to challenge that description. The absence of literacy would not, by itself, exclude oral knowledge or learning from others. The verse is quoted below.
    {: .gloss }

    > “You ˹O Prophet˺ could not read any writing ˹even˺ before this ˹revelation˺, nor could you write at all. Otherwise, the people of falsehood would have been suspicious.” (**Sūrat al-ʿAnkabūt 29:48**), Dr. Mustafa Khattab, *The Clear Quran*
    >
    > <div dir="rtl" lang="ar" class="quran-arabic">﴿وَمَا كُنتَ تَتْلُوا۟ مِن قَبْلِهِۦ مِن كِتَـٰبٍ وَلَا تَخُطُّهُۥ بِيَمِينِكَ ۖ إِذًا لَّٱرْتَابَ ٱلْمُبْطِلُونَ ۝٤٨﴾ <span class="ayah-ref">سُورَةُ العَنكَبُوتِ</span></div>
    {: .quote }

1. Its revelation unfolded over twenty-three years in separate portions, often in response to unforeseen questions and circumstances: persecution, migration, war, treaties, family disputes, legal questions, theological challenges, internal failures, victories, and defeats. The material was therefore not delivered from beginning to end as a prewritten book.

1. Its final arrangement is not simply chronological. Islamic transmission reports describe the Prophet ﷺ directing the placement of revealed material within chapters. Passages associated with different occasions consequently stand together in the Qur'an as recited today. Claims about particular compositional patterns should be distinguished from the separate historical evidence for when their parts were revealed.

1. Revelations were recited publicly, memorized by followers, used in prayer, and written down. That setting exposed the proclamation to communal memory and criticism, placing constraints on a theory of private, unrestricted rewriting. The later collection and standardization of written copies are examined in the preservation section.
    > This is crucial context for everything that follows: a proclamation delivered in portions across a changing public mission, and subsequently preserved as one scripture. The Qur'an also acknowledges changes of instruction within revelation; public transmission should not be confused with an assertion that no development or abrogation ever occurred. See [progressive revelation](#progressive-pedagogy).
    {: .gloss }

#### Continuity across Mecca and Medina
{: #meccan-medinan-continuity }

The change in circumstances was immense: the persecuted Meccan preacher became the leader of a community responsible for treaties, conflict, family law, and public affairs. The subjects and styles of passages vary accordingly, yet the foundational demands remain recognizable: worship God alone, answer for one's actions, care for the vulnerable, tell the truth, and prepare for judgment.

The passages traditionally classified as Meccan already join divine oneness to duties toward parents, children, the orphan, honest measurement, and just speech (6:151–153). Medinan passages demand justice even against oneself and one's family, or toward people one dislikes (4:135; 5:8). Increasing authority does not remove moral accountability. [Qur'an 6:151–153](https://quran.com/6:151-153), [4:135](https://quran.com/4:135), and [5:8](https://quran.com/5:8).

The point is continuity of theological and moral purpose through major changes, alongside genuine development in legislation and community life. Detailed assignments of individual passages to occasions and dates still depend on historical interpretation.

### <span class="num-roman">II.</span> Scripture Meant to Be Heard
{: #heard-scripture }

The Qur'an first reaches its audience as a voice. It calls itself a recitation, directs its messenger to recite deliberately, and asks those who hear it to listen attentively. Sound belongs to the way the message is delivered and understood. [Qur'an 73:4](https://quran.com/73:4), [7:204](https://quran.com/7:204).

1. **Meaning unfolds in time.** A listener encounters a question before its answer, an oath before its conclusion, a warning before its exception. Pauses, recurring endings, changes of pace, and shifts of address guide attention through that movement. Reading only a silent translation can obscure how the composition prepares, surprises, and involves its audience.

1. **Repetition helps people hear relationships.** A recurring phrase can join passages separated by several minutes of recitation. In chapter 55, the repeated question about denying the Lord's favors returns after creation, judgment, and descriptions of Paradise. Its wording remains stable while its surroundings deepen what the listener is being asked to acknowledge. [Qur'an 55](https://quran.com/55).

1. **Recitation connects literature, worship, and memory.** The same passages are learned by ear, recited in prayer, corrected by teachers, and examined in written copies. Their acoustic patterns help listeners retain both wording and sequence. That interaction helps explain the Qur'an's enduring presence as a spoken scripture across communities whose everyday languages differ.

William A. Graham and Navid Kermani treat the Qur'an's audible character and its reception through recitation as central to understanding it. This does not make writing secondary as historical evidence: early manuscripts and living recitation must be studied together. [“Recitation and aesthetic reception,” *The Cambridge Companion to the Qur'ān*](https://doi.org/10.1017/CCOL0521831601.007).

1. The Qur'an contains 114 chapters and fills about 600 pages in a common modern layout; page totals vary by edition. The literary argument concerns that whole work, as well as the quality of particular passages.

1. The Qur'an belongs to neither ordinary prose nor metrical poetry, but sustains a distinctive Qur'anic mode of expression—previously unknown to Arabic—in which rhythm, rhyme, cadence, syntax, and shifts of register are fused with precision of meaning.

1. The Qur'an maintains a recognizable literary identity across the reported twenty-three years of revelation, despite changes in circumstance, subject matter, audience, and political situation. Meccan and Medinan passages also have differences of style and emphasis; continuity does not require stylistic uniformity.

1. Major theorists of Qur'anic inimitability focused on **naẓm** [syntactic-semantic arrangement]: the precise relationships created by syntax, word order, omission, emphasis, definiteness and indefiniteness, conjunctions, grammatical person, expectation, context, rhythm, sound and meaning.
    > This gives the literary argument a more precise standard than simply saying that the Qur'an "sounds beautiful." In the mature Arabic rhetorical tradition associated especially with ʿAbd al-Qāhir al-Jurjānī, eloquence is produced by the way meanings are arranged through syntax and context. Modern historian of Arabic poetics Lara Harb likewise notes that medieval authors primarily located Qur'anic inimitability in sentence construction and the additional meanings conveyed through such features as omission, unusual word order, grammatical shifts, and definiteness.
    {: .gloss }
    > On this account, eloquence is not produced by collecting unusually ornate words. A common word can become extraordinary because of where it is placed, what is omitted around it, how the sentence is ordered, what the listener expects next, and how sound and meaning reinforce one another. This is one reason translation can communicate the basic meaning while losing much of the literary argument.

1. The Qur'an repeatedly uses highly compressed rhetorical devices in which form contributes directly to meaning: deliberate omission, changes in expected word order, movement between first-, second-, and third-person address, parallelism, contrast, recurring formulas, and abrupt changes in sentence length or cadence. These grammatical shifts perform recognized rhetorical functions in Arabic, changing perspective, emphasis, immediacy, and emotional force.
    > One famous category is *iltifāt* [a rhetorical shift in grammatical person or mode of address]. M. A. S. Abdel Haleem has shown how these shifts function systematically for rhetorical purposes rather than simply being grammatical accidents.
    {: .gloss }
    > A simple example appears in the opening chapter: it begins by speaking **about** God—“Lord of all worlds ... Master of the Day of Judgment”—and then turns to speaking **to** God: “You alone we worship and You alone we ask for help.” The shift enacts a movement from knowledge and praise into direct worship and prayer.

    > M. A. S. Abdel Haleem, ["Grammatical Shift for Rhetorical Purposes: Iltifāt and Related Features in the Qur'an"](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0041977X00003621), *Bulletin of the School of Oriental and African Studies* 55.3 (1992), 407–432.

1. The classical doctrine of inimitability became one of the major engines behind the development of Arabic rhetoric and literary theory. Scholars such as al-Khaṭṭābī, al-Rummānī, al-Bāqillānī, and ʿAbd al-Qāhir al-Jurjānī did not merely repeat that the Qur'an was eloquent; they attempted to analyze what its language was doing and why imitation was difficult.
    > Modern studies of classical Arabic poetics likewise show how deeply Qur'anic inimitability shaped the development of Arabic rhetorical theory; see *The Oxford Handbook of Qur'anic Studies* and Lara Harb's *Arabic Poetics*.
    {: .src }

1. The Qur'an became one of the foundational authorities used by early grammarians and philologists when describing Arabic. Its language helped establish the very grammatical and rhetorical tradition by which classical Arabic came to be analyzed and taught.

1. **Repetition with variation:** the Qur'an retells events—especially episodes involving Moses—many times. Repeated stories are not simply copied verbatim: details, dialogue, vocabulary, and emphasis are reshaped to serve the argument of the chapter in which each retelling appears.

    > One retelling may expand a confrontation, another suppress it; one emphasizes fear, another prophetic reassurance; one places a speech before a sign, another highlights the sign itself. The same narrative material is repeatedly reshaped to serve the argument of the chapter in which it appears.
    {: .gloss }
    > Leyla Ozgur Alhassen's *How the Qur'ān Works: Reading Sacred Narrative* studies Qur'anic repetition through motifs, themes, action sequences, parallels, reversals, and deliberate suppression of repeated material.

1. **Compositional architecture:** The Qur’an repeatedly displays intricate large-scale organization across individual passages, entire chapters, and even groups of chapters. Themes, key words, images, and arguments are introduced, developed, echoed, contrasted, and later returned to in deliberate ways. Many chapters use patterns of framing, parallel sections, thematic symmetry, recurring key terms, verbal echoes, and carefully placed transitions to connect distant parts of the text. These patterns give the composition a strong internal unity that often becomes fully apparent only when the whole composition is examined.

1. **Ring composition** is one striking example: many verses and chapters display tightly balanced chiastic structures in which the beginning and end correspond and the central passage carries special emphasis.
    > This is especially remarkable given that the Qur’an was revealed orally in separate portions over many years, often outside its final order and in response to changing circumstances, rather than composed from beginning to end as a manuscript that could be repeatedly reorganized and revised.

#### Three short chapters examined closely
{: #literary-demonstrations }

The literary claim becomes clearer when its parts can be inspected. These examples use simple English renderings prepared for this discussion, alongside the Arabic. The analysis identifies features an alternative composition would have to answer; it does not turn literary judgment into a mechanical score.

**1. Sūrat al-Ikhlāṣ [The Declaration of God's Oneness], chapter 112.** Four short verses move from a positive account of God to the exclusion of dependence, descent, and any equal.

> Say: He is God, uniquely One. God, the One on whom all depend. He neither begets nor was begotten. And no one is comparable to Him. [Qur'an 112:1–4](https://quran.com/112).
>
> <div dir="rtl" lang="ar" class="quran-arabic">قُلْ هُوَ اللَّهُ أَحَدٌ ۝ اللَّهُ الصَّمَدُ ۝ لَمْ يَلِدْ وَلَمْ يُولَدْ ۝ وَلَمْ يَكُنْ لَهُ كُفُوًا أَحَدٌ</div>
{: .quote }

| Wording | What it contributes |
| --- | --- |
| قُلْ — *qul* [say] | The messenger is instructed to transmit the declaration; the command itself remains in the recitation. |
| هُوَ اللَّهُ أَحَدٌ — *huwa … aḥad* [He is God, uniquely One] | The opening identifies the subject and asserts divine uniqueness. |
| اللَّهُ الصَّمَدُ — *al-ṣamad* [the One on whom all depend] | A compact divine name adds sovereignty and freedom from need. Classical explanations develop its richness; one English equivalent cannot exhaust it. |
| لَمْ يَلِدْ وَلَمْ يُولَدْ — *lam yalid wa-lam yūlad* [He neither begets nor was begotten] | The same root appears in active and passive forms. The balanced clauses exclude both directions of divine genealogy. |
| وَلَمْ يَكُنْ لَهُ كُفُوًا أَحَدٌ — [and no one is comparable to Him] | The final clause generalizes the exclusion to every equal or counterpart. The word *aḥad* returns: first “One,” then “anyone” within a negation. |

The chapter joins affirmation and negation, verbal symmetry, a returning key word, and a sequence of endings in the sound *d*. Replacing it with a list of theological propositions would communicate much of its teaching while losing that composition. Its brevity also explains how a child can learn its central message while a theologian continues to examine its implications. [Ibn Kathir on chapter 112](https://quran.com/112:1/tafsirs/en-tafisr-ibn-kathir).

**2. Sūrat al-ʿAṣr [Time], chapter 103.** An oath, a diagnosis of the human condition, and the way out are compressed into three verses.

> By time: humanity is indeed in loss, except those who believe, do righteous deeds, and urge one another to truth and to patience. [Qur'an 103:1–3](https://quran.com/103).
>
> <div dir="rtl" lang="ar" class="quran-arabic">وَالْعَصْرِ ۝ إِنَّ الْإِنْسَانَ لَفِي خُسْرٍ ۝ إِلَّا الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا وَعَمِلُوا الصَّالِحَاتِ وَتَوَاصَوْا بِالْحَقِّ وَتَوَاصَوْا بِالصَّبْرِ</div>
{: .quote }

The opening *wa-* [by] makes time the object of an oath. *Inna* and the emphatic *la-* [indeed] reinforce the warning; *fī khusr* [in loss] presents loss as the condition surrounding humanity. *Illā* [except] then turns the warning into a practical account of salvation. Faith and action concern what people become and do; the twice-repeated *tawāṣaw* [urge one another] makes truth and perseverance mutual responsibilities. The endings *ʿaṣr* [time], *khusr* [loss], and *ṣabr* [patience] bind that argument acoustically. The longer final verse supplies the answer to the two short, arresting opening verses.

**3. Sūrat al-Kawthar [Abundant Good], chapter 108.** Three verses connect gift, gratitude, and the reversal of an enemy's contempt.

> We have indeed given you abundant good. So pray to your Lord and sacrifice. Surely the one who hates you is the one cut off. [Qur'an 108:1–3](https://quran.com/108).
>
> <div dir="rtl" lang="ar" class="quran-arabic">إِنَّا أَعْطَيْنَاكَ الْكَوْثَرَ ۝ فَصَلِّ لِرَبِّكَ وَانْحَرْ ۝ إِنَّ شَانِئَكَ هُوَ الْأَبْتَرُ</div>
{: .quote }

*Innā* [indeed We] establishes assurance; *aʿṭaynāka* [We have given you] presents the gift as granted. *Al-kawthar* [abundant good] conveys overflowing bounty; an explanation attributed to Ibn ʿAbbās includes a river in Paradise within that gift. The connecting *fa-* [so] makes worship the response to generosity. *Li-rabbika* [to your Lord] directs gratitude toward its source. The final *huwa* [he] sharpens the reversal: the hater is the one described as *al-abtar* [cut off]. Abundance answers deprivation, while the three endings retain their final *r* sound. [Sahih al-Bukhari 4966](https://sunnah.com/bukhari:4966).

In each example, syntax, sound, economy, structure, and theological purpose work together. The individual devices are available to human speakers; the Muslim argument concerns their quality and combination, the wider book in which they occur, and the circumstances of its proclamation.

#### Comparing different kinds of Arabic discourse
{: #arabic-comparisons }

The following excerpts make differences of form visible. The English renderings explain the Arabic; they do not reproduce its meter or sound. A short sample illustrates a genre without representing everything that genre can achieve.

| Kind of discourse | Arabic excerpt and English rendering | What to compare |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Pre-Islamic poetry, the opening attributed to Imruʾ al-Qays | قِفَا نَبْكِ مِنْ ذِكْرَى حَبِيبٍ وَمَنْزِلِ — [Stop, you two; let us weep at the memory of a beloved and a dwelling.] | A personal, elegiac opening within a poem governed by a recurring metrical scheme. The Qur'an uses rhythm without sustaining that system of metrical verse. |
| *Sajʿ* [rhymed prose], a litigant's objection preserved in hadith | مَنْ لَا أَكَلَ وَلَا شَرِبَ وَلَا اسْتَهَلَّ فَمِثْلُ ذَلِكَ يُطَلُّ — [One who neither ate nor drank nor cried out: such a case should count for nothing.] | A sequence of clauses ends in *istahalla / yuṭall* [cried out / counts for nothing]. It demonstrates that rhyme itself was familiar and cannot alone establish a miracle. The Prophet ﷺ rejects this use of rhetoric to evade responsibility. |
| Prophetic hadith [reported teaching] | إِنَّمَا الأَعْمَالُ بِالنِّيَّاتِ — [Actions are judged according to intentions.] | A concise moral maxim, followed in the report by explanation and a practical example. Hadith can be highly eloquent while using a register distinct from sustained Qur'anic recitation. |
| An imitation attributed to Musaylima | وَالْفِيلُ وَمَا أَدْرَاكَ مَا الْفِيلُ لَهُ زُلُومٌ طَوِيلٌ — [The elephant—and what will make you know what the elephant is? It has a long trunk.] | It borrows an arresting Qur'anic question, then answers with a familiar physical feature. The comparison asks whether the conclusion warrants the rhetorical buildup and supplies comparable depth. |
| Qur'anic proclamation, 103:1–3 | وَالْعَصْرِ … إِنَّ الْإِنْسَانَ لَفِي خُسْرٍ — [By time … humanity is indeed in loss.] | An oath leads to a universal moral diagnosis, then an exception that sets out a whole pattern of life. The changing verse lengths serve that argument. |

Sources: [the opening of Imruʾ al-Qays as reproduced in *History of Arabic Literature*](https://www.hindawi.org/books/70629206/3.60/), [Sahih Muslim 1682a](https://sunnah.com/muslim:1682a), [Sahih al-Bukhari 1](https://sunnah.com/bukhari:1), and [Ibn Kathir's account of Musaylima](https://ftp.shamela.ws/book/23708/2094).

The Musaylima material survives in later, hostile Muslim reports, in differing forms. It illustrates how those writers understood failed imitation; it is not a securely preserved recording of the strongest work any opponent could have produced. A serious comparison must also consider strong Arabic literature and allow informed disagreement.

#### Rhyme, oaths, and parables that carry an argument
{: #rhetorical-arguments }

1. **Rhyme under semantic constraint.** The task is to sustain patterned sound while choosing words that also fit grammar, argument, imagery, and context. Chapter 93, for example, moves through endings such as *ḍuḥā* [morning brightness], *sajā* [grew still], and *qalā* [forsook or detested], then ends with instructions concerning the orphan, the petitioner, and God's blessing. The changing endings accompany a movement from reassurance into responsibility. This is a closer literary observation than a claim that every Qur'anic verse has the same rhyme. [Qur'an 93](https://quran.com/93).

1. **Oaths as miniature arguments.** In chapter 91, sun and moon, day and night, heaven and earth prepare the transition to the human soul and its capacity for wrongdoing and moral growth. In chapter 93, morning brightness and the stillness of night precede reassurance that the Prophet ﷺ has not been abandoned. Chapter 95 moves from sacred and natural imagery to human dignity and judgment; chapter 103 makes passing time the setting for human loss and salvation. Exegetes differ about some individual connections, but the images clearly frame the conclusions readers must consider. [Qur'an 91:1–10](https://quran.com/91), [93:1–11](https://quran.com/93), [95:1–8](https://quran.com/95), and [103:1–3](https://quran.com/103).

1. **Parables as compressed reasoning.** A good word is compared to a firmly rooted, fruitful tree, and a corrupt word to a tree torn loose from the ground (14:24–26): stability and fruitfulness become ways of judging belief and speech. Those who take protectors besides God resemble a spider relying on its frail house (29:41): the image questions the protection being sought, not the tensile strength of spider silk. Rain reviving dead land makes the possibility of resurrection imaginable through a recurring experience (35:9). Charity followed by injury is likened to soil washed off a smooth rock (2:264): its apparent promise produces no lasting crop. Each image supplies a relationship the reader can examine. [Qur'an 14:24–26](https://quran.com/14:24-26), [29:41](https://quran.com/29:41), [35:9](https://quran.com/35:9), and [2:264](https://quran.com/2:264).

#### Context-sensitive wording and divine names
{: #context-sensitive-wording }

Apparent synonyms do not necessarily carry the same implications. These examples concern the actual setting of each expression; they do not require a claim that an Arabic word has one rigid meaning everywhere.

| Passages | Difference in wording | Why it matters in context |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 6:151 and 17:31 | *Min imlāq* [because of poverty] and *khashyata imlāq* [for fear of poverty]. The first says God provides for “you and them”; the second for “them and you.” | The first addresses existing deprivation and reassures the parents first. The second addresses fear that children will cause deprivation and names the children's provision first. Exchanging the formulations would lose that close fit between anxiety and answer. |
| 18:78 and 18:82 | *Lam tastaṭiʿ* and *lam tasṭiʿ* [both: you could not bear]. | The fuller form appears before the difficult events are explained; the shorter form closes the explanation. Ibn Kathir reads the verbal lightening in relation to the difficulty being lifted. Both forms are grammatical; the rhetorical observation concerns their placement. |
| 11:44 | *Iblaʿī* [swallow] rather than an ordinary verb for drinking. | The command to the earth pictures floodwater being taken in decisively. Replacing “swallow” with “drink” would change the force and imagery, even though both concern taking in liquid. The paired command to the sky, *aqliʿī* [cease], gives the two movements a compact verbal balance. |
| 35:28 and 28:21 | *Yakhshā* [fears with awe] in the setting of knowledge of God; *khāʾifan* [afraid] when Moses leaves the city in danger. | In these settings, one expression belongs to reverent awareness and the other to an immediate human threat. A flat English “fear” conceals the difference. This is a contextual comparison, not a rule that the two Arabic roots never overlap. |
| 108:1 and 108:3 | *Al-kawthar* [abundant good] and *al-abtar* [cut off]. | These are opposing images, not interchangeable labels for success and failure. The final deprivation answers the opening abundance, giving the compact chapter its reversal. |

See [Qur'an 11:44](https://quran.com/11:44), [35:28](https://quran.com/35:28), [28:21](https://quran.com/28:21), [Ibn Kathir on 17:31](https://quran.com/17:31/tafsirs/en-tafisr-ibn-kathir) and [18:82](https://quran.com/18:82/tafsirs/en-tafisr-ibn-kathir), and [chapter 108](https://quran.com/108).

The same attention to context appears in **divine names at verse endings**:

1. In [2:226–227](https://quran.com/2:226-227), a return from marital estrangement is followed by **Forgiving, Merciful**; a decision to divorce is followed by **Hearing, Knowing**. The first ending holds open reconciliation and pardon. The second reminds the parties that their declarations and intentions are known to God.

1. In [5:118](https://quran.com/5:118), Jesus entrusts judgment to God and ends with **Mighty, Wise**, even when mentioning forgiveness. The focus is God's authority and wisdom in deciding the matter. Automatically substituting “Forgiving, Merciful” would shift that emphasis.

1. In [49:13](https://quran.com/49:13), human worth is measured by consciousness of God, followed by **Knowing, Aware**. The closing names fit the claim: the inward quality by which people are judged is fully known to God, beyond the lineage and status visible to others.

These readings explain why familiar endings can do precise work. Their recurrence provides a theological vocabulary whose local use still needs to be examined.

#### Connections across the book
{: #connections-across-the-book }

1. **One passage illuminates another.** The command to worship in 51:56 is followed by the clarification that God needs neither provision nor food from human beings (51:57–58). The promise of forgiveness in 39:53 continues with a call to return to God in 39:54. The praise of security for those whose faith is not mixed with wrongdoing (6:82) was explained by the Prophet ﷺ through the description of associating partners with God as a grave wrong in 31:13. These connections clarify meaning, scope, and application. [Qur'an 51:56–58](https://quran.com/51:56-58), [39:53–54](https://quran.com/39:53-54), and [Sahih al-Bukhari 3360](https://sunnah.com/bukhari:3360).

1. **Chapters also speak to their neighbors.** Chapters 105–106 move from the defeat of the army associated with the elephant to the security and provision enjoyed by Quraysh, then to the duty to worship the Lord of the House. Chapters 93–94 pair divine care and reassurance with responsibility, effort, and the promise of ease with hardship. The final two chapters, 113–114, both begin by seeking refuge, moving from external dangers to the whispering that reaches the human heart. These are identifiable verbal and thematic relationships, even where scholars disagree about the full extent or origin of larger arrangements. [Qur'an 105](https://quran.com/105), [106](https://quran.com/106), [93](https://quran.com/93), [94](https://quran.com/94), [113](https://quran.com/113), and [114](https://quran.com/114).

1. **Larger organizing themes have been seriously studied.** Exegetes including al-Biqāʿī, al-Farāḥī, and Iṣlāḥī explored relationships among passages and chapters; modern scholarship examines both those proposals and particular compositional patterns. A claimed structure should account for the whole unit, explain its boundaries, and acknowledge difficult passages. It should not be built by selecting only matching phrases. Nicolai Sinai discusses this tradition of thematic coherence and, separately, the study of inner-Qur'anic interpretation. [“Reading Sūrat al-Anʿām”](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/reclaiming-islamic-tradition/reading-surat-alanam-with-muhammad-rashid-rida-and-sayyid-qusb/8431797630F199956F13CC01456B43B9), [“Historical Criticism and Recent Trends in Western Scholarship on the Qur'an”](https://doi.org/10.29117/jcsis.2020.0259).

### <span class="num-roman">IV.</span> Challenges and Bold Claims
{: #challenges-and-bold-claims }

1. The Qur'an claims to transmit revelation from God in its own wording. The force of this claim depends on how its language, teaching, and history bear examination; its assertion of authority is the beginning of the inquiry.

1. Near the beginning of the book it presents itself without hesitation:
    > "This is the Book! There is no doubt about it—a guide for those mindful ˹of God˺" (**Sūrat al-Baqarah 2:2**), Dr. Mustafa Khattab, *The Clear Quran*
    >
    > <div dir="rtl" lang="ar" class="quran-arabic">﴿ذَٰلِكَ ٱلْكِتَـٰبُ لَا رَيْبَ ۛ فِيهِ ۛ هُدًى لِّلْمُتَّقِينَ ۝٢﴾ <span class="ayah-ref">سُورَةُ البَقَرَةِ</span></div>
    {: .quote }

1. The Qur'an openly challenges its opponents and all of humanity to produce something comparable to it. Different passages frame the challenge as an equivalent to the Qur'an, ten chapters, or even one chapter, with whatever help can be assembled.
    > “Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ ‘If ˹all˺ humans and jinn were to come together to produce the equivalent of this Qur'an, they could not produce its equal, no matter how they supported each other.'” (**Sūrat al-Isrāʾ 17:88**), Dr. Mustafa Khattab, *The Clear Quran*

#### What would count as answering the challenge?
{: #standard-for-literary-challenge }

On the literary account developed here, a proposed answer should be an original Arabic composition of comparable scope to the passage challenged, capable of sustaining precise meaning, grammatical control, economical expression, coherent structure, and effective sound together. Its rhetorical purpose should justify its choices. Merely borrowing Qur'anic phrases or appending rhyming endings would not answer that case; disagreement with Islam would not disqualify a text.

Classical theorists did not supply one uncontested checklist: some emphasized arrangement, others additional dimensions or different explanations of inimitability. A fair comparison therefore states the theory being tested, examines strong candidates closely, and permits informed critical disagreement. It cannot define success as conversion, popularity, or agreement by people already committed to the impossibility of a rival. Literary assessment is reasoned judgment rather than laboratory measurement. [Lara Harb on classical theories of arrangement](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/arabic-poetics/nazm-wonder-and-the-inimitability-of-the-quran/B980231F9936B751EF11029D69CF4554).

### <span class="num-roman">V.</span> A Scripture That Explains Its Own Claim
{: #self-referential-character }

The Qur'an repeatedly speaks about itself: its source, purpose, language, delivery, interpretation, reception, and preservation. Its self-description is woven into the message, so the listener is asked to consider both what it teaches and why it should be accepted as revelation.

| What it explains | Examples |
| --- | --- |
| **What it is and where it comes from** | Revelation from the Lord of all worlds, delivered to the messenger's heart in clear Arabic (26:192–195); guidance and a criterion for distinguishing truth from falsehood (2:185). |
| **Why it was revealed** | To bring people from darkness into light (14:1), guide them toward what is most upright (17:9), and invite reflection (38:29). |
| **Why it came in stages** | The objection that it should have arrived all at once is answered in terms of strengthening the messenger's heart and responding to objections (25:32–33). |
| **How it should be approached** | Through careful recitation (73:4), attentive listening (7:204), and reflection (47:24), with a distinction between foundational and less immediately clear passages (3:7). |
| **What opponents say about it** | The text preserves accusations of human assistance and ancient tales (25:4–5), as well as poetry and confused dreams (21:5). |
| **What response it seeks and what it promises** | Awe and softened hearts (39:23), guidance expressed in conduct, and divine protection of the message (15:9). |

These features can be checked in the passages themselves: [26:192–195](https://quran.com/26:192-195), [25:32–33](https://quran.com/25:32-33), [3:7](https://quran.com/3:7), and [39:23](https://quran.com/39:23). The repeated command *qul* [say] makes the relationship especially visible: the messenger recites even the instruction directing him to speak.

Anne-Sylvie Boisliveau's research examines how the Qur'an describes its own status and argues for its authority; Daniel Madigan and Stefan Wild have also made its self-referential character a subject of sustained study. These scholars investigate the phenomenon without thereby endorsing the theological conclusion drawn here. [Boisliveau's explanation of her research, International Qur'anic Studies Association](https://iqsaweb.org/2013/02/04/sr/).

A declaration of divine origin cannot authenticate itself. Its importance is that it defines a claim that can be compared with the text's actual character and history. The Qur'an does not leave later readers to invent its central claim on its behalf.

### <span class="num-roman">VI.</span> Appeal to Reason and Invitation of Scrutiny
{: #reason-and-scrutiny }

1. The Qur'an grounds its own authenticity in being coherent and invites the reader to examine it closely.
    > "Then do they not reflect upon the Qur’ān? If it had been from [any] other than God, they would have found within it much contradiction." (**Sūrat al-Nisāʾ 4:82**), Saheeh International
    >
    > <div dir="rtl" lang="ar" class="quran-arabic">﴿أَفَلَا يَتَدَبَّرُونَ ٱلْقُرْءَانَ ۚ وَلَوْ كَانَ مِنْ عِندِ غَيْرِ ٱللَّهِ لَوَجَدُوا۟ فِيهِ ٱخْتِلَـٰفًا كَثِيرًا ۝٨٢﴾ <span class="ayah-ref">سُورَةُ النِّسَاءِ</span></div>
    {: .quote }

1. The Qur'an repeatedly argues rather than merely asserting. It appeals to reason and asks the reader to reflect and consider implications. It also appeals to history, nature, conscience, and consistency.
    > “Or were they created by nothing, or are they ˹their own˺ creators?” (**Sūrat al-Ṭūr 52:35**), Dr. Mustafa Khattab, *The Clear Quran*
    >
    > <div dir="rtl" lang="ar" class="quran-arabic">﴿أَمْ خُلِقُوا۟ مِنْ غَيْرِ شَىْءٍ أَمْ هُمُ ٱلْخَـٰلِقُونَ ۝٣٥﴾ <span class="ayah-ref">سُورَةُ الطُّورِ</span></div>
    {: .quote }

    > “Had there been other gods besides God in the heavens or the earth, both ˹realms˺ would have surely been corrupted. So Glorified is God, Lord of the Throne, far above what they claim.” (**Sūrat al-Anbiyāʾ 21:22**), Dr. Mustafa Khattab, *The Clear Quran*
    >
    > <div dir="rtl" lang="ar" class="quran-arabic">﴿لَوْ كَانَ فِيهِمَآ ءَالِهَةٌ إِلَّا ٱللَّهُ لَفَسَدَتَا ۚ فَسُبْحَـٰنَ ٱللَّهِ رَبِّ ٱلْعَرْشِ عَمَّا يَصِفُونَ ۝٢٢﴾ <span class="ayah-ref">سُورَةُ الأَنبِيَاءِ</span></div>
    {: .quote }

#### The listener is asked to participate
{: #reader-participation }

The Qur'an repeatedly turns its argument toward the person hearing it. Questions about human origin, food, water, and fire ask listeners to consider their dependence (56:58–73). The repeated question in chapter 55 asks for acknowledgment; the question in 81:26—*fa-ayna tadhhabūn* [where, then, are you going?]—turns description into a demand to reconsider one's direction. [Qur'an 56:58–73](https://quran.com/56:58-73), [55:13](https://quran.com/55:13), and [81:26](https://quran.com/81:26).

The audience is consequently part of the composition. A parable requires a judgment, a warning calls for a response, and an objection is voiced before it is answered. This helps explain how argument, recitation, and moral self-examination reinforce one another.

#### Publicly testable claims during revelation
{: #public-scrutiny-during-revelation }

The Qur'an addresses people who knew its messenger and lived through events it discussed. It appeals to his earlier life among them (10:16) and his lack of prior reading and writing of scripture (29:48); it records accusations about teachers and collaborators (16:103; 25:4–5); it comments on public defeats and promises future outcomes. In their original setting, such claims exposed the message to contradiction from people with relevant knowledge. [Qur'an 10:16](https://quran.com/10:16), [29:48](https://quran.com/29:48), and [25:4–5](https://quran.com/25:4-5).

This is **public accountability**, not a claim that every religious assertion is experimentally falsifiable. A contemporary could challenge a statement about the messenger's background or a promised event; they could not directly inspect the unseen source of revelation. Nor do we possess a complete archive of every opponent's response. The Qur'an's own report of an exchange must not be counted as independent verification of the answer it gives.

The historical argument becomes stronger when public circumstances are supported by evidence beyond the claim being tested. Predictions especially require a defensible date before fulfillment and a clear account of what would count as failure. The evidence is considered under [prophecies](#prophecies) and [the standard for reassessment](#what-would-weaken-the-case).

The Qur'an presents itself as the final revelation in the same prophetic line as Abraham, Moses, and Jesus. It recounts earlier sacred history with an authoritative and independent voice, correcting theological distortions, restoring pure monotheism, and reshaping familiar narratives with remarkable precision.

1. Its historical narratives contain details that Muslim writers have argued are strikingly accurate in comparison with earlier accounts. Specific claims require independent historical evidence and careful dating of the events described.

#### One prophetic message across human history
{: #universality-of-prophecy }

The Qur'an places Muhammad ﷺ within a much wider history of divine guidance. It teaches that every community has received a messenger or warner (16:36; 35:24), and describes the message of earlier prophets as the call to worship God alone (21:25). Noah, Abraham, Moses, Jesus, and Muhammad ﷺ belong to one underlying religious mission, even where communities have received different legal prescriptions (42:13; 5:48). [Qur'an 16:36](https://quran.com/16:36), [21:25](https://quran.com/21:25), [42:13](https://quran.com/42:13), and [5:48](https://quran.com/5:48).

This offers a broad account of religious history: humanity is repeatedly guided, people are responsible for their response, and the final messenger calls them back to the same Creator. The Qur'an also says that not every messenger has been named (40:78). Its universal claim therefore extends beyond the particular nations and figures whose stories it recounts. [Qur'an 40:78](https://quran.com/40:78).

#### Jesus and Mary: exceptional honor within monotheism
{: #jesus-and-mary }

Mary is described as chosen and purified (3:42), and her chastity and faith are held up as an example (66:12). Jesus is the Messiah, born miraculously, associated with a word from God, and granted extraordinary signs by God's permission (3:45–49). The Qur'an's honor for them is integral to its own message. [Qur'an 3:42–49](https://quran.com/3:42-49) and [66:12](https://quran.com/66:12).

At the same time, it insists that Jesus is God's messenger and servant, not God, and rejects making divine unity a plurality (4:171; 19:30). His exceptional birth does not require divinity: the Qur'an compares his creation with Adam's (3:59). Its description of Jesus as a word from God is read within that creative command, not as an endorsement of an incarnate divine person. [Qur'an 4:171](https://quran.com/4:171), [19:30](https://quran.com/19:30), and [3:59](https://quran.com/3:59).

The resulting position is distinctive: it honors Jesus and Mary while drawing a firm distinction between the Creator and even the most honored of His creatures. For the cumulative case, that combination matters as a sustained theological position, beyond isolated comparisons of historical detail.

#### A wider religious setting than a single written source
{: #late-antique-engagement }

The Qur'an engages a world of scriptural stories, preaching, worship, and debate. Modern scholars compare its narratives with biblical, rabbinic, Syriac Christian, apocryphal, and other late-antique traditions, many of which could also circulate orally. Tommaso Tesei, for example, distinguishes knowledge of particular writings from encounters with themes transmitted through a wider religious environment. [“Echoes of Pseudepigrapha in the Qur'ān”](https://doi.org/10.1017/9789048540105.009).

The presence of parallels does not make the Qur'an a transcript of one source. Its versions characteristically foreground divine unity, prophetic mission, repentance, and judgment. Nor does their transformation by itself rule out human composition. Both the relationships and the differences need close study; a historical dependence theory must explain the route of transmission as well as identify a resemblance. Those questions are examined under [alternative explanations](#borrowing-hypothesis).

### <span class="num-roman">VIII.</span> Knowledge of Nature
{: #nature-knowledge }

The Qur'an repeatedly directs human beings to the natural world as evidence of God's wisdom and power: the heavens and earth, rain, animals, human development, day and night, winds, seas, mountains, and the origins and fragility of life.

The examples below have different kinds of significance. The Qur'an's appeal to the created world is clear; identifying a specific modern discovery in a verse often requires additional interpretive and historical arguments.

#### A standard for claims about science
{: #scientific-claim-standards }

| Kind of claim | What it establishes |
| --- | --- |
| **A clear statement about nature** | Something the Arabic directly says, such as describing rain-bearing clouds as heavy. Whether that knowledge was exceptional for its time is a separate question. |
| **Compatibility with later knowledge** | An established meaning can be read consistently with a scientific finding. Compatibility does not by itself constitute prediction. |
| **A suggestive correspondence** | A particular image or formulation invites comparison with modern knowledge, while other readings remain plausible. |
| **A disputed retrospective reading** | The claim depends on selecting an uncertain meaning or supplying technical detail absent from the text. It should not be presented as settled evidence. |

A strong claim should fit the Arabic and immediate context, account for earlier interpretations, distinguish observation from prediction, and establish what was available in the historical setting. Scientific findings also need appropriate scientific sources. The case for revelation should not depend on making every popular correspondence succeed.

### <span class="num-roman">IX.</span> Numerical Patterns
{: #numerical-patterns }

Counts can reveal how repetition and distribution contribute to composition. A numerical coincidence, however striking, is not automatically evidence of divine authorship. A responsible claim should be reproducible before its significance is assessed.

1. **Define the text.** Identify the reading, edition, verse-numbering system, and whether opening invocations are included. A count cannot silently move between different written forms.
1. **Publish the counting rule.** State whether the unit is an exact written form, a dictionary form, a root, a letter, or a phrase. Explain the treatment of attached particles, spelling variation, and vocalization before selecting the result.
1. **Show every occurrence.** Readers should be able to repeat the count from a concordance, an explicit verse list, or code. Related forms cannot be included on one side and excluded on the other merely to obtain a match.
1. **Assess chance and flexibility.** A large text permits many comparisons. The more words, numbers, spellings, and combinations searched, the easier accidental matches become. A proposed miracle needs a reason to expect this particular relationship and a fair comparison with what flexible searching can find elsewhere.
1. **Separate a count from its explanation.** A correct count establishes a textual fact. Its literary function may be clear even when an inference to supernatural design is not justified.

Two transparent examples concern whole-verse refrains in the common Ḥafṣ text and Kufan verse numbering:

| Refrain | Exact rule and occurrences | Literary role |
| --- | --- | --- |
| فَبِأَيِّ آلَاءِ رَبِّكُمَا تُكَذِّبَانِ — [Which of your Lord's favors will you both deny?] | **31** complete occurrences in chapter 55, at verses 13, 16, 18, 21, 23, 25, 28, 30, 32, 34, 36, 38, 40, 42, 45, 47, 49, 51, 53, 55, 57, 59, 61, 63, 65, 67, 69, 71, 73, 75, and 77. ### <span class="num-roman">X.</span> Preservation and Memorization
{: #preservation-and-memorization }

The Qur'an's preservation is one of the most remarkable features of its history: it was transmitted simultaneously through writing, memorization, public recitation, and teacher-to-student instruction from the earliest Muslim community onward.

1. The Qur'an's standard written text has been transmitted with exceptional stability for over fourteen centuries through mutually reinforcing channels: early manuscripts, inherited recitation traditions, memorization, public worship, and continuous copying in Arabic. A precise account also includes recognized readings, early variation, and the history of standardization, examined below.
    > These overlapping channels form an unusually powerful preservation system. The same text was simultaneously recited, memorized, taught, written, copied, and heard publicly across expanding Muslim communities, making alteration extraordinarily difficult and preserving the Qur'an in the language in which it was revealed.
    {: .gloss }
    > This combination gives later readers unusually direct access to an early Arabic scripture. Comparisons with other ancient texts should use the same standards: the age and extent of surviving witnesses, the kinds of variation, and the relationship between written and oral transmission.
    {: .gloss }

1. **Shared spelling peculiarities point to a common written archetype.** Marijn van Putten compared early Qur'an manuscripts and found idiosyncratic spellings recurring in the same locations across manuscripts. Because these spellings often make no difference in meaning, independent scribes would not be expected repeatedly to make the same arbitrary choice in the same place or recreate it independently from fresh oral dictation. The pattern strongly points to descent from a single written exemplar.
    > Van Putten concludes that the early manuscripts he studied descend from a single written archetype and that their date is consistent with the traditional attribution of the standardization to the caliph ʿUthmān ibn ʿAffān. This is materially stronger than the mere existence of an old manuscript: genealogy is being inferred from shared scribal fingerprints.
    {: .gloss }
    > Marijn van Putten, [“‘The Grace of God’ as evidence for a written Uthmanic archetype: the importance of shared orthographic idiosyncrasies”](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0041977X19000338), *Bulletin of the School of Oriental and African Studies* 82.2 (2019), 271–288.

#### What is preserved, and what a manuscript records
{: #text-and-writing-system }

A careful preservation argument distinguishes the **wording of the text**, the **script used to record it**, and the **readings through which it is recited**. Calling every written difference a change of scripture confuses these categories; calling every variant merely a spelling difference does the same.

| Feature | What it records or changes | What follows |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Rasm [consonantal framework]** | The early written outline of words, including some letters used for long vowels. Several consonants share a basic shape when unpointed. | It is the principal object of arguments for an early standard written text. It does not encode every detail of pronunciation by itself. |
| **Consonantal dots** | Marks distinguishing letters that share an outline. Early Arabic already used some dots, though their application was less systematic than in modern print. | More complete dotting can make an existing reading explicit. Different dotting can also distinguish readings, so each case must be examined. |
| **Vowel and recitation marks** | Short vowels, doubled consonants, pauses, and other aids to pronunciation. | Adding a fuller notation system does not by itself create new wording. Different vowels can nevertheless produce differences in grammar or meaning. |
| **Spelling conventions** | Whether, for example, a long vowel is written with a full letter or represented more economically. | Two spellings can represent the same recitation. Old spelling also preserves evidence about how manuscripts are related. |
| **Reading variants** | Inherited differences of pronunciation, vowels, consonantal pointing, morphology, and occasionally words, including variants associated with regional standard codices. | Some are differences of sound; some affect meaning or interpretation. They should be identified rather than described collectively as accents. |
| **Scribal or early nonstandard variants** | Copying errors, corrections, or wording outside the standard textual tradition. | These belong to textual history. A mistake in a particular copy is not automatically a change adopted by the community, but substantive variants cannot simply be dismissed as calligraphy. |

The dated inscription of Zuhayr, from 24 AH / 644–645 CE, is one material reminder that consonantal dots were already in use very early. The issue is their extent and consistency, not a story in which every dot was invented centuries later. [ʿAli ibn Ibrahim Ghabban and Robert Hoyland, study of the Zuhayr inscription](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1600-0471.2008.00297.x).

#### Canonical readings: a bounded tradition
{: #canonical-readings }

The **qirāʾāt [recognized Qur'anic reading traditions]** are transmitted ways of reciting the text. The familiar Ḥafṣ transmission from ʿĀṣim and Warsh transmission from Nāfiʿ belong to this wider tradition. Classical Sunni scholarship eventually recognized ten canonical readings, with named transmitters and further specified transmission routes. That later classification should not be confused with the beginning of Qur'anic recitation.

Acceptance traditionally required sound transmission, conformity with Arabic, and compatibility with the written form of at least one of the standard regional codices. Those regional exemplars shared the same overwhelmingly stable text while differing at a limited number of places. The traditional concept of seven *aḥruf* [authorized modes of revelation, whose precise meaning is disputed] is a separate subject; it should not be equated with the seven readings selected by Ibn Mujāhid or the later ten-reading canon. [Marijn van Putten, *Quranic Arabic: From Its Hijazi Origins to Its Classical Reading Traditions*](https://doi.org/10.1163/9789004506251).

A familiar example is [1:4](https://quran.com/1:4): *mālik* [Master or Owner] and *malik* [King] of the Day of Judgment. Both fit the early spelling and belong to recognized transmission. Their meanings differ while affirming compatible aspects of divine sovereignty. Other variants can bear on grammatical analysis or legal interpretation; preservation need not be defended by claiming that none affects meaning.

Modern work makes the interaction especially interesting. In his study of Hišām's *Ibrāhām/Ibrāhīm* [two recited forms of Abraham's name], van Putten argues that the distribution of the readings was influenced by manuscript spelling. He also identifies readings whose agreement is difficult to explain without an inherited oral component. His conclusion supports an interaction between writing and recitation, rather than two entirely independent streams. [“Hišām's ʾIbrāhām”](https://doi.org/10.1017/S1356186319000518).

The apologetic significance is the preservation of identifiable, constrained traditions whose differences can be named and studied. Islamic belief that the accepted readings transmit authorized revelation goes beyond what manuscript analysis alone can establish; the historical evidence should be stated at its own level.

#### The difficult early evidence
{: #difficult-textual-evidence }

1. **The Ṣanʿāʾ palimpsest contains real textual variants.** A palimpsest is parchment whose earlier writing was erased and written over. In this manuscript, the upper layer belongs to the standard Qur'anic text type; the lower layer preserves a different early textual form. It includes differences in words, additions or omissions, and arrangement, not just dots or spelling. Behnam Sadeghi and Mohsen Goudarzi interpret it as evidence of an early nonstandard textual tradition; Asma Hilali offers a different account of its production and use. Some readings of the damaged undertext are themselves disputed. [Sadeghi and Goudarzi, “Ṣanʿāʾ 1 and the Origins of the Qur'ān”](https://doi.org/10.1515/islam-2011-0025); [Hilali, *The Sanaa Palimpsest*](https://www.iis.ac.uk/publications-listing/the-sanaa-palimpsest/).

   This evidence qualifies an absolute claim that no early copy ever differed in wording. It also places recognizable Qur'anic material, including an early alternative form, near Islam's beginnings. It does not support the claim that the Qur'an first came into existence centuries later. The proper questions are when the variations arose, how extensive they were, and how they relate to standardization.

1. **Companion codices require separate examination.** Muslim sources report collections associated with Companions such as Ibn Masʿūd and Ubayy ibn Kaʿb, with differences in order and wording. Reports also raise questions about the inclusion of the opening and final refuge chapters in Ibn Masʿūd's collection, and supplicatory texts associated with Ubayy's. Their reliability and interpretation are debated: a transmitted report about a personal collection is not a surviving autograph, and notes or prayers in a collection are not automatically claims to additional revelation. These cases must be assessed individually, without assuming that every report is either authentic scripture or meaningless error. The Ṣanʿāʾ study compares the manuscript with reported Companion variants. [Sadeghi and Goudarzi](https://doi.org/10.1515/islam-2011-0025).

1. **Standardization was an intervention in transmission.** The traditional account says that ʿUthmān commissioned standard copies and ordered other written materials to be destroyed amid disagreements over recitation. A credible defense should state this openly. The historical argument is that a common written standard was established extraordinarily early and then widely transmitted; it is not that early Muslims never encountered variation or made decisions about copies. [Sahih al-Bukhari 4987](https://sunnah.com/bukhari:4987).

1. **Parchment dates are not ink dates.** The Birmingham test dates the animal skin, not the act of writing. Its date range must be considered alongside script, layout, and textual relationships. A fragment is evidence for the passages it contains, not by itself a physical witness to every verse in the complete book. [University of Birmingham, manuscript FAQ](https://www.birmingham.ac.uk/facilities/cadbury/birmingham-quran-mingana-collection/birmingham-quran/faqs).

These distinctions support a precise claim: **the standard Qur'anic text was established very early and has been transmitted with exceptional stability, alongside a defined tradition of readings**. They do not warrant saying that all manuscripts, marks, readings, and early personal collections were identical in every respect.

#### How early was the standard written text established?
{: #early-textual-closure }

Shared spelling peculiarities and the family relationships among manuscripts, discussed above, point toward a common written ancestor. Nicolai Sinai argues that a standard consonantal text by about **650 CE or earlier** should be the default historical position unless persuasive evidence requires a later date. This is close to the traditional period of ʿUthmān's standardization, within a generation of the Prophet's ﷺ death. [“When did the consonantal skeleton of the Quran reach closure? Part II”](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0041977X14000111).

The inference is substantial: theories requiring the Qur'an's basic text to be assembled two or three centuries later face early physical evidence that they cannot explain away. It remains a historical inference about the standard text, not a date assigned to every surviving manuscript or proof of every traditional detail about collection. Questions about the earliest variation, regional exemplars, spelling, and readings remain legitimate areas of research.

#### Evidence beyond Qur'an manuscripts
{: #early-documentary-evidence }

Different materials answer different historical questions. An inscription can attest the public use of a passage; a coin can date a religious formula; a receipt can show language and administrative practice without quoting an entire verse.

| Material witness | Date and content | What it establishes |
| --- | --- | --- |

#### One recited language across many peoples
{: #global-arabic-transmission }

Muslims whose first languages include Indonesian, Turkish, Hausa, Persian, Urdu, and English learn Qur'anic passages in Arabic and share them in worship. Translations explain the meaning, while the original Arabic remains the common liturgical reference. Recognized reading traditions remain part of that shared inheritance; the claim is a common scriptural language, not one worldwide pronunciation in every detail.

The Qur'an also helped preserve access to the language in which it first appeared. Recitation, memorization, grammar, commentary, and lexicography maintained a continuing path into early Arabic. Spoken Arabic has changed, and historical expertise still matters, but later readers are not dependent solely on translations into languages far removed from the original. These practices make linguistic continuity part of the transmission itself. [Graham and Kermani on recitation](https://doi.org/10.1017/CCOL0521831601.007).

The Qur'an contains bold predictions made in circumstances where their fulfillment was far from guaranteed. ### <span class="num-roman">XII.</span> God, Human Purpose, and the Meaning of Life
{: #theology-of-god }

The Qur'an's guidance begins with a sustained account of who God is and what it means to live before Him. Divine oneness connects its theology to worship, ethics, human dignity, and judgment. Colin Turner likewise treats divine unity as the organizing center of the Qur'anic worldview in his study of Said Nursi's interpretation. [“On Divine Unity,” *The Qur'an Revealed*](https://doi.org/10.1017/9783940924292.003).

#### One Creator, without need or equal
{: #oneness-and-divine-attributes }

1. **God is uniquely One and independent of creation.** Chapter 112 excludes divine offspring, ancestry, and equals. The Throne Verse joins life, sustaining power, knowledge, sovereignty, and freedom from weariness in one concentrated account (2:255). Human beings need God; He does not need their worship or provision (35:15; 51:56–58). The theology directs worship toward the source of existence rather than toward any dependent being within it. [Qur'an 112](https://quran.com/112), [2:255](https://quran.com/2:255), and [35:15](https://quran.com/35:15).

1. **Power is joined to knowledge, mercy, and justice.** God knows what is hidden as well as what is outward, and His sovereignty is accompanied by wisdom. His mercy encompasses all things (7:156), yet He wrongs no one even by an atom's weight (4:40). The same revelation that warns against evil repeatedly opens the possibility of repentance. These attributes form a connected account of the God before whom people are responsible. [Qur'an 59:22–24](https://quran.com/59:22-24), [4:40](https://quran.com/4:40), and [39:53–54](https://quran.com/39:53-54).

#### Transcendent, yet near
{: #transcendence-and-nearness }

> Nothing is like Him; He is the Hearing, the Seeing. [Qur'an 42:11](https://quran.com/42:11), translation prepared for this discussion.
>
> <div dir="rtl" lang="ar" class="quran-arabic">لَيْسَ كَمِثْلِهِ شَيْءٌ وَهُوَ السَّمِيعُ الْبَصِيرُ</div>
{: .quote }

> When My servants ask you about Me, I am near. I answer the caller's prayer when they call upon Me. [Qur'an 2:186](https://quran.com/2:186), translation of an excerpt prepared for this discussion.
>
> <div dir="rtl" lang="ar" class="quran-arabic">وَإِذَا سَأَلَكَ عِبَادِي عَنِّي فَإِنِّي قَرِيبٌ أُجِيبُ دَعْوَةَ الدَّاعِ إِذَا دَعَانِ</div>
{: .quote }

The first passage denies any likeness between God and creation; the second makes prayer intimate and immediate. [50:16](https://quran.com/50:16) places knowledge of the soul's inward whispering alongside nearness greater than the jugular vein. Exegetes explain that nearness in different ways, including divine knowledge and the proximity of God's angels; it should not be reduced to God occupying a location inside the body. [Ibn Kathir on 50:16](https://quran.com/50:16/tafsirs/en-tafisr-ibn-kathir).

The combination is powerful: the Creator is beyond comparison, yet no hidden grief, thought, or plea is outside His knowledge. Transcendence does not make Him inaccessible, and nearness does not make Him a creature.

#### Repentance and direct access to God
{: #direct-access-to-god }

A person can turn directly to the Creator. Adam receives words of repentance and is forgiven (2:37); every person bears responsibility for their own wrongdoing (53:38–39); those who have greatly wronged themselves are told to return rather than despair (39:53–54). Neither ancestry nor wealth secures nearness to God (34:37). [Qur'an 2:37](https://quran.com/2:37), [53:38–39](https://quran.com/53:38-39), and [34:37](https://quran.com/34:37).

Within this account, forgiveness does not require inherited guilt to be transferred, God to become incarnate, membership in a favored ethnicity, or a sacramental priesthood to authorize repentance. Prophets teach and guide, but God's answer to sincere prayer is not theirs to grant or withhold. Asking another person to pray for oneself is a different matter; it does not make that person an indispensable gatekeeper to God's mercy.

Direct access also carries responsibility. Repentance is joined to faith, changed conduct, and making amends where others have been wronged. It is an invitation to return and reform, rather than a way to declare harm inconsequential. [Qur'an 4:110](https://quran.com/4:110), [5:39](https://quran.com/5:39), and [25:70–71](https://quran.com/25:70-71).

#### A continuous account of human purpose
{: #human-purpose }

The Qur'an connects creation, moral choice, temptation, worship, suffering, death, resurrection, and judgment within one account of life. Human beings receive life as a gift, are shown a path, and are answerable for their response. Worship acknowledges the Giver and forms the worshipper; God has no need that human devotion must supply. Death ends the present opportunity to act, while resurrection brings actions and their consequences into the light. [Qur'an 76:2–3](https://quran.com/76:2-3), [51:56–58](https://quran.com/51:56-58), [67:2](https://quran.com/67:2), and [99:6–8](https://quran.com/99:6-8).

This makes ordinary choices significant: how someone treats a parent, handles money, responds to anger, keeps a promise, or helps a stranger belongs to the same moral life as prayer. Human choices are real and accountable within God's sovereignty; Islamic theologians have developed differing explanations of how those truths relate. The Qur'an's central practical demand remains clear: turn toward God and do good with the life entrusted to you.

#### Suffering and apparent injustice
{: #suffering-and-justice }

1. **Life tests people through comfort as well as hardship.** The Qur'an explicitly describes both good and evil circumstances as a trial (21:35). Wealth does not automatically signify divine approval, nor deprivation divine contempt; chapter 89 rejects those assumptions and turns attention to treatment of orphans and the poor. [Qur'an 21:35](https://quran.com/21:35) and [89:15–20](https://quran.com/89:15-20).

1. **Human knowledge is limited.** A person may dislike something that brings good or desire something that brings harm (2:216). This is a reason for humility about the whole course of events, not a claim to know the hidden purpose of every tragedy. The Qur'an also recognizes suffering caused by human wrongdoing and demands that injustice be confronted. [Qur'an 2:216](https://quran.com/2:216), [30:41](https://quran.com/30:41), and [4:75](https://quran.com/4:75).

1. **Justice extends beyond this life.** The apparent prosperity of oppressors does not mean their actions are forgotten (14:42). God promises exact justice, reward for patience, and an enduring life in which suffering is not the final condition of those received into mercy (21:47; 39:10; 35:34–35). The afterlife therefore supplies the completion that earthly experience visibly lacks. [Qur'an 14:42](https://quran.com/14:42), [21:47](https://quran.com/21:47), [39:10](https://quran.com/39:10), and [35:34–35](https://quran.com/35:34-35).

This is a coherent theological response to suffering, whose force depends on the reasons for believing in God and resurrection. It supports patience, hope, relief of suffering, and resistance to injustice together; it does not explain away grief or license indifference to it.

### <span class="num-roman">XIII.</span> Guidance, Law, and Morality
{: #guidance-law-and-morality }

The Qur'an presents its guidance as the renewal and completion of earlier revelation. The theology described above gives unity to its practical demands: mercy, repentance, justice, responsibility, and care for other people.

1. The Qur'an repeatedly presents mercy as a defining attribute of God. Every chapter but one opens with the invocation, **"In the name of God, the Most Compassionate, the Most Merciful."** Muslims are taught to say these words before beginning their actions, so mercy frames not only the Qur'an but the rhythm of everyday life.

    > "My mercy encompasses all things." (**Sūrat al-Aʿrāf 7:156**), Saheeh International
    >
    > <div dir="rtl" lang="ar" class="quran-arabic">﴿وَرَحْمَتِى وَسِعَتْ كُلَّ شَىْءٍ﴾ <span class="ayah-ref">[سُورَةُ الأَعۡرَافِ: ١٥٦]</span></div>
    {: .quote }


1. The Qur'an keeps forgiveness open for every sin, no matter how far a person has gone, and tells people never to despair of God's mercy.

    > "Say, 'O My servants who have transgressed against themselves [by sinning], do not despair of the mercy of God. Indeed, God forgives all sins. Indeed, it is He who is the Forgiving, the Merciful.'" (**Sūrat al-Zumar 39:53**), Saheeh International
    >
    > <div dir="rtl" lang="ar" class="quran-arabic">﴿۞ قُلْ يَـٰعِبَادِىَ ٱلَّذِينَ أَسْرَفُوا۟ عَلَىٰٓ أَنفُسِهِمْ لَا تَقْنَطُوا۟ مِن رَّحْمَةِ ٱللَّهِ ۚ إِنَّ ٱللَّهَ يَغْفِرُ ٱلذُّنُوبَ جَمِيعًا ۚ إِنَّهُۥ هُوَ ٱلْغَفُورُ ٱلرَّحِيمُ ۝٥٣﴾ <span class="ayah-ref">سُورَةُ الزُّمَرِ</span></div>
    {: .quote }


1. The Qur'an rejects inherited guilt: nobody is born owing another person's moral debt, and no soul bears another's burden.

    > "That no bearer of burdens will bear the burden of another. And that there is not for man except that [good] for which he strives." (**Sūrat al-Najm 53:38-39**), Saheeh International
    >
    > <div dir="rtl" lang="ar" class="quran-arabic">﴿أَلَّا تَزِرُ وَازِرَةٌ وِزْرَ أُخْرَىٰ ۝٣٨ وَأَن لَّيْسَ لِلْإِنسَـٰنِ إِلَّا مَا سَعَىٰ ۝٣٩﴾ <span class="ayah-ref">سُورَةُ النَّجۡمِ</span></div>
    {: .quote }

1. It emphasizes genuine character over hollow ritual: worship that leaves a person cruel is rejected.
    > "Righteousness is not in turning your faces towards the east or the west. Rather, the righteous are those who believe in God, the Last Day, the angels, the Books, and the prophets; who give charity out of their cherished wealth to relatives, orphans, the poor, ˹needy˺ travellers, beggars, and for freeing captives; who establish prayer, pay alms-tax, and keep the pledges they make; and who are patient in times of suffering, adversity, and in ˹the heat of˺ battle. It is they who are true ˹in faith˺, and it is they who are mindful ˹of God˺." (**Sūrat al-Baqarah 2:177**), Dr. Mustafa Khattab, *The Clear Quran*

1. It commands honesty, keeping one's word, humility, patience, and restraint in anger, while forbidding backbiting, mockery, contempt, and suspicion of others.

1. Care for others—especially the weak and dependent—is treated as a duty across relationships: spouses, children, parents in old age, relatives, orphans, the poor, travellers, debtors, captives, servants, and those in one's charge.

1. It makes the poor's share of wealth an obligation rather than a favour. Alms are a due; an insolvent debtor must be given time, and forgiving the debt is commended; interest is banned; and charity followed by humiliation loses its moral worth.

1. It equates neglect of the orphan and poor as rejection of the religion itself.

1. It treats one life as standing for all of humanity: to kill a person unjustly is as though one had killed everyone, and to save one is as though one had saved everyone.

#### Detailed protections, with conscience built into law
{: #legal-sophistication }

The Qur'an turns moral principles into concrete protections. Its longest verse addresses deferred debts through writing, witnesses, accurate recording, and protection from harm for scribes and witnesses. It even provides for a person unable to dictate their own obligation. The following verse joins trust in practical dealings to accountability before God. [Qur'an 2:282–283](https://quran.com/2:282-283).

| **Debts and trade** | Record deferred obligations; use witnesses; protect scribes; give an insolvent debtor time and commend remission of the debt (2:280–283). | Reduce disputes while preventing a creditor's claim from erasing compassion. |
| **Inheritance** | Recognize both women and men as heirs and specify shares for family relationships (4:7; 4:11–12; 4:176). | Constrain arbitrary exclusion and distribute obligations and entitlements through rules. Their detailed application belongs to the developed discipline of inheritance law. |
| **Orphan property** | Protect it from consumption, test readiness before transferring it, and witness its handover (4:2; 4:6; 4:10). | Treat guardianship as a trust owed to someone vulnerable. |
| **Divorce** | Regulate waiting periods, housing, maintenance, and witnessing, and prohibit retaining a spouse in order to cause harm (2:231; 65:1–7). | Subject separation to duties when emotion or power could otherwise dominate. |
| **Commercial honesty and testimony** | Demand fair measure and weight and justice even against oneself or close relatives (17:35; 4:135). | Make truth and the rights of others binding despite personal advantage. |
| **Treaties** | Require fulfillment of commitments and observance of treaties with parties who have kept them (5:1; 9:4). | Make agreements morally binding across communal boundaries. |

Read the provisions in [2:280–283](https://quran.com/2:280-283), [4:6–12](https://quran.com/4:6-12), [65:1–7](https://quran.com/65:1-7), and [9:4](https://quran.com/9:4). These examples demonstrate practical detail; they do not imply that every legal question is settled without interpretation or that other ancient societies lacked sophisticated law.

**Law is repeatedly joined to conscience.** The debt passage ends by reminding readers of God's knowledge (2:282); the next verse locates concealed testimony in a sinful heart (2:283). Rules about divorce are surrounded by reminders of mindfulness, trust, and God's knowledge (65:1–7). Formal compliance cannot exhaust the obligation when God also knows the intention behind it.

#### Progressive revelation as moral education
{: #progressive-pedagogy }

The Qur'an's law sometimes develops in stages. The passages traditionally understood as stages in the prohibition of intoxicants first identify harm greater than benefit (2:219), then prohibit approaching prayer while intoxicated (4:43), and finally command avoidance, identifying enmity, distraction from God, and neglect of prayer among the harms (5:90–91). [Qur'an 2:219](https://quran.com/2:219), [4:43](https://quran.com/4:43), and [5:90–91](https://quran.com/5:90-91).

This combines stable purposes with gradual formation: understanding the harm, changing an established habit, and accepting a binding rule. A report from Aisha likewise explains that early revelation first spoke about Paradise and Hell before detailed prohibitions were imposed. [Sahih al-Bukhari 4993](https://sunnah.com/bukhari:4993).

The Qur'an itself answers the demand for a single delivery by referring to strengthening the messenger's heart (25:32). It also acknowledges replacement of instructions within revelation (2:106; 16:101), a subject whose extent and interpretation scholars debate. Continuity therefore concerns foundational theology and moral purposes, not an assertion that every legal instruction was identical throughout the mission. [Qur'an 25:32](https://quran.com/25:32), [2:106](https://quran.com/2:106), and [16:101](https://quran.com/16:101).

#### From tribal privilege to universal responsibility
{: #universal-moral-identity }

The declaration that peoples and tribes exist so that they may know one another (49:13) changes the basis of worth before God. Belonging remains real, but ancestry, wealth, and political success do not settle moral standing. Believing women and men are placed in parallel before the same promise of forgiveness and reward (33:35), and the rule of justice applies even when it cuts against one's own family or people (4:135; 5:8). [Qur'an 49:13](https://quran.com/49:13), [33:35](https://quran.com/33:35), and [4:135](https://quran.com/4:135).

The transformation is a moral demand, not a claim that every Muslim society has always fulfilled it. A person's ultimate dignity and accountability cannot be reduced to inherited rank. That supplies a standard by which tribal arrogance and communal favoritism can themselves be criticized.

### <span class="num-roman">XIV.</span> Moral Psychology and the Formation of Character
{: #moral-psychology }

The Qur'an examines how people avoid truths they find uncomfortable. It describes motives, habits, and social pressures that can separate what someone knows from how they live. Its portraits invite readers to recognize those tendencies in themselves.

#### How people deceive themselves
{: #self-deception }

| Recurring tendency | Qur'anic diagnosis |
| --- | --- |
| **Pride and status** | A person transgresses when imagining themselves self-sufficient (96:6–7). The owner of the two gardens treats wealth as evidence that his security will last (18:34–36). |
| **Envy and resentment** | Joseph's brothers compare their father's affection, plan wrongdoing, and reassure themselves that they can become righteous afterward (12:8–9). Moral repair becomes a future excuse for present harm. |
| **Conformity** | People defend inherited practices because they found their ancestors following them, even when their ancestors lacked understanding (2:170). Familiarity substitutes for examination. |
| **Fear of poverty and attachment to possessions** | Fear of deprivation can obstruct generosity (2:268); rivalry for accumulation distracts people until they reach the graves (102:1–2). |
| **Hypocrisy and rationalization** | People who cause corruption describe themselves as reformers (2:11–12); others think they are doing well while their efforts are misdirected (18:103–104). A flattering self-description can survive destructive conduct. |
| **Crisis devotion and comfortable forgetfulness** | A person calls upon God in distress, then passes on as though they had never called when relief comes (10:12). Need exposes a dependence that comfort conceals. |
| **Ingratitude, despair, and boastfulness** | Loss of a blessing can lead to hopelessness and ingratitude; relief can lead to boastful assurance that trouble is over (11:9–10). The following verse commends patience and righteous action (11:11). |

See [12:8–9](https://quran.com/12:8-9), [2:170](https://quran.com/2:170), [2:268](https://quran.com/2:268), [18:103–104](https://quran.com/18:103-104), [10:12](https://quran.com/10:12), and [11:9–11](https://quran.com/11:9-11).

These are morally searching descriptions, not modern clinical diagnoses. Their force is recognizable: envy disguises itself as fairness, appetite borrows the language of reason, and wrongdoing can be defended as a contribution to the good.

#### How actions change perception
{: #actions-and-the-heart }

The Qur'an also describes wrongdoing as something that changes the person doing it. Repeated acts leave a stain upon the heart (83:14); broken commitments are associated with hardening (5:13); forgetting God becomes a kind of self-forgetfulness (59:19). Denial is therefore not always presented as a detached intellectual mistake. It can become a habit sustained by what a person wants to protect. [Qur'an 83:14](https://quran.com/83:14), [5:13](https://quran.com/5:13), and [59:19](https://quran.com/59:19).

The movement can also be reversed. Those conscious of God recover awareness through remembrance when temptation touches them (7:201). Sincere repentance and good action reopen the future (25:70–71). The repeated call to examine the heart connects knowledge with honesty, attention, and practice. It should lead first to self-examination, not to the assumption that everyone who disagrees is insincere. [Qur'an 7:201](https://quran.com/7:201) and [25:70–71](https://quran.com/25:70-71).

#### Worship as practical moral formation
{: #worship-and-character }

The Qur'an gives virtues repeated forms of practice. It connects prayer with remembrance and restraint from wrongdoing (20:14; 29:45), fasting with consciousness of God (2:183), and charitable giving with purification (9:103). Pilgrimage joins worship to disciplined conduct and shared remembrance, while expressly rejecting boasting about ancestry (2:197–200). [Qur'an 20:14](https://quran.com/20:14), [29:45](https://quran.com/29:45), [2:183](https://quran.com/2:183), [9:103](https://quran.com/9:103), and [2:197–200](https://quran.com/2:197-200).

In the prophetic practice through which these commands are lived, the five daily prayers repeatedly interrupt ordinary pursuits; fasting trains restraint of appetite; obligatory charity confronts attachment to wealth; and pilgrimage gathers people of different backgrounds into shared rites. The five-prayer schedule and the detailed rites come through the Prophet's ﷺ teaching and practice, rather than a complete ritual manual in the Qur'an alone. [Sahih al-Bukhari 46](https://sunnah.com/bukhari:46).

These disciplines are intended to form character, not certify it automatically. Chapter 107 condemns worship joined to showmanship and neglect of others. The Qur'an thus supplies both the practices and the standard by which their moral fruit should be judged. [Qur'an 107](https://quran.com/107).

### <span class="num-roman">XV.</span> Historical Impact and Transformation
{: #historical-impact-and-transformation }

1. Within a single generation, the vast majority of Arabs accepted it, including tribes that had fought hardest to suppress it.
1. It has shaped the lives of billions across fourteen centuries. Around two billion Muslims today accept it as scripture.
    > *Pew Research Center*, June 2025: the global Muslim population grew from
    > 1.7 billion in 2010 to 2.0 billion in 2020, reaching 26% of the world's population.
    {: .src }

1. Taken together, the Qur'an's teachings form a complete moral and legal framework that holds justice and mercy in balance and historically guided a flourishing civilization. Its guidance remains directly applicable: what it addressed to seventh-century Arabs still addresses readers today.

1. It gave rise to the second-largest religion on earth, after Christianity—its own predecessor—and the fastest-growing of the major religions.
    > *Pew Research Center* projects that Muslims and Christians will approach numerical parity by 2050, at roughly 2.8 billion and 2.9 billion respectively.
    {: .src }

1. The Prophet and the first hearers of the Qur'an remained committed through thirteen years of persecution in Mecca—mockery, beatings, starvation, boycott, and exile. Renouncing it would have ended their suffering, but they did not.

1. The Qur'an became foundational to Arabic grammar, lexicography, rhetoric, calligraphy, law, theology, philosophy, devotional life, and education.
    > The Qur'an became a foundational reference for the sciences of Arabic grammar, lexicography, rhetoric, and recitation, shaping the formal study of the language for centuries.
    {: .gloss }

1. Its historical influence is extraordinary.
    > A seventh-century Arabic revelation became simultaneously a memorized liturgy, a foundational legal and theological source, a central literary reference point, and a continuously transmitted scripture across peoples, languages, continents, and fourteen centuries.
    {: .gloss }

### <span class="num-roman">XVI.</span> The Qur'an and the Messenger Who Proclaimed It
{: #quran-and-messenger }

The relationship between the Qur'an and the Messenger ﷺ is itself one of the clearest signs that he was transmitting a revelation rather than composing a book of his own.

1. The Qur'an repeatedly presents Prophet Muhammad ﷺ as subject to the revelation rather than in control of it.
    > “When Our clear revelations are recited to them, those who do not expect to meet Us say ˹to the Prophet˺, ‘Bring us a different Qur'an or make some changes in it.’ Say, ‘It is not for me to change it on my own. I only follow what is revealed to me.’” (**Sūrat Yūnus 10:15**), Dr. Mustafa Khattab, *The Clear Quran*
    >
    > <div dir="rtl" lang="ar" class="quran-arabic">﴿وَإِذَا تُتْلَىٰ عَلَيْهِمْ ءَايَاتُنَا بَيِّنَـٰتٍ ۙ قَالَ ٱلَّذِينَ لَا يَرْجُونَ لِقَآءَنَا ٱئْتِ بِقُرْءَانٍ غَيْرِ هَـٰذَآ أَوْ بَدِّلْهُ ۚ قُلْ مَا يَكُونُ لِىٓ أَنْ أُبَدِّلَهُۥ مِن تِلْقَآئِ نَفْسِىٓ ۖ إِنْ أَتَّبِعُ إِلَّا مَا يُوحَىٰٓ إِلَىَّ ۖ إِنِّىٓ أَخَافُ إِنْ عَصَيْتُ رَبِّى عَذَابَ يَوْمٍ عَظِيمٍ ۝١٥﴾ <span class="ayah-ref">سُورَةُ يُونُسَ</span></div>
    {: .quote }

1. It contains an extraordinary threat against the messenger himself were he to fabricate revelation.
    > “Had the Messenger made up something in Our Name, We would have certainly seized him by his right hand, then severed his aorta, and none of you could have shielded him ˹from Us˺!” (**Sūrat al-Ḥāqqah 69:44–47**), Dr. Mustafa Khattab, *The Clear Quran*
    >
    > <div dir="rtl" lang="ar" class="quran-arabic">﴿وَلَوْ تَقَوَّلَ عَلَيْنَا بَعْضَ ٱلْأَقَاوِيلِ ۝٤٤ لَأَخَذْنَا مِنْهُ بِٱلْيَمِينِ ۝٤٥ ثُمَّ لَقَطَعْنَا مِنْهُ ٱلْوَتِينَ ۝٤٦ فَمَا مِنكُم مِّنْ أَحَدٍ عَنْهُ حَـٰجِزِينَ ۝٤٧﴾ <span class="ayah-ref">سُورَةُ الحَاقَّةِ</span></div>
    {: .quote }

1. It permanently records public corrections of him.
    > In Sūrat ʿAbasa 80:1–10 he is corrected for turning away from a blind believer while addressing influential Meccan leaders. In 9:43 he is questioned for granting permission too readily. In 66:1 he is asked why he prohibited for himself what God had made lawful. In 18:23–24 he is corrected regarding speaking confidently about what he would do the next day.
    {: .gloss }
    > These corrections were not hidden in private records. They became part of the Qur'an itself, recited publicly in worship for all time. The man accused of inventing the revelation faithfully transmitted verses that corrected and disciplined him before his own followers.
    {: .gloss }

1. The Qur'an is strikingly non-autobiographical. Muhammad ﷺ is the human messenger through whom the entire text comes, yet the Qur'an does not read like his memoir.
    > It gives no continuous account of his childhood, business career, marriage to Khadijah, genealogy, or most of the events later biographies consider central. His name Muhammad appears at four places (3:144; 33:40; 47:2; 48:29), with Ahmad [the praised one] in 61:6. He is also addressed by titles and pronouns, so the name count is not a count of his total presence. Nevertheless, the center of gravity remains God, earlier prophets, judgment, moral responsibility, and the community's response.
    {: .gloss }

1. The first Muslim community is not portrayed as an unblemished heroic group.
    > The Qur'an preserves disobedience and collapse at Uḥud (3:152–155), panic and misplaced confidence at Ḥunayn (9:25), hypocrisy, desertion, quarrels, and failures of discipline. A purely triumphalist founding document could have erased or softened much of this material; the Qur'an instead makes communal failure part of permanent moral instruction.
    {: .gloss }

1. Personal events enter the text but are repeatedly universalized.
    > The Qur'an consoles the Prophet ﷺ, addresses his household, responds to accusations, and comments on political and military crises. The remarkable feature is therefore not that his circumstances leave no trace. It is that personal and contingent events are repeatedly reframed into principles meant to outlive the event that triggered them.
    {: .gloss }

#### Revelations that restrict or burden the messenger
{: #revelation-and-strategic-interest }

The relationship goes beyond rebukes. The text places limits on what the Prophet ﷺ can claim and on the advantages his community may seek.

1. **It denies independent supernatural control.** He is directed to say that he does not possess God's treasures or knowledge of the unseen by his own power (6:50), and that he cannot independently secure benefit or avert harm even for himself (7:188). These disclaimers resist the image of a leader who can promise whatever his audience demands. [Qur'an 6:50](https://quran.com/6:50) and [7:188](https://quran.com/7:188).
1. **It refuses to make spectacular demands the terms of belief.** When opponents demand a spring, gardens, a house of gold, or an ascent into heaven, the instructed answer identifies him as a human messenger (17:90–93). The passage does not deny every miracle; it denies that he must manufacture the requested spectacle on command. [Qur'an 17:90–93](https://quran.com/17:90-93).
1. **It imposes demanding worship and limits expectations of worldly comfort.** The messenger is summoned to sustained night prayer in preparation for a weighty message (73:1–5). His household is presented with a choice between worldly enjoyment with an honorable release and commitment to God, His messenger, and the life to come (33:28–29). [Qur'an 73:1–5](https://quran.com/73:1-5) and [33:28–29](https://quran.com/33:28-29).
1. **It binds the community to duties that can run against immediate advantage.** Justice remains obligatory even against oneself or family, and hatred does not authorize injustice (4:135; 5:8). A revelation that claims authority over its followers also makes their conduct answerable to standards they do not control. [Qur'an 4:135](https://quran.com/4:135) and [5:8](https://quran.com/5:8).

Such passages fit the picture of a messenger under an authority greater than himself. Their force depends on the wider evidence: sincere human reformers can also accept burdens, and self-restraint can serve credibility. They are substantial parts of the comparison, not automatic disproof of every possible human motive.

#### Particular events become lasting instruction
{: #incidents-and-enduring-principles }

The Qur'an repeatedly draws enduring duties from immediate circumstances. The accusation discussed in chapter 24 becomes instruction about evidence, rumor, charitable assumptions, and reputational harm (24:11–20). The defeat at Uḥud becomes reflection on discipline, attachment to worldly gain, and perseverance (3:152–155). A woman's complaint about her marriage opens a passage affirming that God has heard her and regulating the practice that harmed her (58:1–4). [Qur'an 24:11–20](https://quran.com/24:11-20), [3:152–155](https://quran.com/3:152-155), and [58:1–4](https://quran.com/58:1-4).

Names and background details are often left to transmitted accounts, while the text preserves what future readers must learn. Historical context remains necessary for careful interpretation, but the discourse repeatedly reaches beyond the original incident to recognizable human responsibilities.

### <span class="num-roman">XVII.</span> Literary Appraisals and Modern Scholarship
{: #outside-appraisals }

The Qur'an's extraordinary character has not been recognized only by Muslims. Arabists, translators, historians, Christian scholars, and major European literary figures who approached the Qur'an from outside the Muslim tradition have repeatedly remarked on its unique literary form, rhetorical power, beauty, influence, and resistance to translation. Their judgments are especially striking because many of these writers did not accept the Qur'an's claim to divine revelation, and some were openly critical of Islam in other respects.

1. **E. H. Palmer**, Cambridge Arabist and translator of the Qur'an for Oxford's *Sacred Books of the East*, wrote that:

   > “the best of Arab writers has never succeeded in producing anything equal in merit to the Qur'an itself”

   Palmer argued that later attempts at the Qur'anic style appeared laboured beside its spontaneous force. Elsewhere in the same introduction he described early Qur'anic passages as “curt, grand, and often almost sublime” and its language as universally acknowledged as a highly perfected form of Arabic speech.

   > E. H. Palmer, trans., *The Qur'ân*, *Sacred Books of the East*, vols. 6 and 9, Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1880, Introduction.
   > {: .src }

2. **George Sale**, whose 1734 English translation remained enormously influential in Europe for generations, wrote despite his explicitly Christian criticism of Islam:

   > “The style of the Korân is generally beautiful and fluent”

   and described passages concerning God's majesty and attributes as **“sublime and magnificent.”** He further observed that the Qur'an's harmony of expression and rhetorical power helped account for the extraordinary effect it produced upon its first hearers.

   > George Sale, *The Koran, Commonly Called the Alcoran of Mohammed*, 1734, Preliminary Discourse, §III.
   > {: .src }

3. **H. A. R. Gibb**, one of the twentieth century's leading Western scholars of Arabic and Islam, wrote:

   > “As a literary monument the Koran thus stands by itself”

   calling it a production **unique in Arabic literature**, without a predecessor or successor in its particular idiom. He also emphasized the extraordinary freedom and originality with which Qur'anic rhetoric exploits the resources of Arabic syntax.

   > H. A. R. Gibb, *Arabic Literature: An Introduction*, Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1963, p. 36.
   > {: .src }

4. **Arthur J. Arberry**, Cambridge professor of Arabic and one of the most respected English translators of the Qur'an, said that its rhythms establish its:

   > “undeniable claim to rank amongst the greatest literary masterpieces of mankind.”

   Arberry deliberately structured his translation around the Qur'an's changing rhythms because he believed previous translations had failed to convey the rhetorical character of the Arabic original. Oxford University Press describes the Qur'an in connection with his translation as the **“supreme classic of Arabic literature”** and “a unique fusion” of prose and poetry.

   > Arthur J. Arberry, *The Koran Interpreted*, Introduction, 1955.
   > {: .src }

5. **Alfred Guillaume**, Christian Arabist and professor of Arabic and Islamic studies, wrote that the Qur'an:

   > “has a rhythm of peculiar beauty and a cadence that charms the ear.”

   He noted the admiration of Christian Arabs for its style and the widespread recognition of its excellence among Arabists, concluding that within the vast literature of Arabic there is **“nothing to compare with it.”**

   > Alfred Guillaume, *Islam*, Penguin Books, 1954, pp. 73–74.
   > {: .src }

6. **John Alden Williams**, professor of religion and a specialist in Islamic civilization, was equally emphatic about what is lost outside the Arabic original. He wrote:

   > “no translation, however faithful to the meaning, has ever been fully successful.”

   He described Qur'anic Arabic as, by turns, **“striking, soaring, vivid, terrible, tender and breathtaking.”** This is precisely why literary judgments based only on an English rendering can miss much of what Arabic readers are evaluating.

   > John Alden Williams, *Islam*, George Braziller.
   > {: .src }

7. **Neal Robinson**, Qur'anic scholar and Anglican priest, has devoted extensive study to features once dismissed by some Western critics as irregularities. Examining the Qur'an's abrupt shifts of grammatical person and perspective, he concluded that they:

   > “are a very effective rhetorical device.”

   His broader structural analysis likewise argues that apparently non-linear passages can form a **forceful cumulative argument**, reinforced by recurring rhythm and rhyme.

   > Neal Robinson, *Discovering the Qur'an: A Contemporary Approach to a Veiled Text*, 2nd ed., Georgetown University Press, 2003.
   > {: .src }

8. **Émile Dermenghem**, the French historian and biographer of Prophet Muhammad ﷺ, drew particular attention to the effect of Qur'anic recitation. In the English translation of *The Life of Mahomet*, he is cited describing:

   > “Its literary beauty, its irradiation, an enigma even today”

   and remarking on its power to produce religious fervour even in people who are otherwise not especially devout.

   > Émile Dermenghem, *The Life of Mahomet*, trans. Arabella Yorke, London: George Routledge, 1930, p. 249.
   > {: .src }

9. **Edward Gibbon**, the great Enlightenment historian and a famously skeptical critic of organized religion, nevertheless wrote in *The History of the Decline and Fall of the Roman Empire*:

   > “the Koran is a glorious testimony to the unity of God.”

   He particularly admired the uncompromising conception of one eternal and transcendent Creator that runs throughout it.

   > Edward Gibbon, *The History of the Decline and Fall of the Roman Empire*, ch. 50.
   > {: .src }

10. **Thomas Carlyle** is an especially revealing case because he did **not** enjoy reading Sale's English translation of the Qur'an and criticized it harshly. Yet after working through it, he still concluded:

    > “Sincerity, in all senses, seems to me the merit of the Koran”

    and acknowledged within it **“a vein of true direct insight”** approaching poetry. Carlyle's reluctant concession is arguably more significant than uncomplicated praise: despite his aversion to the translated literary form, he found something powerful and genuine running through the text.

    > Thomas Carlyle, *On Heroes, Hero-Worship, and the Heroic in History*, Lecture II, “The Hero as Prophet,” 1841.
    > {: .src }

11. **Johann Wolfgang von Goethe**, one of the greatest figures in German literature, studied the Qur'an repeatedly and had a complex attitude toward it. Although he criticized its repetitions, he also described its style as:

    > “stern, grand, terrible ... truly sublime”

    and wrote that repeated engagement with the book moves the reader from resistance to astonishment and ultimately reverence. He predicted that the Qur'an would continue exercising a powerful influence through the ages.

    > Johann Wolfgang von Goethe, *Notes and Essays for a Better Understanding of the West-Eastern Divan*, 1819.
    > {: .src }

The significance of these testimonies is that qualities central to the Muslim claim about the Qur'an have repeatedly forced themselves upon readers who did **not** begin from that belief. Across very different periods and intellectual backgrounds, outside observers have independently remarked upon its unique place in Arabic literature, its rhythm and rhetorical force, its unusual effect when recited, the difficulty of reproducing it in translation, its powerful proclamation of God's unity, and its extraordinary historical influence.

That agreement is particularly striking because these writers were not simply repeating Muslim devotional language. Sale, Palmer, Gibb, Guillaume, Robinson, Carlyle, Goethe, and others disagreed sharply with Islam on various questions. Yet even criticism repeatedly gave way to acknowledgment that the Qur'an was not an ordinary literary phenomenon.

#### Contemporary scholarship: specific findings
{: #modern-quranic-scholarship }

Modern scholarship adds something different from literary praise: sustained analysis of features that readers can investigate. The scholars below do not all share the same religious commitments or historical conclusions. Citing a finding does not make its author an advocate of this page's apologetic argument.

1. **Angelika Neuwirth — composition and discourse.** Her work examines the Qur'an's distinctive organization, its early emphasis on judgment, and its later engagement with interlocutors about monotheistic scripture. It treats the text as a subject for detailed literary and historical analysis. [“Structural, linguistic and literary features”](https://doi.org/10.1017/CCOL0521831601.006).
1. **William A. Graham and Navid Kermani — recitation and reception.** They explain why the audible, recited character of the Qur'an is essential to understanding its religious and aesthetic life. [“Recitation and aesthetic reception”](https://doi.org/10.1017/CCOL0521831601.007).
1. **Lara Harb — a precise account of eloquence.** Her study of classical Arabic poetics examines how syntactic arrangement and additional meanings became central to major theories of inimitability. The question concerns analyzable relationships in language, not merely admiration for ornament. [“Naẓm, Wonder, and the Inimitability of the Quran”](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/arabic-poetics/nazm-wonder-and-the-inimitability-of-the-quran/B980231F9936B751EF11029D69CF4554).
1. **Anne-Sylvie Boisliveau — the Qur'an's account of itself.** Her research studies the vocabulary and arguments by which the text describes revelation and establishes its claimed authority. [Interview on self-referentiality and argumentation](https://iqsaweb.org/2013/02/04/sr/).
1. **Nicolai Sinai — coherence and historical dating.** His work engages thematic interpretation and the date at which the standard consonantal text was established. His argument for early closure is a historical conclusion, not an endorsement of divine authorship. [Thematic coherence](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/reclaiming-islamic-tradition/reading-surat-alanam-with-muhammad-rashid-rida-and-sayyid-qusb/8431797630F199956F13CC01456B43B9); [early textual closure](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0041977X14000111).
1. **Marijn van Putten and Hythem Sidky — written and oral transmission.** Their studies investigate manuscript relationships and the interaction of script with reading traditions. These provide evidence for early transmission while also complicating simplified accounts of uniformity. [Van Putten on the written archetype](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0041977X19000338); [Sidky on consonantal dotting](https://doi.org/10.7817/jaos.143.4.2023.ar029).

The value of this work lies in its arguments and evidence. A scholar's recognition of a literary or historical feature should not be quoted as though it established the further theological inference on its own.

### <span class="num-roman">XVIII.</span> Testing Alternative Explanations
{: #alternative-quranic-authorship }

A cumulative argument must compare explanations. Calling the Qur'an remarkable does not settle who produced it, and rejecting one weak human-authorship theory does not establish revelation by elimination. The serious question is which explanation best accounts for the evidence as a whole, with the fewest unsupported assumptions.

#### What needs to be explained?
{: #authorship-evidence }

The relevant evidence includes the text's literary character, the traditional account of twenty-three years of oral proclamation, its recognizably continuous theology across changing circumstances, its engagement with earlier traditions, its distinct register from hadith, its public claim to revelation, corrections of its messenger, moral and legal program, predictions, and early transmission.

These points do not all have the same status. The wording and structure can be inspected directly. Manuscripts and inscriptions provide physical historical evidence. Details of revelation chronology and the Prophet's ﷺ biography depend on assessing transmitted reports. Whether a passage is unsurpassed, a prediction exceptionally unlikely, or the whole combination miraculous requires further argument. A fair comparison must preserve those distinctions.

| Explanation | What it could explain | What it must still account for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Extraordinary human genius** | A gifted speaker can create a distinctive style, draw powerful connections, teach persuasively, and develop a coherent worldview. Literacy is not a prerequisite for oral creativity. | The theory must explain this particular combination of qualities across the reported career, including constraints imposed on the messenger and any predictions securely established as preceding their outcomes. “Genius” names a capacity; detailed literary and historical analysis is needed to show how it explains the evidence. |
| **Adaptation of Jewish and Christian traditions** | Shared narratives and concepts are understandable in a region connected to wider religious cultures. Oral transmission can convey material without direct reading of its original language. | Specific proposals must identify plausible earlier forms, channels of contact, chronology, and the relationship between similarities and deliberate differences. Access to stories does not by itself explain their integration into the Qur'an's sustained Arabic discourse. |
| **A secret teacher or small circle of teachers** | Sustained instruction could account for particular knowledge or ideas. | The proposed teacher's identity, competence, access, duration of involvement, and connection to particular passages require evidence. The Qur'an records contemporary accusations of instruction (16:103; 25:4–5), but an accusation is not proof of its mechanism. Equally, the absence of a surviving confession is not proof that private instruction was impossible. |
| **Collaborative authorship** | A group could supply different kinds of expertise, and a principal speaker or editor could impose stylistic unity. | A historical case needs identifiable contributors, evidence of their activity, and an explanation of how the collaboration relates to public proclamation and the early text. Continuity of voice weighs against some versions of this theory, but cannot by itself exclude a coordinated group. |
| **Later editing or fabrication** | Editing can produce arrangement, coherence, and apparent predictions after events. | Extensive late reconstruction must overcome manuscripts descending from an early written standard and seventh-century documentary witnesses. Editing within the earliest period is a different hypothesis and must be assessed passage by passage. Early closure strongly constrains later fabrication without proving that every stage of collection is fully recoverable. |
| **Sincere religious experience expressed in human language** | A sincere preacher may understand powerful experiences as revelation without intending deception; moral seriousness alone does not distinguish that possibility from prophecy. | This account must engage with the content, historical claims, and alleged signs as well as the sincerity of the claimant. A psychiatric label is not an explanation that can be established from the text, nor does coherent leadership by itself rule out every mistaken religious belief. |

The manuscript constraints are discussed in [preservation and early closure](#early-textual-closure); the comparisons of speech in [the literary section](#arabic-comparisons); the relevant contemporary allegations in [the invitation to scrutiny](#reason-and-scrutiny). Several human mechanisms could operate together, so the alternatives need not be treated as mutually exclusive.

#### How would borrowing have worked?
{: #borrowing-hypothesis }

A historically useful borrowing theory should ask concrete questions:

1. **Which source?** Identify the relevant version of a story, teaching, or liturgical formula, establish that it predates the passage, and distinguish a close sequence of distinctive details from a broadly shared religious motif.
1. **Through which channel?** Direct use of a Syriac, Hebrew, Greek, or other written source requires evidence of access or mediation. An oral explanation requires a plausible setting for transmission. It is unnecessary to assume that every source language had to be mastered personally.
1. **Over what period?** The proposed route should fit the chronology and the range of material it is meant to explain. One reported encounter cannot simply be expanded into decades of instruction without supporting evidence.
1. **What explains the differences?** A theory must account for the Qur'an's selection, compression, theological disagreements, and repeated reworking of related narratives. Such transformation is relevant evidence, although human authors can also creatively adapt inherited material.

Modern discussion of late-antique traditions makes these questions more precise. It also means the argument should not rest on imagining Arabia sealed off from the wider world. The Qur'an itself claims continuity with earlier revelation; parallels are compatible with that claim, but must still be evaluated when offered as evidence of human dependence. [Tommaso Tesei, “Echoes of Pseudepigrapha in the Qur'ān”](https://doi.org/10.1017/9789048540105.009).

#### What would fabrication lead us to expect?
{: #fabrication-expectations }

If a particular fabrication theory assumes that its author primarily sought comfort, unrestricted authority, or personal glorification, several features call for explanation:

| Expectation under that motive | Evidence to examine |
| --- | --- |
| Remove embarrassing failures | Public corrections of the messenger and community remain in the text (80:1–10; 3:152–155). |
| Retain control over the message | The messenger denies permission to change it on his own (10:15). |
| Promise powers that impress an audience | He is directed to disclaim independent control over benefit, harm, and the unseen (7:188), and to answer demands for spectacular signs by identifying himself as a human messenger (17:90–93). |
| Make the author's life the book's center | The text repeatedly redirects attention to God, earlier prophets, moral responsibility, and judgment. |
| Avoid public commitments that could disappoint followers | It preserves the Roman prediction and the promise of secure entry into the Sacred Mosque, whose historical force must be tested against their dating (30:2–4; 48:27). |

These expectations concern specified motives. An ingenious fabricator could use self-criticism to gain credibility, and a sincere human author might willingly accept burdens. The force of the comparison is therefore cumulative: such replies must fit the broader life and text, rather than be invented separately whenever a difficulty arises.

#### Revelation as an explanation
{: #revelation-as-explanation }

The Qur'an's own explanation is that one divine source accounts for the unity of its proclamation, guidance, authority over its messenger, and signs. The Muslim case presented here is that this explanation fits the convergence more persuasively than its alternatives. It does not follow merely because a complete naturalistic reconstruction is unavailable. Its strength depends on the quality of the positive evidence, the credibility of belief in God, and fair comparison with the strongest available human explanations.

### <span class="num-roman">XIX.</span> The Cumulative Case
{: #quran-cumulative-case }

The case for the Qur'an's divine origin rests on the extraordinary convergence of its features. A text publicly proclaimed over a long and turbulent prophetic career:

- develops a highly distinctive and analyzable literary discourse;
- repeatedly integrates sound, syntax, structure, narrative, and meaning;
- reshapes repeated stories according to local rhetorical goals;
- openly argues with its opponents and invites scrutiny;
- repeatedly places its messenger beneath rather than above the revelation;
- permanently records corrections of that messenger and failures of the first Muslim community;
- makes historically risky claims about future events;
- enters the documentary record extremely early;
- is traceable through overlapping written and oral transmission channels;
- engages earlier religious traditions while maintaining a distinct theological program;
- develops a broad moral and epistemic worldview across changing circumstances;
- and remains a continuously recited and memorized scripture in its original language.

The Muslim argument is that this convergence is most convincingly explained by the Qur'an's own claim: revelation from God. The conclusion is a reasoned theological judgment built on the literary, historical, moral, and personal evidence; it is not a conclusion that manuscript dating or literary admiration establishes alone. The alternatives must be weighed against the same evidence and with the same attention to their assumptions.

#### How the lines of evidence reinforce one another
{: #independence-of-evidence }

The case becomes stronger when its parts are supported by different kinds of evidence and do different explanatory work. It becomes weaker if the same premise is counted repeatedly under new labels.

| Evidence | Contribution | Dependency to remember |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Manuscripts and material documents** | Establish early wording, distribution, and public use. | Manuscripts descended from one ancestor are related witnesses, not independent acts of composition. |
| **Recitation and teaching traditions** | Explain continuing transmission, correction, and the preservation of recognized readings. | Oral and written transmission interact; they cannot simply be multiplied as wholly independent proofs. |
| **Literary analysis** | Examines syntax, sound, structure, and the relation of form to meaning. | Rhyme, memorability, and emotional effect overlap; aesthetic judgments require informed comparison. |
| **Historical predictions** | Can support knowledge beyond ordinary expectation if timing and fulfillment are secure. | The date of proclamation and the independence of the evidence for fulfillment must be established. |
| **The Prophet's ﷺ conduct** | Bears on sincerity, motives, and his relationship to the message. | Biographical reports require assessment; a correction quoted from the Qur'an is still evidence from the Qur'an. |
| **Theology and moral guidance** | Show the scope, continuity, and coherence of the worldview. | Coherence and moral insight are evaluative arguments; their presence does not alone identify a supernatural source. |

Preservation establishes access to the object being examined. Literary analysis investigates that object's qualities. History tests claims about its setting and reception. Theology and the messenger's life inform the judgment about its source. Their convergence is the argument; there is no justified numerical probability obtained simply by multiplying the number of points on the page.

#### What would significantly weaken the case?
{: #what-would-weaken-the-case }

An invitation to examine the Qur'an should allow the evidence to change the assessment. The following findings, if convincingly established, would require substantial revision of the argument:

1. **A demonstrably late basic text, or extensive later rewriting.** Securely dated evidence that central parts of today's Qur'an were composed well after the prophetic period would undermine the claim that they preserve Muhammad's ﷺ proclamation. The already acknowledged early variants must be assessed for what they show, rather than hidden to protect a stronger claim.
1. **A demonstrated human production history.** Reliable evidence identifying sources, teachers, or collaborators and showing how the text was produced would materially strengthen a human-authorship explanation. A resemblance or uncorroborated accusation would not be enough.
1. **A persuasive answer to the literary challenge.** A serious Arabic work judged by the same stated criteria, with sustained critical comparison, would challenge claims of inimitability. It could not be dismissed simply because its author was an opponent or because its conclusion was unwelcome.
1. **A clear failed prediction.** A securely dated, unambiguous claim about an event that did not occur as stated would count against the text's claimed reliability. Ambiguous timing cannot be treated as precise evidence when favorable and ignored when unfavorable.
1. **A convincingly demonstrated contradiction or error.** The passages would need to make incompatible claims in the same relevant sense and context. Apparent tensions require honest examination of Arabic, genre, scope, and chronology; those considerations must not become a license to invent an escape from any difficulty.

If an individual numerical, scientific, literary, or historical argument fails, it should be corrected or withdrawn. The effect on the overall case depends on the importance of the failed premise and how much of the remaining argument depends on it. Some findings would weaken a supporting illustration; others would challenge a central claim of divine revelation.

## The Messenger of God ﷺ

Everything described above was recited by one man who could neither read nor write, over the course of twenty-three years in seventh-century Arabia. But the Qur'an cannot be separated from the man who delivered it.

The two lines of evidence therefore reinforce one another. The extraordinary qualities of the Qur'an came through a man whose character, life, and message were themselves extraordinary. Taken together, they point overwhelmingly toward the same conclusion: that Prophet Muhammad ﷺ was what he claimed to be—a true prophet of God.

His life presents a remarkable case of its own. The man through whom the Qur'an came was known for his integrity, endured years of persecution and sacrifice for his message, lived according to what he preached, and left behind teachings and an example that transformed Arabia and shaped the lives of generations after him. His life was also preserved in extraordinary detail and rigor through reports from his family, companions, servants, followers, and even opponents.

### Was Muhammad ﷺ Truly a Prophet?
{: #was-muhammad-truly-a-prophet }

Broadly speaking, there are five possible explanations for Prophet Muhammad ﷺ's life: He was either a conscious fraud, sincerely mistaken, inspired by an evil source, turned into a prophet by later legend, or he was what he claimed to be: a messenger of God. His life, message, and the historical record make the first four explanations increasingly difficult to sustain.

### <span class="num-roman">I.</span> The Fraud Hypothesis
{: #the-fraud-hypothesis }

Fraud usually serves some gain: wealth, status, comfort, power, or admiration. Muhammad's mission brought him years of ridicule, isolation, persecution, and danger. He continued preaching when compromise would have been easier.

The Qur'an also preserves public corrections of him. It rebukes him for turning away from a blind man while speaking to Meccan leaders (Qur'an 80:1–10) and questions his decision to permit some men to stay behind from an expedition (9:43). A religious impostor controlling his own scripture would have little reason to preserve revelations that diminish his image before his followers.

Success did not turn him into a king. Early reports describe a simple household, periods of scarcity, and a life far removed from luxury. His conduct does not resemble a man who invented revelation to enrich or glorify himself.

The evidence on his motives, his household, and the Qur'an's corrections of him is collected under [the competing explanations](/messenger/claim), [what those closest to him saw](/messenger/character), and [the Qur'an he delivered](/messenger/quran).

### <span class="num-roman">II.</span> Sincere, but Mistaken
{: #sincere-but-mistaken }

A sincere delusion can produce conviction, but Muhammad's case involved more than conviction.

For twenty-three years, the Qur'an addressed theology, ethics, law, worship, family life, war and peace, earlier prophets, personal crises, and changing political circumstances while maintaining a stable religious core: worship God alone, live morally, prepare for judgment, defend the weak, repent, forgive, give charity, and resist pride.

Muhammad also showed sustained practical judgment. He negotiated treaties, resolved disputes, led a community, delegated authority, altered strategy when circumstances changed, and distinguished personal opinion from revelation. The message repeatedly imposed difficult duties on him, corrected him, and constrained his own preferences.

A supposed delusion that remained coherent across decades, accompanied effective leadership, and repeatedly disciplined the person experiencing it is possible in principle, but poorly fits the evidence.

For the range and consistency of the teaching, and for his conduct as a leader, see [worship and the inner life](/messenger/worship), [work and public responsibility](/messenger/work-and-authority), and [his life through hardship and power](/messenger/life).

### <span class="num-roman">III.</span> Devil-Inspired
{: #devil-inspired }

The Qur'an relentlessly condemns Satan, idolatry, arrogance, dishonesty, exploitation, sexual immorality, and injustice. It commands prayer, charity, humility, repentance, forgiveness, care for parents and orphans, moral accountability, and worship of the Creator alone.

An evil source would therefore have produced a movement centered on rejecting evil worship, restraining vice, resisting spiritual pride, condemning Satan, and directing human beings toward repentance and obedience to God. Such an explanation works against the moral and theological direction of the message itself.

What the message actually commands is set out at length under [worship and the inner life](/messenger/worship), [justice and restraint in conflict](/messenger/justice), and [care for the vulnerable and human dignity](/messenger/human-dignity).

### <span class="num-roman">IV.</span> A Later Fabricated Legend
{: #a-later-fabricated-legend }

The Qur'an was preserved through both public recitation and writing. It was memorized across the Muslim community, taught from teacher to student, recited in worship, and copied in written form. These overlapping channels limited the possibility of a later authority quietly replacing the text across geographically separated communities.

The Birmingham Qur'an folios preserve parts of Surahs 18–20 in an early Hijazi script. The parchment was radiocarbon dated to 568–645 CE with 95.4% probability, and the University of Birmingham places the manuscript in the mid-seventh century, within decades of Muhammad's death. Manuscript specialist Marijn van Putten has also argued that shared, highly specific spellings across numerous early Qur'an manuscripts point back to a single written archetype in the first Islamic century.

Modern critical historians likewise place Muhammad and the Qur'an firmly in the seventh century. Sean W. Anthony treats the Qur'an as the earliest and strongest literary witness to the historical Muhammad and draws on seventh-century inscriptions, documentary evidence, and non-Muslim writings that independently attest an Arabian prophetic movement associated with him. Gregor Schoeler's work supports an early culture in which reports about Muhammad circulated through both oral teaching and written notes.

Hadith transmission developed its own critical machinery. Reports circulated with named chains of transmitters, often through multiple routes. Scholars compared chains and textual variants, examined whether transmitters had met, assessed memory and honesty, compiled biographical dictionaries, and classified reports as strong, weak, or fabricated. Modern isnad-cum-matn analysis has shown that some traditions can be traced substantially earlier than the surviving canonical collections.

Muhammad's life and message are anchored in first-century scripture, manuscripts, inscriptions, outside testimony, and traceable streams of transmission. They do not first appear as a polished legend centuries later.

[Sources, transmission, and bibliography](/messenger/sources) sets out how the hadith record was preserved and tested; [Muhammad ﷺ and the Qur'an he delivered](/messenger/quran) covers the text's own preservation.

### <span class="num-roman">V.</span> The Prophetic Explanation
{: #the-prophetic-explanation }

The fraud theory conflicts with Muhammad's sacrifices, austerity, and the Qur'an's corrections of him. The delusion theory fits poorly with decades of coherent teaching, disciplined conduct, and effective leadership. The demonic theory runs against the message's sustained orientation toward God, repentance, justice, and moral reform. The legend theory is incompatible with the early documentary and transmission evidence.

Muhammad's own explanation remains: the source of the message was God.

His claim was not confined to a private experience or a brief burst of enthusiasm. He lived under it through persecution and victory, weakness and power, grief and public responsibility. His character, the content of the Qur'an, the structure of his mission, and the early preservation of the sources converge more naturally on sincere prophethood than on fraud, delusion, demonic inspiration, or later invention.

Belief in his prophethood can therefore rest on a cumulative historical and rational judgment: the prophetic explanation accounts for the evidence more coherently than its principal alternatives.

That cumulative case is laid out under [the competing explanations](/messenger/claim), with the harder-to-weigh evidence kept separate in [predictions](/messenger/predictions) and [reported miracles](/messenger/miracles). [Encounters, changed minds, and later appraisals](/messenger/encounters) collects what people who met him said.
