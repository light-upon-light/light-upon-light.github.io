Taken together, however, they form a cumulative case for the Qur'an's divine origin and the authenticity of the Prophet's mission.

The Qur'an and the life of Prophet Muhammad ﷺ together form a cumulative case for his prophethood.

The Qur'an was revealed orally over twenty-three years in seventh-century Arabia by a man who could neither read nor write. Its passages were revealed separately, often in response to unforeseen events and outside their final order, yet the Qur'an maintains unmatched eloquence, intricate structure, coherence, and depth throughout.

It openly states its claim to revelation, invites scrutiny, and challenges its opponents to rival it. Its fulfilled prophecies, historical and linguistic details, early manuscripts, and continuing recitation further support its divine origin. Its teaching about God gives unity to its account of repentance, human purpose, suffering, justice, and moral growth. Practical laws and regular worship turn that guidance into a way of life. Together, these features strengthen the case for revelation when compared with the alternatives.

The life of Prophet Muhammad ﷺ further strengthens that case. He was known for exceptional integrity, mercy, courage, and humility. He embodied the justice and generosity he taught, endured years of persecution rather than abandon his message, and lived simply even after gaining authority. His public leadership and private conduct reflected the same devotion to God and responsibility toward others. His teachings and example transformed Arabia and shaped billions of lives across fourteen centuries, making him one of history's most influential figures.

The main alternative explanations fail to account for the evidence of his life and message. Deliberate fraud does not explain his personal sacrifices or his faithful transmission of revelations that openly corrected him. Sincere error does not explain decades of coherent teaching, sound judgment, and effective leadership. Evil inspiration is incompatible with a message that calls people to worship God, reject Satan and evil, live morally, and resist wrongdoing. The claim that his story was fabricated later is contradicted by early manuscript evidence and by the extensive record of his life carefully preserved by the first generations of Muslims.

The case is therefore cumulative: the Qur'an's extraordinary qualities, the Prophet Muhammad's ﷺ moral character and lasting influence, and the weakness of alternative explanations all reinforce one another. The Qur'an is presented as a lasting and preserved miracle from God, given to Prophet Muhammad ﷺ as evidence for his message to all humanity. Together with the carefully preserved record of his life, they provide every generation with tangible evidence by which to examine the Qur'an's claim to divine origin and assess Muhammad's ﷺ claim to prophethood.

## The Book

The Qur'an presents a remarkable convergence of signs pointing to its divine origin. Its mode of revelation, literary character, intricate composition, fulfilled predictions, preservation, depth of guidance, and relationship with the Messenger ﷺ reinforce one another. Together, they form a powerful case for its claim to be revelation from God.

**The case is cumulative.** Remarkable qualities provide evidence to assess; calling the Qur'an miraculous is a further judgment about their source. That judgment rests on the strength of the established evidence, its historical setting, and comparison with alternative explanations. Each point contributes according to its significance and its relationship to the others.

*Translation note: English quotations use “God” for the Arabic divine name. New translations are identified, and the literary examples include the Arabic.*

### <span class="num-roman">I.</span> How the Qur'an Emerged
{: #mode-of-revelation }

1. The Qur'an was first proclaimed through oral recitation by Prophet Muhammad ﷺ, an unlettered man from seventh-century Mecca. The Qur'an describes his lack of prior reading and writing of scripture, and Islamic tradition records that he could neither read nor write.
    > The Qur'an stated this before people who had known the Prophet ﷺ throughout his life. His opponents had both the opportunity and the motive to challenge it if he had been known for reading or writing earlier scripture. The verse is quoted below.
    {: .gloss }

    > “You ˹O Prophet˺ could not read any writing ˹even˺ before this ˹revelation˺, nor could you write at all. Otherwise, the people of falsehood would have been suspicious.” (**Sūrat al-ʿAnkabūt 29:48**), Dr. Mustafa Khattab, *The Clear Quran*
    >
    > <div dir="rtl" lang="ar" class="quran-arabic">﴿وَمَا كُنتَ تَتْلُوا۟ مِن قَبْلِهِۦ مِن كِتَـٰبٍ وَلَا تَخُطُّهُۥ بِيَمِينِكَ ۖ إِذًا لَّٱرْتَابَ ٱلْمُبْطِلُونَ ۝٤٨﴾ <span class="ayah-ref">سُورَةُ العَنكَبُوتِ</span></div>
    {: .quote }

1. Its revelation unfolded over twenty-three years in separate portions, often in response to unforeseen questions and circumstances: persecution, migration, war, treaties, family disputes, legal questions, theological challenges, internal failures, victories, and defeats. The material was therefore not delivered from beginning to end as a prewritten book.

1. Its final arrangement brings passages from different occasions together within chapters. Islamic transmission reports describe the Prophet ﷺ directing their placement. Literary analysis examines the resulting composition, while historical reports establish the occasions and sequence of revelation.

1. Revelations were recited publicly, memorized by followers, used in prayer, and written down. The message developed under communal observation, with its words retained in people's memories and written records. This public transmission constrained the opportunity for secret rewriting. Collection and standardization are examined in the preservation section.
    > The Qur'an's literary quality and coherence should be considered alongside this history: revelation in separate portions, across changing circumstances, through public recitation. Its account of [gradual revelation](#progressive-pedagogy) also explains the development of particular instructions during the mission.
    {: .gloss }

#### Continuity across Mecca and Medina
{: #meccan-medinan-continuity }

The Qur'an maintains the same theological and moral foundations across the Meccan and Medinan periods. Muhammad ﷺ went from enduring persecution to leading a community responsible for treaties, conflict, family law, and public affairs. Throughout those changes, the message continued to demand worship of God alone, honesty, care for the vulnerable, and accountability for one's actions.

Meccan passages join divine oneness to duties toward parents, children, and orphans, fair measurement, and just speech (6:151–153). Medinan passages require justice even against oneself and one's family, and toward people one dislikes (4:135; 5:8). The community's growing authority brought further responsibilities under the same principles. [Qur'an 6:151–153](https://quran.com/6:151-153), [4:135](https://quran.com/4:135), and [5:8](https://quran.com/5:8).

This continuity concerns the Qur'an's core teaching. Its legislation developed as the community's circumstances changed; the dates assigned to individual passages depend on historical evidence.

### <span class="num-roman">II.</span> Recitation, Sound, and Memory
{: #heard-scripture }

The Qur'an was revealed to be recited and heard. It commands deliberate recitation and attentive listening, and its sound contributes directly to its meaning. [Qur'an 73:4](https://quran.com/73:4), [7:204](https://quran.com/7:204).

1. **Recitation guides understanding.** Questions prepare their answers, oaths introduce conclusions, and warnings lead into exceptions. Pauses, repeated endings, changes of pace, and shifts of address guide the listener through the argument. Hearing the Arabic reveals features that a written translation cannot fully convey.

1. **Repetition connects passages.** In chapter 55, the same question about denying God's favors follows descriptions of creation, judgment, and Paradise. Each return connects the new passage to the chapter's central demand: acknowledge God's gifts and respond with gratitude. [Qur'an 55](https://quran.com/55).

1. **Sound supports memorization and worship.** Rhythm and recurring patterns help preserve wording and sequence. The same passages are learned by ear, recited in prayer, checked against written copies, and corrected by teachers. These practices sustain the Qur'an's daily use across communities with different languages.

William A. Graham and Navid Kermani examine this central role of recitation in the Qur'an's literary and religious life. [“Recitation and aesthetic reception,” *The Cambridge Companion to the Qur'ān*](https://doi.org/10.1017/CCOL0521831601.007).

1. The Qur'an contains 114 chapters, filling about 600 pages in a common modern layout. Its literary qualities extend across a work of that scale.

1. The Qur'an belongs to neither ordinary prose nor metrical poetry, but sustains a distinctive Qur'anic mode of expression—previously unknown to Arabic—in which rhythm, rhyme, cadence, syntax, and shifts of register are fused with precision of meaning.

1. The Qur'an maintains a distinctive literary voice across twenty-three years of revelation and major changes in subject, audience, and circumstance. Meccan and Medinan passages vary in style and emphasis within that recognizable voice.

1. Major theorists of Qur'anic inimitability focused on **naẓm** [syntactic-semantic arrangement]: the precise relationships created by syntax, word order, omission, emphasis, definiteness and indefiniteness, conjunctions, grammatical person, expectation, context, rhythm, sound and meaning.
    > This gives the literary argument a more precise standard than simply saying that the Qur'an "sounds beautiful." In the mature Arabic rhetorical tradition associated especially with ʿAbd al-Qāhir al-Jurjānī, eloquence is produced by the way meanings are arranged through syntax and context. Modern historian of Arabic poetics Lara Harb likewise notes that medieval authors primarily located Qur'anic inimitability in sentence construction and the additional meanings conveyed through such features as omission, unusual word order, grammatical shifts, and definiteness.
    {: .gloss }
    > On this account, eloquence is not produced by collecting unusually ornate words. A common word can become extraordinary because of where it is placed, what is omitted around it, how the sentence is ordered, what the listener expects next, and how sound and meaning reinforce one another. This is one reason translation can communicate the basic meaning while losing much of the literary argument.
    {: .gloss }

1. The Qur'an repeatedly uses highly compressed rhetorical devices in which form contributes directly to meaning: deliberate omission, changes in expected word order, movement between first-, second-, and third-person address, parallelism, contrast, recurring formulas, and abrupt changes in sentence length or cadence. These grammatical shifts perform recognized rhetorical functions in Arabic, changing perspective, emphasis, immediacy, and emotional force.
    > One famous category is *iltifāt* [a rhetorical shift in grammatical person or mode of address]. M. A. S. Abdel Haleem has shown how these shifts function systematically for rhetorical purposes rather than simply being grammatical accidents.
    {: .gloss }
    > A simple example appears in the opening chapter: it begins by speaking **about** God—“Lord of all worlds ... Master of the Day of Judgment”—and then turns to speaking **to** God: “You alone we worship and You alone we ask for help.” The shift enacts a movement from knowledge and praise into direct worship and prayer.
    {: .gloss }
    > M. A. S. Abdel Haleem, ["Grammatical Shift for Rhetorical Purposes: Iltifāt and Related Features in the Qur'an"](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0041977X00003621), *Bulletin of the School of Oriental and African Studies* 55.3 (1992), 407–432.
    {: .src }

1. The classical doctrine of inimitability became one of the major engines behind the development of Arabic rhetoric and literary theory. Scholars such as al-Khaṭṭābī, al-Rummānī, al-Bāqillānī, and ʿAbd al-Qāhir al-Jurjānī did not merely repeat that the Qur'an was eloquent; they attempted to analyze what its language was doing and why imitation was difficult.
    > Modern studies of classical Arabic poetics likewise show how deeply Qur'anic inimitability shaped the development of Arabic rhetorical theory; see *The Oxford Handbook of Qur'anic Studies* and Lara Harb's *Arabic Poetics*.
    {: .src }

1. The Qur'an became one of the foundational authorities used by early grammarians and philologists when describing Arabic. Its language helped establish the very grammatical and rhetorical tradition by which classical Arabic came to be analyzed and taught.

1. **Repetition with variation:** the Qur'an retells events—especially episodes involving Moses—many times. Repeated stories are not simply copied verbatim: details, dialogue, vocabulary, and emphasis are reshaped to serve the argument of the chapter in which each retelling appears.

    > One retelling may expand a confrontation, another suppress it; one emphasizes fear, another prophetic reassurance; one places a speech before a sign, another highlights the sign itself. The same narrative material is repeatedly reshaped to serve the argument of the chapter in which it appears.
    {: .gloss }
    > Leyla Ozgur Alhassen's *How the Qur'ān Works: Reading Sacred Narrative* studies Qur'anic repetition through motifs, themes, action sequences, parallels, reversals, and deliberate suppression of repeated material.

1. **Compositional architecture:** The Qur’an repeatedly displays intricate large-scale organization across individual passages, entire chapters, and even groups of chapters. Themes, key words, images, and arguments are introduced, developed, echoed, contrasted, and later returned to in deliberate ways. Many chapters use patterns of framing, parallel sections, thematic symmetry, recurring key terms, verbal echoes, and carefully placed transitions to connect distant parts of the text. These patterns give the composition a strong internal unity that often becomes fully apparent only when the whole composition is examined.

1. **Ring composition** is one striking example: many verses and chapters display tightly balanced chiastic structures in which the beginning and end correspond and the central passage carries special emphasis.
    > This is especially remarkable given that the Qur’an was revealed orally in separate portions over many years, often outside its final order and in response to changing circumstances, rather than composed from beginning to end as a manuscript that could be repeatedly reorganized and revised.

#### Three short chapters
{: #literary-demonstrations }

Chapters 112, 103, and 108 show how the Qur'an combines precise wording, sound, structure, and depth in very little space. The English renderings below were prepared for these examples.

**1. Sūrat al-Ikhlāṣ [The Declaration of God's Oneness], chapter 112.** Four verses establish God's oneness, independence, and freedom from ancestry, offspring, or equals.

> Say: He is God, uniquely One. God, the One on whom all depend. He neither begets nor was begotten. And no one is comparable to Him. [Qur'an 112:1–4](https://quran.com/112).
>
> <div dir="rtl" lang="ar" class="quran-arabic">قُلْ هُوَ اللَّهُ أَحَدٌ ۝ اللَّهُ الصَّمَدُ ۝ لَمْ يَلِدْ وَلَمْ يُولَدْ ۝ وَلَمْ يَكُنْ لَهُ كُفُوًا أَحَدٌ</div>
{: .quote }

| Wording | Contribution to meaning |
| --- | --- |
| قُلْ — *qul* [say] | God instructs the messenger to transmit the declaration. The command itself remains part of the recitation. |
| هُوَ اللَّهُ أَحَدٌ — *huwa … aḥad* [He is God, uniquely One] | The opening identifies God and affirms His unique oneness. |
| اللَّهُ الصَّمَدُ — *al-ṣamad* [the One on whom all depend] | One divine name expresses sovereignty, independence, and the dependence of creation upon Him. Classical commentary develops these connected meanings. |
| لَمْ يَلِدْ وَلَمْ يُولَدْ — *lam yalid wa-lam yūlad* [He neither begets nor was begotten] | The same root appears in active and passive forms. The balanced clauses exclude both offspring and ancestry. |
| وَلَمْ يَكُنْ لَهُ كُفُوًا أَحَدٌ — [and no one is comparable to Him] | The final clause excludes every equal. *Aḥad* returns with a related use: “One” in the opening, “anyone” within the final negation. |

The repeated word, balanced clauses, and endings in the sound *d* give the chapter a unified form. Its central teaching is easy to understand and memorize, while its wording supports detailed theological and linguistic study. [Ibn Kathir on chapter 112](https://quran.com/112:1/tafsirs/en-tafisr-ibn-kathir).

**2. Sūrat al-ʿAṣr [Time], chapter 103.** Three verses connect the passage of time with human loss and the requirements of salvation.

> By time: humanity is indeed in loss, except those who believe, do righteous deeds, and urge one another to truth and to patience. [Qur'an 103:1–3](https://quran.com/103).
>
> <div dir="rtl" lang="ar" class="quran-arabic">وَالْعَصْرِ ۝ إِنَّ الْإِنْسَانَ لَفِي خُسْرٍ ۝ إِلَّا الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا وَعَمِلُوا الصَّالِحَاتِ وَتَوَاصَوْا بِالْحَقِّ وَتَوَاصَوْا بِالصَّبْرِ</div>
{: .quote }

*Wa-* [by] introduces the oath. *Inna* and the emphatic *la-* [indeed] reinforce the warning, while *fī khusr* [in loss] describes humanity's condition. *Illā* [except] introduces the four requirements for avoiding that loss: faith, righteous action, mutual commitment to truth, and mutual encouragement to patience.

The repeated *tawāṣaw* [urge one another] makes truth and perseverance shared responsibilities. The endings *ʿaṣr* [time], *khusr* [loss], and *ṣabr* [patience] connect the verses through sound. Two short verses establish the warning; the longer final verse explains how to respond.

**3. Sūrat al-Kawthar [Abundant Good], chapter 108.** Three verses connect God's generosity with worship and assurance against an opponent's contempt.

> We have indeed given you abundant good. So pray to your Lord and sacrifice. Surely the one who hates you is the one cut off. [Qur'an 108:1–3](https://quran.com/108).
>
> <div dir="rtl" lang="ar" class="quran-arabic">إِنَّا أَعْطَيْنَاكَ الْكَوْثَرَ ۝ فَصَلِّ لِرَبِّكَ وَانْحَرْ ۝ إِنَّ شَانِئَكَ هُوَ الْأَبْتَرُ</div>
{: .quote }

*Innā* [indeed We] emphasizes assurance, and *aʿṭaynāka* [We have given you] presents the gift as already granted. *Al-kawthar* [abundant good] expresses the extent of that generosity; an explanation attributed to Ibn ʿAbbās includes a river in Paradise among its gifts. *Fa-* [so] connects the gift to the duty of worship, and *li-rabbika* [to your Lord] directs that worship to God. [Sahih al-Bukhari 4966](https://sunnah.com/bukhari:4966).

The final *huwa* [he] places emphasis on the opponent: he is *al-abtar* [cut off]. The contrast between abundant good and being cut off gives the chapter its structure, while all three verses end in the sound *r*.

These chapters combine several qualities at once: concise expression, grammatical precision, coherent structure, patterned sound, and substantial teaching. Their significance increases when considered alongside the rest of the Qur'an and the circumstances of its revelation.

#### Comparison with other Arabic forms
{: #arabic-comparisons }

Comparison with poetry, rhymed prose, hadith [reported prophetic teaching], and attempted imitation helps identify the Qur'an's distinctive form. These short excerpts illustrate differences in style and purpose; the English conveys their meaning.

| Kind of discourse | Arabic excerpt and English rendering | Literary features |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Pre-Islamic poetry, the opening attributed to Imruʾ al-Qays | قِفَا نَبْكِ مِنْ ذِكْرَى حَبِيبٍ وَمَنْزِلِ — [Stop, you two; let us weep at the memory of a beloved and a dwelling.] | A personal expression of grief within a recurring metrical scheme. Qur'anic rhythm follows a different form from this metrical verse. |
| *Sajʿ* [rhymed prose], a litigant's objection preserved in hadith | مَنْ لَا أَكَلَ وَلَا شَرِبَ وَلَا اسْتَهَلَّ فَمِثْلُ ذَلِكَ يُطَلُّ — [One who neither ate nor drank nor cried out: such a case should count for nothing.] | The endings *istahalla / yuṭall* [cried out / counts for nothing] illustrate familiar Arabic rhyme. The Prophet ﷺ rejects the speaker's use of it to evade responsibility. |
| Prophetic hadith [reported teaching] | إِنَّمَا الأَعْمَالُ بِالنِّيَّاتِ — [Actions are judged according to intentions.] | A concise moral principle followed by explanation and a practical example. Its eloquence uses a different style from sustained Qur'anic recitation. |
| An imitation attributed to Musaylima | وَالْفِيلُ وَمَا أَدْرَاكَ مَا الْفِيلُ لَهُ زُلُومٌ طَوِيلٌ — [The elephant—and what will make you know what the elephant is? It has a long trunk.] | A Qur'anic form of question is followed by a familiar physical observation. The borrowed form carries little of the depth or purpose found in the Qur'anic examples. |
| Qur'anic proclamation, 103:1–3 | وَالْعَصْرِ … إِنَّ الْإِنْسَانَ لَفِي خُسْرٍ — [By time … humanity is indeed in loss.] | An oath introduces a universal warning and a concise account of salvation. The changing verse lengths support the argument. |

Sources: [the opening of Imruʾ al-Qays as reproduced in *History of Arabic Literature*](https://www.hindawi.org/books/70629206/3.60/), [Sahih Muslim 1682a](https://sunnah.com/muslim:1682a), [Sahih al-Bukhari 1](https://sunnah.com/bukhari:1), and [Ibn Kathir's account of Musaylima](https://ftp.shamela.ws/book/23708/2094).

The words attributed to Musaylima survive in differing versions in later Muslim accounts hostile to him. They document those writers' examples of failed imitation. Assessing the wider literary challenge also requires close comparison with the strongest Arabic works.

#### Rhyme, oaths, and parables
{: #rhetorical-arguments }

1. **Rhyme supports precise meaning.** Qur'anic endings fit the grammar, imagery, argument, and context together. Chapter 93 moves through *ḍuḥā* [morning brightness], *sajā* [grew still], and *qalā* [forsook or detested], then changes its endings as reassurance leads into duties toward the orphan, the petitioner, and God's blessings. The sound develops with the subject. [Qur'an 93](https://quran.com/93).

1. **Oaths prepare the argument.** In chapter 91, sun and moon, day and night, and heaven and earth introduce the human soul and its capacity for wrongdoing and moral growth. In chapter 93, morning brightness and night precede reassurance that the Prophet ﷺ has not been abandoned. Chapter 95 connects sacred and natural imagery with human dignity and judgment; chapter 103 connects time with loss and salvation. The images direct attention to the conclusions that follow. [Qur'an 91:1–10](https://quran.com/91), [93:1–11](https://quran.com/93), [95:1–8](https://quran.com/95), and [103:1–3](https://quran.com/103).

1. **Parables explain through concrete comparisons.** A good word resembles a rooted, fruitful tree; a corrupt word resembles a tree torn from the ground (14:24–26). Reliance on protectors besides God resembles reliance on a spider's frail house (29:41). Rain reviving dead land illustrates resurrection (35:9). Charity ruined by injury resembles soil washed from a smooth rock, leaving it unproductive (2:264). Each comparison makes a moral or theological relationship easier to understand. [Qur'an 14:24–26](https://quran.com/14:24-26), [29:41](https://quran.com/29:41), [35:9](https://quran.com/35:9), and [2:264](https://quran.com/2:264).

#### Precise wording and divine names
{: #context-sensitive-wording }

The Qur'an selects words that fit the particular concern, action, or emphasis of a passage. Closely related expressions reveal different meanings when read in context.

| Passages | Difference in wording | Contribution to meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 6:151 and 17:31 | *Min imlāq* [because of poverty] and *khashyata imlāq* [for fear of poverty]. God provides for “you and them” in the first passage, and “them and you” in the second. | Existing poverty is answered by reassuring the parents first. Fear that children will cause poverty is answered by naming the children's provision first. The word order matches the concern. |
| 18:78 and 18:82 | *Lam tastaṭiʿ* and *lam tasṭiʿ* [both: you could not bear]. | The fuller form precedes the explanation of difficult events; the shorter form follows it. Ibn Kathir connects this shortening with the removal of confusion. Both forms are grammatical, and their placement contributes to the meaning. |
| 11:44 | *Iblaʿī* [swallow] in the command to the earth. | “Swallow” conveys the decisive absorption of floodwater. The paired command to the sky, *aqliʿī* [cease], balances the two actions in a compact expression. |
| 35:28 and 28:21 | *Yakhshā* [fears with awe] in relation to knowledge of God; *khāʾifan* [afraid] as Moses leaves the city in danger. | These contexts distinguish reverent awareness from fear of an immediate threat. The Arabic roots have overlapping uses, and context establishes the emphasis here. |
| 108:1 and 108:3 | *Al-kawthar* [abundant good] and *al-abtar* [cut off]. | The contrast between abundance and deprivation connects the opening gift to the final assurance. |

See [Qur'an 11:44](https://quran.com/11:44), [35:28](https://quran.com/35:28), [28:21](https://quran.com/28:21), [Ibn Kathir on 17:31](https://quran.com/17:31/tafsirs/en-tafisr-ibn-kathir) and [18:82](https://quran.com/18:82/tafsirs/en-tafisr-ibn-kathir), and [chapter 108](https://quran.com/108).

**Divine names at verse endings also fit their context:**

1. In [2:226–227](https://quran.com/2:226-227), reconciliation after marital estrangement is followed by **Forgiving, Merciful**. A decision to divorce is followed by **Hearing, Knowing**. The first encourages reconciliation and pardon; the second reminds the parties that God knows their words and intentions.

1. In [5:118](https://quran.com/5:118), Jesus entrusts judgment to God and concludes with **Mighty, Wise**, including when he mentions forgiveness. These names affirm God's authority and wisdom in deciding the matter.

1. In [49:13](https://quran.com/49:13), the declaration that righteousness determines human worth ends with **Knowing, Aware**. God knows the inward quality by which people are judged, beyond their visible lineage or status.

#### Connections across the book
{: #connections-across-the-book }

1. **One passage explains another.** The command to worship in 51:56 is followed by the clarification that God needs neither provision nor food (51:57–58). The promise of forgiveness in 39:53 leads into the call to return to God in 39:54. The Prophet ﷺ explained the wrongdoing mentioned in 6:82 through 31:13, which describes associating partners with God as a grave wrong. These connections clarify a passage's meaning and application. [Qur'an 51:56–58](https://quran.com/51:56-58), [39:53–54](https://quran.com/39:53-54), and [Sahih al-Bukhari 3360](https://sunnah.com/bukhari:3360).

1. **Adjacent chapters develop related themes.** Chapters 105–106 connect the defeat of the army of the elephant with Quraysh's security, provision, and duty to worship. Chapters 93–94 join divine care and reassurance to responsibility, effort, and ease with hardship. Chapters 113–114 share the opening appeal for refuge and move from surrounding dangers to inward whispering. [Qur'an 105](https://quran.com/105), [106](https://quran.com/106), [93](https://quran.com/93), [94](https://quran.com/94), [113](https://quran.com/113), and [114](https://quran.com/114).

1. **Groups of passages and chapters display broader organization.** Scholars including al-Biqāʿī, al-Farāḥī, and Iṣlāḥī studied these relationships. A sound structural analysis accounts for the whole unit, explains its boundaries, and addresses passages that initially seem out of place. Nicolai Sinai examines this tradition of thematic coherence and the Qur'an's interpretation of its own passages. [“Reading Sūrat al-Anʿām”](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/reclaiming-islamic-tradition/reading-surat-alanam-with-muhammad-rashid-rida-and-sayyid-qusb/8431797630F199956F13CC01456B43B9), [“Historical Criticism and Recent Trends in Western Scholarship on the Qur'an”](https://doi.org/10.29117/jcsis.2020.0259).

#### The standard of the literary challenge
{: #standard-for-literary-challenge }

Answering the literary challenge requires an original Arabic composition comparable in scope to the passage being challenged. It must combine precise meaning, grammatical control, concise expression, coherent structure, and effective sound. Each choice of wording and form should serve the passage's purpose.

Classical theories of inimitability emphasize different features, particularly the arrangement of words and meanings. A clear comparison therefore identifies its criteria and applies them consistently to strong examples. Literary merit is assessed through close analysis and informed judgment, with room for disagreement. Popularity, religious agreement, and the author's identity are separate questions. [Lara Harb on classical theories of arrangement](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/arabic-poetics/nazm-wonder-and-the-inimitability-of-the-quran/B980231F9936B751EF11029D69CF4554).

### <span class="num-roman">V.</span> The Qur'an's Account of Itself
{: #self-referential-character }

The Qur'an repeatedly explains its own source, purpose, delivery, interpretation, and preservation. It states why it should be accepted as revelation, records objections, and answers them within the text.

| Subject | Qur'anic explanation |
| --- | --- |
| **Source and identity** | Revelation from the Lord of all worlds, delivered to the messenger's heart in clear Arabic (26:192–195); guidance and a criterion for distinguishing truth from falsehood (2:185). |
| **Purpose** | To bring people from darkness into light (14:1), guide them toward what is most upright (17:9), and invite reflection (38:29). |
| **Gradual revelation** | Its delivery in stages strengthens the messenger's heart and answers objections as they arise (25:32–33). |
| **How to approach it** | Recite carefully (73:4), listen attentively (7:204), and reflect (47:24). Recognize the distinction between foundational passages and those whose meaning is less immediately clear (3:7). |
| **Objections** | It records accusations of human assistance and ancient tales (25:4–5), poetry, and confused dreams (21:5). |
| **Response and preservation** | It describes awe and softened hearts (39:23), calls for conduct guided by its teaching, and promises divine protection of the message (15:9). |

The repeated command *qul* [say] makes the relationship between source and messenger explicit: the Prophet ﷺ recites the instruction directing him to speak. Its self-description also gives readers a clear claim to assess against the text and its history. [Qur'an 26:192–195](https://quran.com/26:192-195), [25:32–33](https://quran.com/25:32-33), [3:7](https://quran.com/3:7), and [39:23](https://quran.com/39:23).

Anne-Sylvie Boisliveau studies how the Qur'an describes itself and argues for its authority. Daniel Madigan and Stefan Wild have also examined this feature. [Boisliveau's account of her research, International Qur'anic Studies Association](https://iqsaweb.org/2013/02/04/sr/).

#### Direct engagement with the listener
{: #reader-participation }

The Qur'an repeatedly asks listeners to examine their own lives and answer its questions. Human origin, food, water, and fire become subjects for reflection on dependence upon God (56:58–73). Chapter 55 repeatedly asks people to acknowledge His favors. The question *fa-ayna tadhhabūn* [where, then, are you going?] in 81:26 calls them to reconsider their direction. [Qur'an 56:58–73](https://quran.com/56:58-73), [55:13](https://quran.com/55:13), and [81:26](https://quran.com/81:26).

Questions, parables, warnings, and answers to objections make reflection part of the discourse. The listener is called to understand the message and respond to it.

#### Public scrutiny during revelation
{: #public-scrutiny-during-revelation }

The Qur'an made claims before people who knew its messenger and witnessed the events it discussed. It referred to his earlier life (10:16), his lack of prior reading and writing of scripture (29:48), accusations of teachers and collaborators (16:103; 25:4–5), public defeats, and promised outcomes. Contemporaries with relevant knowledge could challenge these claims directly. [Qur'an 10:16](https://quran.com/10:16), [29:48](https://quran.com/29:48), and [25:4–5](https://quran.com/25:4-5).

This public scrutiny concerns claims open to historical examination, including the messenger's background and predictions about events. The divine source of revelation is assessed through its signs. Our surviving record of contemporary responses is partial, and the Qur'an's account of an exchange remains one source of evidence.

Independent historical evidence strengthens the assessment. A prediction requires an established date before fulfillment, a clear meaning, and an identifiable outcome. The relevant evidence is discussed under [prophecies](#prophecies) and [criteria for reassessment](#what-would-weaken-the-case).

It recounts earlier sacred history with an authoritative and independent voice, correcting theological distortions, restoring pure monotheism, and reshaping familiar narratives with remarkable precision.

1. Its historical narratives contain specific details that Muslim scholars compare with earlier accounts and independent historical evidence. The strength of each comparison rests on the wording, dating, and available records.

#### The universality of prophecy
{: #universality-of-prophecy }

The Qur'an places Muhammad ﷺ within a continuous history of divine guidance. Every community has received a messenger or warner (16:36; 35:24), and the prophets share the call to worship God alone (21:25). Noah, Abraham, Moses, Jesus, and Muhammad ﷺ carry the same foundational message, with legal prescriptions suited to their communities (42:13; 5:48). [Qur'an 16:36](https://quran.com/16:36), [21:25](https://quran.com/21:25), [42:13](https://quran.com/42:13), and [5:48](https://quran.com/5:48).

Divine guidance therefore extends across human history and beyond the figures named in the Qur'an. The text explicitly states that some messengers' stories have been recounted and others have not (40:78). Muhammad's ﷺ mission renews this common call to the Creator. [Qur'an 40:78](https://quran.com/40:78).

#### Jesus and Mary
{: #jesus-and-mary }

The Qur'an gives Jesus and Mary exceptional honor. Mary is chosen and purified (3:42), and her chastity and faith are held up as an example (66:12). Jesus is the Messiah, born miraculously through God's word and granted extraordinary signs by His permission (3:45–49). [Qur'an 3:42–49](https://quran.com/3:42-49) and [66:12](https://quran.com/66:12).

Their place in the Qur'an is consistent with its monotheism. Jesus is God's servant and messenger (4:171; 19:30). His creation is compared with Adam's: both exist through God's command (3:59). This explains his miraculous birth and designation as a word from God while maintaining the distinction between the Creator and His creatures. [Qur'an 4:171](https://quran.com/4:171), [19:30](https://quran.com/19:30), and [3:59](https://quran.com/3:59).

The Qur'an thus affirms Jesus' prophethood and Mary's righteousness within the same theology that governs its account of every prophet.

#### Engagement with earlier religious traditions
{: #late-antique-engagement }

The Qur'an engages a wide range of scriptural narratives, religious teachings, and debates. Scholars study its relationship with biblical, rabbinic, Syriac Christian, apocryphal, and other late-antique traditions, including material transmitted orally. Tommaso Tesei distinguishes encounters with themes circulating in this wider setting from direct knowledge of particular writings. [“Echoes of Pseudepigrapha in the Qur'ān”](https://doi.org/10.1017/9789048540105.009).

Its treatment of this material consistently emphasizes God's oneness, prophecy, repentance, and judgment. A historical explanation must account for both the parallels and the Qur'an's selection, organization, and theological differences. Specific claims of dependence also require a plausible route of transmission, examined under [alternative explanations](#borrowing-hypothesis).

### <span class="num-roman">VIII.</span> Knowledge of Nature
{: #nature-knowledge }

The Qur'an repeatedly directs human beings to the natural world as evidence of God's wisdom and power: the heavens and earth, rain, animals, human development, day and night, winds, seas, mountains, and the origins and fragility of life.

Its descriptions invite reflection on creation. Claims that a verse identifies a specific scientific discovery are assessed through its language, context, and historical setting.

#### Assessing claims about science
{: #scientific-claim-standards }

| Kind of claim | Basis |
| --- | --- |
| **Direct description** | The Arabic clearly describes a natural feature, such as heavy rain clouds. Historical evidence establishes whether the knowledge was exceptional for its time. |
| **Compatibility with later knowledge** | An established meaning agrees with a scientific finding. Demonstrating prediction requires further evidence of specificity and prior knowledge. |
| **Possible correspondence** | An image or expression has a plausible connection with modern knowledge, alongside other supported readings. |
| **Retrospective interpretation** | A modern comparison relies on an uncertain meaning or adds technical details. Its strength depends on resolving those interpretive questions. |

Sound assessment begins with the Arabic and its context, considers earlier interpretations, and establishes the relevant historical and scientific evidence. Clear observations, possible correspondences, and demonstrated predictions carry different weight.

### <span class="num-roman">IX.</span> Numerical Patterns
{: #numerical-patterns }

Repeated words and phrases contribute to the Qur'an's organization. Numerical claims are useful when their counting rules are clear, their results are reproducible, and their significance is explained.

1. **Specify the text.** Identify the reading, edition, verse-numbering system, and treatment of opening invocations.

1. **Define the counting rule.** State whether the unit is a written form, dictionary form, root, letter, or phrase. Apply consistent rules to attached particles, spelling, and vowel marks.

1. **List the occurrences.** Provide a concordance, verse list, or code so readers can repeat the count. Apply the same inclusion rules throughout.

1. **Assess chance and selection.** A large text permits many comparisons. The likelihood of accidental matches increases with the number of combinations searched. Claims of exceptional design require a justified comparison and consistent methods across texts.

1. **Explain the significance.** A verified count establishes a textual fact. Its literary role and any further claim of miraculous design each require their own explanation.

Two examples are the complete verse refrains in chapters 55 and 77, counted in the common Ḥafṣ reading with Kufan verse numbering:

### <span class="num-roman">X.</span> Preservation and Memorization
{: #preservation-and-memorization }

The Qur'an's preservation is one of the most remarkable features of its history: it was transmitted simultaneously through writing, memorization, public recitation, and teacher-to-student instruction from the earliest Muslim community onward.

1. The Qur'an's standard written text has been transmitted with exceptional stability for over fourteen centuries. Early manuscripts, inherited readings, memorization, public worship, and continuous copying in Arabic reinforce its preservation. Its early variants and standardization are examined below.
    > These overlapping channels form an unusually powerful preservation system. The same text was simultaneously recited, memorized, taught, written, copied, and heard publicly across expanding Muslim communities, making alteration extraordinarily difficult and preserving the Qur'an in the language in which it was revealed.
    {: .gloss }
    > These connected practices give later readers unusually direct access to an early Arabic scripture. Its preservation can be compared with other ancient texts through the dates and extent of surviving copies, their variations, and the evidence of oral transmission.

#### The text, its script, and its readings
{: #text-and-writing-system }

Qur'anic preservation involves three connected elements: **the wording of the text**, **the script that records it**, and **the traditions through which it is recited**. Distinguishing them makes the manuscript evidence clear.

| Feature | Function | Significance for preservation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Rasm [consonantal framework]** | Records the basic word forms, including some long vowels. Several consonants share an outline. | Provides the basis for tracing the early standard written text. Recitation supplies details beyond this framework. |
| **Consonantal dots** | Distinguish letters with the same outline. Early Arabic used dots less consistently than modern print. | Fuller dotting makes readings explicit; differences in dotting sometimes distinguish readings. |
| **Vowel and recitation marks** | Record short vowels, doubled consonants, pauses, and pronunciation. | Fuller notation records an existing recitation more precisely. Differences in vowels can also affect grammar and meaning. |
| **Spelling conventions** | Represent the same sound in different ways, such as writing or omitting a letter for a long vowel. | Distinct spellings can preserve the same recitation. Shared spelling details also reveal relationships among manuscripts. |
| **Reading variants** | Preserve transmitted differences in pronunciation, vowels, consonants, grammatical forms, and occasionally words. | Some affect sound; others affect meaning or interpretation. Each belongs to an identifiable reading tradition. |
| **Scribal and early nonstandard variants** | Include copying errors, corrections, and wording outside the standard tradition. | Their extent, date, and transmission determine their importance. An error in one copy differs from a change adopted across a community. |

Zuhayr's inscription, dated 24 AH / 644–645 CE, includes consonantal dots. It provides direct evidence of their early use, before later systems made notation more consistent. [ʿAli ibn Ibrahim Ghabban and Robert Hoyland, study of the Zuhayr inscription](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1600-0471.2008.00297.x).

#### The canonical readings
{: #canonical-readings }

The **qirāʾāt [recognized Qur'anic reading traditions]** preserve transmitted ways of reciting the Qur'an. The familiar Ḥafṣ transmission from ʿĀṣim and Warsh transmission from Nāfiʿ belong to this tradition. Classical Sunni scholarship recognized ten canonical readings, each with named transmitters and specified transmission routes. This classification organized an existing recitation tradition.

Acceptance required sound transmission, conformity with Arabic, and compatibility with at least one standard regional codex. Those codices shared an overwhelmingly stable text with limited regional differences. The seven *aḥruf* [authorized modes of revelation, whose precise meaning is disputed] are distinct from both the seven readings selected by Ibn Mujāhid and the later canon of ten. [Marijn van Putten, *Quranic Arabic: From Its Hijazi Origins to Its Classical Reading Traditions*](https://doi.org/10.1163/9789004506251).

In [1:4](https://quran.com/1:4), for example, *mālik* [Master or Owner] and *malik* [King] of the Day of Judgment both fit the early spelling and have recognized transmission. They express complementary aspects of God's sovereignty. Other variants contribute to grammatical analysis and legal interpretation.

Written and oral transmission supported one another. In his study of Hišām's *Ibrāhām/Ibrāhīm* [two recited forms of Abraham's name], van Putten identifies both the influence of spelling on recitation and agreements that point to inherited oral transmission. [“Hišām's ʾIbrāhām”](https://doi.org/10.1017/S1356186319000518).

The readings preserve a defined body of transmitted variation whose wording and transmission can be studied. Muslims receive the accepted readings as authorized revelation; manuscript research traces their relationship to the early written text.

#### Early variants and standardization
{: #difficult-textual-evidence }

1. **The Ṣanʿāʾ palimpsest preserves an early textual variant.** A palimpsest is parchment whose earlier writing was erased and written over. Its upper text follows the standard Qur'anic text; the lower text includes differences in wording, additions, omissions, and arrangement. Behnam Sadeghi and Mohsen Goudarzi interpret it as an early nonstandard textual tradition. Asma Hilali offers a different account of its production and use, and some readings of the damaged undertext remain disputed. [Sadeghi and Goudarzi, “Ṣanʿāʾ 1 and the Origins of the Qur'ān”](https://doi.org/10.1515/islam-2011-0025); [Hilali, *The Sanaa Palimpsest*](https://www.iis.ac.uk/publications-listing/the-sanaa-palimpsest/).

   The manuscript places recognizable Qur'anic material near Islam's beginnings and documents early variation in wording. Its significance concerns the date, extent, and transmission of those differences. It rules out the claim that every early copy had identical wording while providing early evidence against theories of a Qur'an first assembled centuries later.

1. **Reports of Companion collections preserve evidence of early variation.** Muslim sources describe differences in order and wording in collections associated with Ibn Masʿūd, Ubayy ibn Kaʿb, and others. They also report questions about the opening and final refuge chapters in Ibn Masʿūd's collection and supplicatory texts in Ubayy's. These are reports about collections, rather than surviving copies in the Companions' own hands. Their reliability and the status of notes or prayers require individual assessment. Sadeghi and Goudarzi compare reported Companion variants with the Ṣanʿāʾ evidence. [Their study](https://doi.org/10.1515/islam-2011-0025).

1. **ʿUthmān's standardization established common written copies.** The traditional account describes a commission to prepare standard copies and an order to destroy other written materials amid disagreements over recitation. This was an early intervention to establish a shared written standard, followed by its widespread transmission. [Sahih al-Bukhari 4987](https://sunnah.com/bukhari:4987).

1. **Manuscript dates draw on several kinds of evidence.** Radiocarbon testing of the Birmingham fragments dates the animal skin. Dating the writing also requires attention to script, layout, and textual relationships. Each fragment directly attests the passages it contains. [University of Birmingham, manuscript FAQ](https://www.birmingham.ac.uk/facilities/cadbury/birmingham-quran-mingana-collection/birmingham-quran/faqs).

The evidence supports **an exceptionally early standard text, transmitted with remarkable stability alongside defined reading traditions**. Early variants and the history of standardization belong within that account of preservation.

#### Early establishment of the standard text
{: #early-textual-closure }

Shared spelling details and relationships among manuscripts point to a common written ancestor. Nicolai Sinai argues that a standard consonantal text by about **650 CE or earlier** is the best starting point for historical analysis. This places its establishment within a generation of the Prophet's ﷺ death, close to the traditional date of ʿUthmān's standardization. [“When did the consonantal skeleton of the Quran reach closure? Part II”](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0041977X14000111).

This early evidence directly challenges theories that place the Qur'an's basic composition two or three centuries later. Research into the earliest collection, regional copies, spelling, and readings refines the history of a text already established near Islam's beginnings.

#### A shared language across generations and peoples
{: #global-arabic-transmission }

Muslims with first languages as varied as Indonesian, Turkish, Hausa, Persian, Urdu, and English recite the Qur'an in Arabic. Translations make its meaning accessible, while its original language remains the shared language of worship across recognized reading traditions.

Recitation, memorization, grammar, commentary, and dictionaries have also sustained access to the Arabic in which the Qur'an first appeared. Spoken Arabic has developed over time, and understanding early usage requires study. The continuing preservation and teaching of the original language give later generations direct access to the text itself. [Graham and Kermani on recitation](https://doi.org/10.1017/CCOL0521831601.007).

### <span class="num-roman">XI.</span> Prophecies and Fulfilled Predictions
{: #prophecies }

The Qur'an makes predictions in circumstances where their fulfillment was far from guaranteed. Their force rests on the content of the prediction, its date, the likelihood of the outcome, and independent evidence of fulfillment. The section also includes declarations about the finality of prophecy, preservation, and enduring guidance, each assessed according to its claim.

1. The Qur'an declares itself the final revelation from God to humanity and Prophet Muhammad ﷺ the seal and last of the prophets—a standing claim of finality that has remained intact across fourteen centuries.
    > "Muḥammad is not the father of [any] one of your men, but [he is] the Messenger of God and seal [i.e., last] of the prophets. And ever is God, of all things, Knowing." (**Sūrat al-Aḥzāb 33:40**), Saheeh International

1. It foretells the message's wider reach. Meccan passages address the worlds and all those whom the message will reach (38:87–88; 6:19). ### <span class="num-roman">XII.</span> God, Human Purpose, and the Meaning of Life
{: #theology-of-god }

The Qur'an gives a coherent account of God and humanity's relationship with Him. Divine oneness connects its teaching about worship, ethics, human dignity, and judgment. Colin Turner examines this unifying role in his study of Said Nursi's interpretation. [“On Divine Unity,” *The Qur'an Revealed*](https://doi.org/10.1017/9783940924292.003).

#### God's oneness and attributes
{: #oneness-and-divine-attributes }

1. **God is One and independent of creation.** Chapter 112 affirms His oneness and excludes offspring, ancestry, and equals. The Throne Verse brings together His life, sustaining power, knowledge, sovereignty, and freedom from weariness (2:255). Human beings depend on God; He is free of any need for their worship or provision (35:15; 51:56–58). Worship is therefore directed to the Creator upon whom everything depends. [Qur'an 112](https://quran.com/112), [2:255](https://quran.com/2:255), and [35:15](https://quran.com/35:15).

1. **His power is joined to knowledge, mercy, and justice.** God knows what is hidden and what is open, and governs with wisdom. His mercy encompasses all things (7:156), and He wrongs no one even by an atom's weight (4:40). His warnings against wrongdoing accompany repeated invitations to repent. These attributes explain both human accountability and the continuing possibility of forgiveness. [Qur'an 59:22–24](https://quran.com/59:22-24), [4:40](https://quran.com/4:40), and [39:53–54](https://quran.com/39:53-54).

#### God's transcendence and nearness
{: #transcendence-and-nearness }

> Nothing is like Him; He is the Hearing, the Seeing. [Qur'an 42:11](https://quran.com/42:11), translation prepared for this discussion.
>
> <div dir="rtl" lang="ar" class="quran-arabic">لَيْسَ كَمِثْلِهِ شَيْءٌ وَهُوَ السَّمِيعُ الْبَصِيرُ</div>
{: .quote }

> When My servants ask you about Me, I am near. I answer the caller's prayer when they call upon Me. [Qur'an 2:186](https://quran.com/2:186), translation of an excerpt prepared for this discussion.
>
> <div dir="rtl" lang="ar" class="quran-arabic">وَإِذَا سَأَلَكَ عِبَادِي عَنِّي فَإِنِّي قَرِيبٌ أُجِيبُ دَعْوَةَ الدَّاعِ إِذَا دَعَانِ</div>
{: .quote }

God is beyond comparison with creation and directly accessible in prayer. He knows every thought, need, and concern. [50:16](https://quran.com/50:16) joins His knowledge of inward whispering with nearness greater than the jugular vein. Explanations of this nearness include divine knowledge and the proximity of His angels, consistent with His transcendence of creation. [Ibn Kathir on 50:16](https://quran.com/50:16/tafsirs/en-tafisr-ibn-kathir).

#### Repentance and direct access to God
{: #direct-access-to-god }

Every person can turn directly to God. Adam receives words of repentance and is forgiven (2:37). Each person bears responsibility for their own conduct (53:38–39). Those who have greatly wronged themselves are called to return to God with hope in His mercy (39:53–54). Nearness to Him depends on faith and righteous action, beyond ancestry or wealth (34:37). [Qur'an 2:37](https://quran.com/2:37), [53:38–39](https://quran.com/53:38-39), and [34:37](https://quran.com/34:37).

Forgiveness is open through sincere repentance, without inherited guilt, a privileged ethnicity, divine incarnation, or a priesthood authorizing access to God. Prophets teach and guide; God hears prayer and grants forgiveness. Asking someone else to pray for us complements this direct relationship.

Repentance includes faith, changed conduct, and making amends for harm done to others. The invitation to seek forgiveness therefore also calls people to reform their lives. [Qur'an 4:110](https://quran.com/4:110), [5:39](https://quran.com/5:39), and [25:70–71](https://quran.com/25:70-71).

#### Human purpose and responsibility
{: #human-purpose }

The Qur'an connects creation, choice, temptation, worship, suffering, death, resurrection, and judgment in one account of life. Human beings receive life and guidance, choose how to respond, and answer for their actions. Worship expresses gratitude and forms character. Resurrection brings the full consequences of human conduct before God's judgment. [Qur'an 76:2–3](https://quran.com/76:2-3), [51:56–58](https://quran.com/51:56-58), [67:2](https://quran.com/67:2), and [99:6–8](https://quran.com/99:6-8).

This purpose gives ordinary choices lasting importance: how we treat parents, handle money, control anger, keep promises, and help others. Human responsibility operates within God's sovereignty, a relationship Islamic theologians have explained in different ways. The practical duty is clear: worship God and use the life He has given us well.

#### Suffering and justice
{: #suffering-and-justice }

1. **Life tests people through ease and hardship.** Both are described as trials (21:35). Chapter 89 rejects the assumption that wealth proves God's approval or poverty His contempt. It directs attention to how people treat orphans and the poor. [Qur'an 21:35](https://quran.com/21:35) and [89:15–20](https://quran.com/89:15-20).

1. **Human knowledge is limited.** What we dislike may bring good, and what we desire may bring harm (2:216). This calls for humility about purposes we cannot fully see. The Qur'an also identifies suffering caused by human wrongdoing and commands action against injustice. [Qur'an 2:216](https://quran.com/2:216), [30:41](https://quran.com/30:41), and [4:75](https://quran.com/4:75).

1. **Justice is completed beyond this life.** God knows the conduct of oppressors and promises exact judgment (14:42; 21:47). He promises reward for patience and an enduring life free of grief for those received into His mercy (39:10; 35:34–35). The afterlife completes the justice that earthly life often leaves unfinished. [Qur'an 14:42](https://quran.com/14:42), [21:47](https://quran.com/21:47), [39:10](https://quran.com/39:10), and [35:34–35](https://quran.com/35:34-35).

These teachings connect patience and hope with the duty to relieve suffering and resist injustice. Trust in God's wisdom accompanies responsibility for what we can do.

### <span class="num-roman">XIII.</span> Guidance, Law, and Morality
{: #guidance-law-and-morality }

The Qur'an renews the call of earlier revelation and connects belief in God with mercy, repentance, justice, responsibility, and care for others.

1. The Qur'an repeatedly presents mercy as a defining attribute of God. Every chapter but one opens with the invocation, **"In the name of God, the Most Compassionate, the Most Merciful."** Muslims are taught to say these words before beginning their actions, so mercy frames not only the Qur'an but the rhythm of everyday life.

    > "My mercy encompasses all things." (**Sūrat al-Aʿrāf 7:156**), Saheeh International
    >
    > <div dir="rtl" lang="ar" class="quran-arabic">﴿وَرَحْمَتِى وَسِعَتْ كُلَّ شَىْءٍ﴾ <span class="ayah-ref">[سُورَةُ الأَعۡرَافِ: ١٥٦]</span></div>
    {: .quote }


1. The Qur'an keeps forgiveness open for every sin, no matter how far a person has gone, and tells people never to despair of God's mercy.

    > "Say, 'O My servants who have transgressed against themselves [by sinning], do not despair of the mercy of God. Indeed, God forgives all sins. Indeed, it is He who is the Forgiving, the Merciful.'" (**Sūrat al-Zumar 39:53**), Saheeh International
    >
    > <div dir="rtl" lang="ar" class="quran-arabic">﴿۞ قُلْ يَـٰعِبَادِىَ ٱلَّذِينَ أَسْرَفُوا۟ عَلَىٰٓ أَنفُسِهِمْ لَا تَقْنَطُوا۟ مِن رَّحْمَةِ ٱللَّهِ ۚ إِنَّ ٱللَّهَ يَغْفِرُ ٱلذُّنُوبَ جَمِيعًا ۚ إِنَّهُۥ هُوَ ٱلْغَفُورُ ٱلرَّحِيمُ ۝٥٣﴾ <span class="ayah-ref">سُورَةُ الزُّمَرِ</span></div>
    {: .quote }


1. The Qur'an rejects inherited guilt: nobody is born owing another person's moral debt, and no soul bears another's burden.

    > "That no bearer of burdens will bear the burden of another. And that there is not for man except that [good] for which he strives." (**Sūrat al-Najm 53:38-39**), Saheeh International
    >
    > <div dir="rtl" lang="ar" class="quran-arabic">﴿أَلَّا تَزِرُ وَازِرَةٌ وِزْرَ أُخْرَىٰ ۝٣٨ وَأَن لَّيْسَ لِلْإِنسَـٰنِ إِلَّا مَا سَعَىٰ ۝٣٩﴾ <span class="ayah-ref">سُورَةُ النَّجۡمِ</span></div>
    {: .quote }

1. It emphasizes genuine character over hollow ritual: worship that leaves a person cruel is rejected.
    > "Righteousness is not in turning your faces towards the east or the west. Rather, the righteous are those who believe in God, the Last Day, the angels, the Books, and the prophets; who give charity out of their cherished wealth to relatives, orphans, the poor, ˹needy˺ travellers, beggars, and for freeing captives; who establish prayer, pay alms-tax, and keep the pledges they make; and who are patient in times of suffering, adversity, and in ˹the heat of˺ battle. It is they who are true ˹in faith˺, and it is they who are mindful ˹of God˺." (**Sūrat al-Baqarah 2:177**), Dr. Mustafa Khattab, *The Clear Quran*
    >
    > <div dir="rtl" lang="ar" class="quran-arabic">﴿۞ لَّيْسَ ٱلْبِرَّ أَن تُوَلُّوا۟ وُجُوهَكُمْ قِبَلَ ٱلْمَشْرِقِ وَٱلْمَغْرِبِ وَلَـٰكِنَّ ٱلْبِرَّ مَنْ ءَامَنَ بِٱللَّهِ وَٱلْيَوْمِ ٱلْـَٔاخِرِ وَٱلْمَلَـٰٓئِكَةِ وَٱلْكِتَـٰبِ وَٱلنَّبِيِّـۧنَ وَءَاتَى ٱلْمَالَ عَلَىٰ حُبِّهِۦ ذَوِى ٱلْقُرْبَىٰ وَٱلْيَتَـٰمَىٰ وَٱلْمَسَـٰكِينَ وَٱبْنَ ٱلسَّبِيلِ وَٱلسَّآئِلِينَ وَفِى ٱلرِّقَابِ وَأَقَامَ ٱلصَّلَوٰةَ وَءَاتَى ٱلزَّكَوٰةَ وَٱلْمُوفُونَ بِعَهْدِهِمْ إِذَا عَـٰهَدُوا۟ ۖ وَٱلصَّـٰبِرِينَ فِى ٱلْبَأْسَآءِ وَٱلضَّرَّآءِ وَحِينَ ٱلْبَأْسِ ۗ أُو۟لَـٰٓئِكَ ٱلَّذِينَ صَدَقُوا۟ ۖ وَأُو۟لَـٰٓئِكَ هُمُ ٱلْمُتَّقُونَ ۝١٧٧﴾ <span class="ayah-ref">سُورَةُ البَقَرَةِ</span></div>

1. It commands honesty, keeping one's word, humility, patience, and restraint in anger, while forbidding backbiting, mockery, contempt, and suspicion of others.

1. Care for others—especially the weak and dependent—is treated as a duty across relationships: spouses, children, parents in old age, relatives, orphans, the poor, travellers, debtors, captives, servants, and those in one's charge.

1. It makes the poor's share of wealth an obligation rather than a favour. Alms are a due; an insolvent debtor must be given time, and forgiving the debt is commended; interest is banned; and charity followed by humiliation loses its moral worth.
    > "And in their wealth there was a rightful share ˹fulfilled˺ for the beggar and the poor." (**Sūrat al-Dhāriyāt 51:19**), Dr. Mustafa Khattab, *The Clear Quran*

    {: .quote }

1. It equates neglect of the orphan and poor as rejection of the religion itself.
    > "Have you seen the one who denies the ˹final˺ Judgment? That is the one who repulses the orphan and does not encourage the feeding of the poor." (**Sūrat al-Māʿūn 107:1-3**), Dr. Mustafa Khattab, *The Clear Quran*

#### Detailed laws and moral responsibility
{: #legal-sophistication }

The Qur'an gives practical legal form to its moral principles. Its longest verse regulates deferred debts through writing, witnesses, accurate records, and protection for scribes and witnesses. It provides for those unable to dictate their own obligations. The following verse connects financial trust with accountability before God. [Qur'an 2:282–283](https://quran.com/2:282-283).

| Area | Provision | Purpose |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Debts and trade** | Record deferred obligations, use witnesses, protect scribes, and give an insolvent debtor time. Forgiving the debt is commended (2:280–283). | Protect rights, reduce disputes, and require consideration for hardship. |
| **Inheritance** | Recognize both women and men as heirs and specify shares for family relationships (4:7; 4:11–12; 4:176). | Establish entitlements and limit arbitrary exclusion. These provisions form the basis of the developed discipline of inheritance law. |
| **Orphan property** | Protect it from misuse, assess readiness before transferring it, and witness the handover (4:2; 4:6; 4:10). | Make guardians accountable for the property entrusted to them. |
| **Divorce** | Regulate waiting periods, housing, maintenance, and witnessing; prohibit retaining a spouse to cause harm (2:231; 65:1–7). | Protect rights during separation and constrain abuse. |
| **Commerce and testimony** | Require fair weights and measures and truthful testimony, even against oneself or close relatives (17:35; 4:135). | Uphold honesty and justice despite personal interest. |
| **Treaties** | Require fulfillment of commitments and observance of treaties with those who have kept them (5:1; 9:4). | Make agreements binding across communal boundaries. |

See [2:280–283](https://quran.com/2:280-283), [4:6–12](https://quran.com/4:6-12), [65:1–7](https://quran.com/65:1-7), and [9:4](https://quran.com/9:4).

**Legal duties are repeatedly joined to conscience.** The debt passage ends with God's knowledge (2:282); the next describes concealed testimony as a sin of the heart (2:283). Divorce regulations repeatedly call for mindfulness of God and trust in Him (65:1–7). The law addresses intentions as well as outward conduct.

#### Gradual revelation and moral education
{: #progressive-pedagogy }

The Qur'an's gradual revelation prepared people to understand and practice its commands. The prohibition of intoxicants developed through stages: their harm was identified as greater than their benefit (2:219); prayer while intoxicated was prohibited (4:43); then avoidance was commanded, with their effects on relationships, remembrance, and prayer explained (5:90–91). [Qur'an 2:219](https://quran.com/2:219), [4:43](https://quran.com/4:43), and [5:90–91](https://quran.com/5:90-91).

The sequence combines explanation, changes to established habits, and a binding rule. Aisha likewise describes early revelation as teaching Paradise and Hell before detailed prohibitions were imposed. [Sahih al-Bukhari 4993](https://sunnah.com/bukhari:4993).

The Qur'an explains that gradual delivery strengthens the messenger's heart (25:32). It also addresses the replacement of instructions during revelation (2:106; 16:101), whose extent and interpretation scholars discuss under abrogation. Its foundational theology and moral purposes remain consistent as particular instructions develop. [Qur'an 25:32](https://quran.com/25:32), [2:106](https://quran.com/2:106), and [16:101](https://quran.com/16:101).

#### Universal moral responsibility
{: #universal-moral-identity }

The Qur'an makes righteousness the measure of worth before God. Peoples and tribes exist so that they may know one another (49:13). Believing women and men receive the same promise of forgiveness and reward (33:35). Justice remains binding even against one's own family or community (4:135; 5:8). [Qur'an 49:13](https://quran.com/49:13), [33:35](https://quran.com/33:35), and [4:135](https://quran.com/4:135).

These principles replace inherited privilege with shared moral accountability. They also provide a standard for judging tribal arrogance, favoritism, and failures within Muslim societies themselves.

### <span class="num-roman">XIV.</span> Moral Psychology and Character
{: #moral-psychology }

The Qur'an closely examines human motives, habits, and self-deception. It explains how pride, desire, fear, and social pressure lead people to resist what they know or excuse what they do. These descriptions call readers to examine themselves.

#### Self-deception
{: #self-deception }

| Tendency | Qur'anic description |
| --- | --- |
| **Pride and status** | People transgress when they imagine themselves self-sufficient (96:6–7). The owner of the two gardens treats his wealth as evidence of lasting security (18:34–36). |
| **Envy and resentment** | Joseph's brothers resent their father's affection, plan wrongdoing, and reassure themselves that they will become righteous afterward (12:8–9). They use future repentance to excuse present harm. |
| **Conformity** | People defend inherited practices because their ancestors followed them, even when those ancestors lacked understanding (2:170). Familiarity takes the place of examination. |
| **Fear of poverty and attachment to wealth** | Fear of deprivation obstructs generosity (2:268), while rivalry for possessions distracts people until death (102:1–2). |
| **Hypocrisy and rationalization** | Those causing corruption describe themselves as reformers (2:11–12). Others believe they are doing good while their efforts are misdirected (18:103–104). |
| **Forgetfulness after relief** | A person calls upon God in distress, then resumes life as though they had never called when the distress is removed (10:12). |
| **Ingratitude, despair, and boastfulness** | Loss produces hopelessness and ingratitude; relief produces boastful confidence. The response commended is patience and righteous action (11:9–11). |

See [12:8–9](https://quran.com/12:8-9), [2:170](https://quran.com/2:170), [2:268](https://quran.com/2:268), [18:103–104](https://quran.com/18:103-104), [10:12](https://quran.com/10:12), and [11:9–11](https://quran.com/11:9-11).

These descriptions explain recognizable ways in which people justify wrongdoing and overlook their own faults.

#### How actions shape the heart
{: #actions-and-the-heart }

The Qur'an describes how wrongdoing changes the person who practices it. Repeated acts stain the heart (83:14), broken commitments lead to hardening (5:13), and forgetting God leads to self-forgetfulness (59:19). Choices influence what a person notices, accepts, and is willing to acknowledge. [Qur'an 83:14](https://quran.com/83:14), [5:13](https://quran.com/5:13), and [59:19](https://quran.com/59:19).

Remembrance, repentance, and good conduct restore moral awareness. Those conscious of God regain clarity when temptation touches them (7:201), and sincere repentance opens the way to a changed life (25:70–71). The Qur'an therefore connects understanding with honesty and practice, beginning with examination of one's own heart. [Qur'an 7:201](https://quran.com/7:201) and [25:70–71](https://quran.com/25:70-71).

#### Worship and character
{: #worship-and-character }

Qur'anic worship gives moral principles a regular form of practice. Prayer cultivates remembrance and restraint from wrongdoing (20:14; 29:45). Fasting develops consciousness of God (2:183). Charity purifies (9:103). Pilgrimage requires disciplined conduct and directs shared remembrance toward God (2:197–200). [Qur'an 20:14](https://quran.com/20:14), [29:45](https://quran.com/29:45), [2:183](https://quran.com/2:183), [9:103](https://quran.com/9:103), and [2:197–200](https://quran.com/2:197-200).

The Prophet's ﷺ teaching supplies the detailed practice of these commands, including the five daily prayers. Prayer repeatedly renews attention to God; fasting trains restraint; obligatory charity addresses attachment to wealth; pilgrimage brings people of different backgrounds together in shared worship. [Sahih al-Bukhari 46](https://sunnah.com/bukhari:46).

The Qur'an also judges worship by its effect on conduct. Chapter 107 condemns prayer performed for show alongside neglect of those in need. The practice and the moral responsibility remain connected. [Qur'an 107](https://quran.com/107).

### <span class="num-roman">XV.</span> Historical Impact and Transformation
{: #historical-impact-and-transformation }

1. Within a single generation, the vast majority of Arabs accepted it, including tribes that had fought hardest to suppress it.
1. It has shaped the lives of billions across fourteen centuries. Around two billion Muslims today accept it as scripture.
    > *Pew Research Center*, June 2025: the global Muslim population grew from
    > 1.7 billion in 2010 to 2.0 billion in 2020, reaching 26% of the world's population.
    {: .src }

1. Taken together, the Qur'an's teachings form a complete moral and legal framework that holds justice and mercy in balance and historically guided a flourishing civilization. Its guidance remains directly applicable: what it addressed to seventh-century Arabs still addresses readers today.

1. It gave rise to the second-largest religion on earth, after Christianity—its own predecessor—and the fastest-growing of the major religions.
    > *Pew Research Center* projects that Muslims and Christians will approach numerical parity by 2050, at roughly 2.8 billion and 2.9 billion respectively.
    {: .src }

1. The Prophet and the first hearers of the Qur'an remained committed through thirteen years of persecution in Mecca—mockery, beatings, starvation, boycott, and exile. Renouncing it would have ended their suffering, but they did not.

1. The Qur'an became foundational to Arabic grammar, lexicography, rhetoric, calligraphy, law, theology, philosophy, devotional life, and education.
    > The Qur'an became a foundational reference for the sciences of Arabic grammar, lexicography, rhetoric, and recitation, shaping the formal study of the language for centuries.
    {: .gloss }

1. Its historical influence is extraordinary.
    > A seventh-century Arabic revelation became simultaneously a memorized liturgy, a foundational legal and theological source, a central literary reference point, and a continuously transmitted scripture across peoples, languages, continents, and fourteen centuries.
    {: .gloss }

### <span class="num-roman">XVI.</span> The Qur'an and the Messenger Who Proclaimed It
{: #quran-and-messenger }

The relationship between the Qur'an and the Messenger ﷺ is itself one of the clearest signs that he was transmitting a revelation rather than composing a book of his own.

1. The Qur'an repeatedly presents Prophet Muhammad ﷺ as subject to the revelation rather than in control of it.
    > “When Our clear revelations are recited to them, those who do not expect to meet Us say ˹to the Prophet˺, ‘Bring us a different Qur'an or make some changes in it.’ Say, ‘It is not for me to change it on my own. I only follow what is revealed to me.’” (**Sūrat Yūnus 10:15**), Dr. Mustafa Khattab, *The Clear Quran*
    >
    > <div dir="rtl" lang="ar" class="quran-arabic">﴿وَإِذَا تُتْلَىٰ عَلَيْهِمْ ءَايَاتُنَا بَيِّنَـٰتٍ ۙ قَالَ ٱلَّذِينَ لَا يَرْجُونَ لِقَآءَنَا ٱئْتِ بِقُرْءَانٍ غَيْرِ هَـٰذَآ أَوْ بَدِّلْهُ ۚ قُلْ مَا يَكُونُ لِىٓ أَنْ أُبَدِّلَهُۥ مِن تِلْقَآئِ نَفْسِىٓ ۖ إِنْ أَتَّبِعُ إِلَّا مَا يُوحَىٰٓ إِلَىَّ ۖ إِنِّىٓ أَخَافُ إِنْ عَصَيْتُ رَبِّى عَذَابَ يَوْمٍ عَظِيمٍ ۝١٥﴾ <span class="ayah-ref">سُورَةُ يُونُسَ</span></div>
    {: .quote }

1. It contains an extraordinary threat against the messenger himself were he to fabricate revelation.
    > “Had the Messenger made up something in Our Name, We would have certainly seized him by his right hand, then severed his aorta, and none of you could have shielded him ˹from Us˺!” (**Sūrat al-Ḥāqqah 69:44–47**), Dr. Mustafa Khattab, *The Clear Quran*
    >
    > <div dir="rtl" lang="ar" class="quran-arabic">﴿وَلَوْ تَقَوَّلَ عَلَيْنَا بَعْضَ ٱلْأَقَاوِيلِ ۝٤٤ لَأَخَذْنَا مِنْهُ بِٱلْيَمِينِ ۝٤٥ ثُمَّ لَقَطَعْنَا مِنْهُ ٱلْوَتِينَ ۝٤٦ فَمَا مِنكُم مِّنْ أَحَدٍ عَنْهُ حَـٰجِزِينَ ۝٤٧﴾ <span class="ayah-ref">سُورَةُ الحَاقَّةِ</span></div>
    {: .quote }

1. It permanently records public corrections of him.
    > In Sūrat ʿAbasa 80:1–10 he is corrected for turning away from a blind believer while addressing influential Meccan leaders. In 9:43 he is questioned for granting permission too readily. In 66:1 he is asked why he prohibited for himself what God had made lawful. In 18:23–24 he is corrected regarding speaking confidently about what he would do the next day.
    {: .gloss }
    > These corrections were not hidden in private records. They became part of the Qur'an itself, recited publicly in worship for all time. The man accused of inventing the revelation faithfully transmitted verses that corrected and disciplined him before his own followers.
    {: .gloss }

1. The Qur'an is strikingly non-autobiographical. Muhammad ﷺ is the human messenger through whom the entire text comes, yet the Qur'an does not read like his memoir.
    > It gives no continuous account of his childhood, business career, marriage to Khadijah, genealogy, or most events central to later biographies. The name Muhammad appears four times (3:144; 33:40; 47:2; 48:29), and Ahmad [the praised one] once (61:6). Titles and pronouns also refer to him throughout. The Qur'an's main subjects remain God, earlier prophets, judgment, moral responsibility, and the community's response.
    {: .gloss }

1. The first Muslim community is not portrayed as an unblemished heroic group.
    > The Qur'an preserves disobedience and collapse at Uḥud (3:152–155), panic and misplaced confidence at Ḥunayn (9:25), hypocrisy, desertion, quarrels, and failures of discipline. A purely triumphalist founding document could have erased or softened much of this material; the Qur'an instead makes communal failure part of permanent moral instruction.
    {: .gloss }

1. Personal events enter the text but are repeatedly universalized.
    > The Qur'an consoles the Prophet ﷺ, addresses his household, responds to accusations, and comments on political and military crises. The remarkable feature is therefore not that his circumstances leave no trace. It is that personal and contingent events are repeatedly reframed into principles meant to outlive the event that triggered them.
    {: .gloss }

#### Duties and limits imposed on the Messenger ﷺ
{: #revelation-and-strategic-interest }

The Qur'an places the Prophet ﷺ under obligations that constrain his authority and personal advantage.

1. **His power remains dependent on God.** He is instructed to say that he possesses neither God's treasures nor independent knowledge of the unseen (6:50), and that benefit and harm remain subject to God's will (7:188). His authority has clearly stated limits. [Qur'an 6:50](https://quran.com/6:50) and [7:188](https://quran.com/7:188).

1. **He answers demands for signs as a human messenger.** Opponents demand a spring, gardens, a house of gold, and an ascent into heaven. His instructed response affirms his role as a human messenger (17:90–93). Miracles remain acts of God, beyond an audience's power to demand or the messenger's power to produce at will. [Qur'an 17:90–93](https://quran.com/17:90-93).

1. **He accepts demanding worship and a simple life.** He is called to sustained night prayer in preparation for revelation (73:1–5). His household is offered a choice between worldly enjoyment with an honorable release and commitment to God, His messenger, and the life to come (33:28–29). [Qur'an 73:1–5](https://quran.com/73:1-5) and [33:28–29](https://quran.com/33:28-29).

1. **The community is bound by justice even against its own interests.** Justice remains obligatory against oneself or family, and hatred gives no permission for injustice (4:135; 5:8). The same revelation that grants authority also holds its followers accountable. [Qur'an 4:135](https://quran.com/4:135) and [5:8](https://quran.com/5:8).

These passages reinforce the account of a messenger faithfully transmitting an authority above himself. Their significance rests alongside the wider evidence of his conduct, sacrifices, and message.

#### Historical events and lasting guidance
{: #incidents-and-enduring-principles }

The Qur'an draws lasting instruction from particular events. The accusation in chapter 24 becomes teaching about evidence, rumors, charitable assumptions, and reputational harm (24:11–20). Uḥud becomes a lesson in discipline, attachment to worldly gain, and perseverance (3:152–155). A woman's complaint about her marriage introduces a passage affirming that God heard her and regulating the practice that harmed her (58:1–4). [Qur'an 24:11–20](https://quran.com/24:11-20), [3:152–155](https://quran.com/3:152-155), and [58:1–4](https://quran.com/58:1-4).

Names and background details often come through transmitted accounts, while the Qur'an concentrates on the duties and principles future readers need. Historical context explains the occasion; the guidance applies beyond it.

### <span class="num-roman">XVII.</span> Literary Appraisals and Modern Scholarship
{: #outside-appraisals }

The Qur'an's extraordinary character has not been recognized only by Muslims. Arabists, translators, historians, Christian scholars, and major European literary figures who approached the Qur'an from outside the Muslim tradition have repeatedly remarked on its unique literary form, rhetorical power, beauty, influence, and resistance to translation. Their judgments are especially striking because many of these writers did not accept the Qur'an's claim to divine revelation, and some were openly critical of Islam in other respects.

1. **E. H. Palmer**, Cambridge Arabist and translator of the Qur'an for Oxford's *Sacred Books of the East*, wrote that:

   > “the best of Arab writers has never succeeded in producing anything equal in merit to the Qur'an itself”

   Palmer argued that later attempts at the Qur'anic style appeared laboured beside its spontaneous force. Elsewhere in the same introduction he described early Qur'anic passages as “curt, grand, and often almost sublime” and its language as universally acknowledged as a highly perfected form of Arabic speech.

   > E. H. Palmer, trans., *The Qur'ân*, *Sacred Books of the East*, vols. 6 and 9, Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1880, Introduction.
   > {: .src }

2. **George Sale**, whose 1734 English translation remained enormously influential in Europe for generations, wrote despite his explicitly Christian criticism of Islam:

   > “The style of the Korân is generally beautiful and fluent”

   and described passages concerning God's majesty and attributes as **“sublime and magnificent.”** He further observed that the Qur'an's harmony of expression and rhetorical power helped account for the extraordinary effect it produced upon its first hearers.

   > George Sale, *The Koran, Commonly Called the Alcoran of Mohammed*, 1734, Preliminary Discourse, §III.
   > {: .src }

3. **H. A. R. Gibb**, one of the twentieth century's leading Western scholars of Arabic and Islam, wrote:

   > “As a literary monument the Koran thus stands by itself”

   calling it a production **unique in Arabic literature**, without a predecessor or successor in its particular idiom. He also emphasized the extraordinary freedom and originality with which Qur'anic rhetoric exploits the resources of Arabic syntax.

   > H. A. R. Gibb, *Arabic Literature: An Introduction*, Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1963, p. 36.
   > {: .src }

4. **Arthur J. Arberry**, Cambridge professor of Arabic and one of the most respected English translators of the Qur'an, said that its rhythms establish its:

   > “undeniable claim to rank amongst the greatest literary masterpieces of mankind.”

   Arberry deliberately structured his translation around the Qur'an's changing rhythms because he believed previous translations had failed to convey the rhetorical character of the Arabic original. Oxford University Press describes the Qur'an in connection with his translation as the **“supreme classic of Arabic literature”** and “a unique fusion” of prose and poetry.

   > Arthur J. Arberry, *The Koran Interpreted*, Introduction, 1955.
   > {: .src }

5. **Alfred Guillaume**, Christian Arabist and professor of Arabic and Islamic studies, wrote that the Qur'an:

   > “has a rhythm of peculiar beauty and a cadence that charms the ear.”

   He noted the admiration of Christian Arabs for its style and the widespread recognition of its excellence among Arabists, concluding that within the vast literature of Arabic there is **“nothing to compare with it.”**

   > Alfred Guillaume, *Islam*, Penguin Books, 1954, pp. 73–74.
   > {: .src }

6. **John Alden Williams**, professor of religion and a specialist in Islamic civilization, was equally emphatic about what is lost outside the Arabic original. He wrote:

   > “no translation, however faithful to the meaning, has ever been fully successful.”

   He described Qur'anic Arabic as, by turns, **“striking, soaring, vivid, terrible, tender and breathtaking.”** This is precisely why literary judgments based only on an English rendering can miss much of what Arabic readers are evaluating.

   > John Alden Williams, *Islam*, George Braziller.
   > {: .src }

7. **Neal Robinson**, Qur'anic scholar and Anglican priest, has devoted extensive study to features once dismissed by some Western critics as irregularities. Examining the Qur'an's abrupt shifts of grammatical person and perspective, he concluded that they:

   > “are a very effective rhetorical device.”

   His broader structural analysis likewise argues that apparently non-linear passages can form a **forceful cumulative argument**, reinforced by recurring rhythm and rhyme.

   > Neal Robinson, *Discovering the Qur'an: A Contemporary Approach to a Veiled Text*, 2nd ed., Georgetown University Press, 2003.
   > {: .src }

8. **Émile Dermenghem**, the French historian and biographer of Prophet Muhammad ﷺ, drew particular attention to the effect of Qur'anic recitation. In the English translation of *The Life of Mahomet*, he is cited describing:

   > “Its literary beauty, its irradiation, an enigma even today”

   and remarking on its power to produce religious fervour even in people who are otherwise not especially devout.

   > Émile Dermenghem, *The Life of Mahomet*, trans. Arabella Yorke, London: George Routledge, 1930, p. 249.
   > {: .src }

9. **Edward Gibbon**, the great Enlightenment historian and a famously skeptical critic of organized religion, nevertheless wrote in *The History of the Decline and Fall of the Roman Empire*:

   > “the Koran is a glorious testimony to the unity of God.”

   He particularly admired the uncompromising conception of one eternal and transcendent Creator that runs throughout it.

   > Edward Gibbon, *The History of the Decline and Fall of the Roman Empire*, ch. 50.
   > {: .src }

10. **Thomas Carlyle** is an especially revealing case because he did **not** enjoy reading Sale's English translation of the Qur'an and criticized it harshly. Yet after working through it, he still concluded:

    > “Sincerity, in all senses, seems to me the merit of the Koran”

    and acknowledged within it **“a vein of true direct insight”** approaching poetry. Carlyle's reluctant concession is arguably more significant than uncomplicated praise: despite his aversion to the translated literary form, he found something powerful and genuine running through the text.

    > Thomas Carlyle, *On Heroes, Hero-Worship, and the Heroic in History*, Lecture II, “The Hero as Prophet,” 1841.
    > {: .src }

11. **Johann Wolfgang von Goethe**, one of the greatest figures in German literature, studied the Qur'an repeatedly and had a complex attitude toward it. Although he criticized its repetitions, he also described its style as:

    > “stern, grand, terrible ... truly sublime”

    and wrote that repeated engagement with the book moves the reader from resistance to astonishment and ultimately reverence. He predicted that the Qur'an would continue exercising a powerful influence through the ages.

    > Johann Wolfgang von Goethe, *Notes and Essays for a Better Understanding of the West-Eastern Divan*, 1819.
    > {: .src }

The significance of these testimonies is that qualities central to the Muslim claim about the Qur'an have repeatedly forced themselves upon readers who did **not** begin from that belief. Across very different periods and intellectual backgrounds, outside observers have independently remarked upon its unique place in Arabic literature, its rhythm and rhetorical force, its unusual effect when recited, the difficulty of reproducing it in translation, its powerful proclamation of God's unity, and its extraordinary historical influence.

That agreement is particularly striking because these writers were not simply repeating Muslim devotional language. Sale, Palmer, Gibb, Guillaume, Robinson, Carlyle, Goethe, and others disagreed sharply with Islam on various questions. Yet even criticism repeatedly gave way to acknowledgment that the Qur'an was not an ordinary literary phenomenon.

Modern scholarship provides detailed studies of the Qur'an's composition, language, self-description, and transmission. These works contribute specific findings to the subjects examined here; their authors hold differing religious and historical views.

### <span class="num-roman">XVIII.</span> Alternative Explanations
{: #alternative-quranic-authorship }

The case for revelation includes comparison with the strongest explanations of human authorship. Each must account for the same evidence: the Qur'an's literary qualities, message, historical setting, transmission, and relationship with the Prophet ﷺ.

#### The evidence an explanation must address
{: #authorship-evidence }

The Qur'an combines a distinctive literary form, consistent theology, engagement with earlier traditions, practical guidance, predictions, and authority over its own messenger. Islamic transmission places its public recitation across twenty-three years, and material evidence establishes its early written history.

These features are examined through their relevant sources. The text provides direct evidence of wording and structure. Manuscripts and inscriptions establish historical facts. Reports supply details of chronology and biography. Literary comparison and assessment of the whole record support the judgment about authorship.

| Explanation | Proposal | What it needs to explain |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Extraordinary human genius** | An exceptional speaker produces a distinctive style and coherent worldview through oral composition. | The full combination of literary quality, sustained teaching, authority over the messenger, and predictions established before their fulfillment. Naming an exceptional capacity still requires an account of how it explains these features together. |
| **Adaptation of Jewish and Christian traditions** | Earlier narratives and teachings reach the Prophet ﷺ through written or oral transmission. | The relevant sources, channels of contact, chronology, and integration into the Qur'an's Arabic composition. The explanation must address its theological differences and repeated reshaping of shared material. |
| **Private teachers** | One or more people provide sustained instruction. | Evidence of the teachers' knowledge, access, period of involvement, and contribution to particular passages. The accusations recorded in 16:103 and 25:4–5 establish that the charge was made; demonstrating the proposed teaching requires further evidence. |
| **Collaborative authorship** | Several contributors supply expertise, with a principal speaker or editor maintaining unity. | Evidence of contributors and their work, together with an account of the Qur'an's public delivery, continuous voice, and early textual history. |
| **Later editing or fabrication** | Subsequent editors produce the arrangement, coherence, or predictions after the events. | The early manuscript tradition and seventh-century documentary evidence strongly constrain extensive late composition. Proposals about editing within the earliest period require specific textual and historical evidence. |
| **Sincere human religious experience** | The Prophet ﷺ understands his experiences as revelation while expressing them in human language. | The content and claimed signs of the message, alongside his sincerity. Assessing this explanation requires literary and historical evidence; a psychiatric label supplies neither. |

These proposals may overlap. Combining them still requires a coherent account supported by evidence. The relevant details appear under [preservation](#early-textual-closure), [literary comparison](#arabic-comparisons), and [contemporary objections](#reason-and-scrutiny).

#### The requirements of a borrowing theory
{: #borrowing-hypothesis }

A historical account of borrowing must establish how the material reached the Prophet ﷺ and how it became part of the Qur'an.

1. **Identify the source.** Establish the relevant version of a story or teaching and its date. A close sequence of distinctive details provides stronger evidence of dependence than a widely shared religious theme.

1. **Establish the channel.** Direct use of a written source requires access to its language or a mediator. Oral transmission requires a plausible setting and route of contact.

1. **Fit the chronology.** The proposed contact must account for the range of material and the period over which it appeared. A brief encounter supports a more limited claim than sustained instruction.

1. **Explain the transformation.** The account must address the Qur'an's selection, compression, theological disagreements, and repeated adaptation of related narratives to different contexts.

Arabia belonged to a wider religious world, and the Qur'an itself claims continuity with earlier revelation. The historical question concerns what particular similarities establish about dependence. Tommaso Tesei's study distinguishes knowledge of specific writings from the circulation of themes through that wider setting. [“Echoes of Pseudepigrapha in the Qur'ān”](https://doi.org/10.1017/9789048540105.009).

#### Fabrication and personal interest
{: #fabrication-expectations }

Theories of deliberate fabrication often appeal to comfort, unrestricted authority, or personal glorification. Several features of the Qur'an run against those motives.

| Expected advantage | Qur'anic evidence |
| --- | --- |
| Conceal personal or communal failures | Corrections of the messenger and community remain in the text (80:1–10; 3:152–155). |
| Control the message | The messenger denies authority to change it on his own (10:15). |
| Claim unrestricted supernatural power | He declares his dependence on God and answers demands for signs as a human messenger (7:188; 17:90–93). |
| Center the book on its author | The text concentrates on God, earlier prophets, moral responsibility, and judgment. |
| Avoid commitments that could disappoint followers | The text includes the Roman prediction and the promise of secure entry into the Sacred Mosque (30:2–4; 48:27). Their force depends on establishing their dates and fulfillment. |

An explanation that treats self-restraint as a strategy for gaining credibility must fit the wider record of the Prophet's ﷺ life and message. These features gain their force together with the evidence of his sacrifices, conduct, and teaching.

#### Revelation as the explanation
{: #revelation-as-explanation }

Revelation gives a unified account of the Qur'an's literary character, consistent guidance, signs, and authority over its messenger. Together with the evidence for God and the Prophet's ﷺ life, these features support the conclusion that the Qur'an is what it declares itself to be: revelation from God. The case rests on this positive evidence and its explanatory strength when compared with the alternatives.

### <span class="num-roman">XIX.</span> The Cumulative Case
{: #quran-cumulative-case }

The case for the Qur'an's divine origin rests on the extraordinary convergence of its features. A text publicly proclaimed over a long and turbulent prophetic career:

- develops a highly distinctive and analyzable literary discourse;
- repeatedly integrates sound, syntax, structure, narrative, and meaning;
- reshapes repeated stories according to local rhetorical goals;
- openly argues with its opponents and invites scrutiny;
- repeatedly places its messenger beneath rather than above the revelation;
- permanently records corrections of that messenger and failures of the first Muslim community;
- makes historically risky claims about future events;
- enters the documentary record extremely early;
- is traceable through overlapping written and oral transmission channels;
- engages earlier religious traditions while maintaining a distinct theological program;
- develops a broad moral and epistemic worldview across changing circumstances;
- and remains a continuously recited and memorized scripture in its original language.

Together, these features strongly support the Qur'an's own explanation: revelation from God. Its literary character, guidance, predictions, preservation, and relationship with the Messenger ﷺ form a coherent case. Their combined explanatory force is assessed against the same evidence required of alternative accounts.

#### How the evidence fits together
{: #independence-of-evidence }

Each line of evidence contributes to a different part of the case. Assessing their relationships gives the combined argument its proper weight.

| Evidence | Contribution | Relationship to other evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Manuscripts and documents** | Establish early wording, distribution, and public use. | Related manuscripts trace transmission from a shared ancestor. |
| **Recitation and teaching** | Preserve recognized readings through learning, correction, and worship. | Oral and written transmission reinforce and influence one another. |
| **Literary analysis** | Examines wording, sound, structure, and meaning. | Rhyme, memorability, and emotional effect are connected qualities, assessed through close comparison. |
| **Predictions** | Support knowledge beyond ordinary expectation when their timing and fulfillment are established. | Their force depends on the date of proclamation and independent evidence of the outcome. |
| **The Prophet's ﷺ conduct** | Establishes evidence about sincerity, motives, and fidelity to the message. | Biographical reports require assessment. Corrections preserved in the Qur'an remain evidence from the text itself. |
| **Theology and guidance** | Demonstrate the scope, continuity, and coherence of the message. | Their significance for authorship is assessed alongside the literary, historical, and personal evidence. |

Preservation gives access to the text. Literary analysis examines its qualities. History establishes its setting and tests its predictions. Its guidance and the messenger's life further support the judgment about its source. Overlapping observations are weighed together, with independent evidence adding distinct support.

#### Criteria for reassessment
{: #what-would-weaken-the-case }

The invitation to scrutiny applies to the arguments on this page. The following findings, if established, would require reassessment:

1. **Extensive later composition or rewriting.** Secure evidence that central parts of the Qur'an were composed well after the prophetic period would challenge their attribution to the Prophet's ﷺ proclamation. Early variants are assessed by their actual extent and significance.

1. **A demonstrated history of human authorship.** Reliable evidence identifying sources, teachers, or collaborators and explaining the production of the text would strengthen that account. The requirement is a documented connection between the proposed contributors and the text.

1. **A successful answer to the literary challenge.** An Arabic composition that meets the same stated criteria under sustained critical comparison would challenge the claim of inimitability. The criteria apply equally regardless of the author's beliefs.

1. **A failed prediction.** A securely dated, unambiguous prediction whose stated outcome failed to occur would count against the text's reliability. Timing and meaning must be assessed consistently.

1. **An established contradiction or error.** A contradiction requires incompatible claims in the same relevant sense and context. Assessing it requires the Arabic, genre, scope, and chronology, with interpretations supported by evidence.

Individual arguments are corrected or withdrawn when their premises fail. Their effect on the overall case depends on their importance and on which other claims rely upon them. A mistaken supporting example has a different significance from evidence against a central claim of revelation.

## The Messenger of God ﷺ

Everything described above was recited by one man who could neither read nor write, over the course of twenty-three years in seventh-century Arabia. But the Qur'an cannot be separated from the man who delivered it.

The two lines of evidence therefore reinforce one another. The extraordinary qualities of the Qur'an came through a man whose character, life, and message were themselves extraordinary. Taken together, they point overwhelmingly toward the same conclusion: that Prophet Muhammad ﷺ was what he claimed to be—a true prophet of God.

His life presents a remarkable case of its own. The man through whom the Qur'an came was known for his integrity, endured years of persecution and sacrifice for his message, lived according to what he preached, and left behind teachings and an example that transformed Arabia and shaped the lives of generations after him. His life was also preserved in extraordinary detail and rigor through reports from his family, companions, servants, followers, and even opponents.

### Was Muhammad ﷺ Truly a Prophet?
{: #was-muhammad-truly-a-prophet }

Broadly speaking, there are five possible explanations for Prophet Muhammad ﷺ's life: He was either a conscious fraud, sincerely mistaken, inspired by an evil source, turned into a prophet by later legend, or he was what he claimed to be: a messenger of God. His life, message, and the historical record make the first four explanations increasingly difficult to sustain.

### <span class="num-roman">I.</span> The Fraud Hypothesis
{: #the-fraud-hypothesis }

Fraud usually serves some gain: wealth, status, comfort, power, or admiration. Muhammad's mission brought him years of ridicule, isolation, persecution, and danger. He continued preaching when compromise would have been easier.

The Qur'an also preserves public corrections of him. It rebukes him for turning away from a blind man while speaking to Meccan leaders (Qur'an 80:1–10) and questions his decision to permit some men to stay behind from an expedition (9:43). A religious impostor controlling his own scripture would have little reason to preserve revelations that diminish his image before his followers.

Success did not turn him into a king. Early reports describe a simple household, periods of scarcity, and a life far removed from luxury. His conduct does not resemble a man who invented revelation to enrich or glorify himself.

The evidence on his motives, his household, and the Qur'an's corrections of him is collected under [the competing explanations](/messenger/claim), [what those closest to him saw](/messenger/character), and [the Qur'an he delivered](/messenger/quran).

### <span class="num-roman">II.</span> Sincere, but Mistaken
{: #sincere-but-mistaken }

A sincere delusion can produce conviction, but Muhammad's case involved more than conviction.

For twenty-three years, the Qur'an addressed theology, ethics, law, worship, family life, war and peace, earlier prophets, personal crises, and changing political circumstances while maintaining a stable religious core: worship God alone, live morally, prepare for judgment, defend the weak, repent, forgive, give charity, and resist pride.

Muhammad also showed sustained practical judgment. He negotiated treaties, resolved disputes, led a community, delegated authority, altered strategy when circumstances changed, and distinguished personal opinion from revelation. The message repeatedly imposed difficult duties on him, corrected him, and constrained his own preferences.

A supposed delusion that remained coherent across decades, accompanied effective leadership, and repeatedly disciplined the person experiencing it is possible in principle, but poorly fits the evidence.

For the range and consistency of the teaching, and for his conduct as a leader, see [worship and the inner life](/messenger/worship), [work and public responsibility](/messenger/work-and-authority), and [his life through hardship and power](/messenger/life).

### <span class="num-roman">III.</span> Devil-Inspired
{: #devil-inspired }

The Qur'an relentlessly condemns Satan, idolatry, arrogance, dishonesty, exploitation, sexual immorality, and injustice. It commands prayer, charity, humility, repentance, forgiveness, care for parents and orphans, moral accountability, and worship of the Creator alone.

An evil source would therefore have produced a movement centered on rejecting evil worship, restraining vice, resisting spiritual pride, condemning Satan, and directing human beings toward repentance and obedience to God. Such an explanation works against the moral and theological direction of the message itself.

What the message actually commands is set out at length under [worship and the inner life](/messenger/worship), [justice and restraint in conflict](/messenger/justice), and [care for the vulnerable and human dignity](/messenger/human-dignity).

### <span class="num-roman">IV.</span> A Later Fabricated Legend
{: #a-later-fabricated-legend }

The Qur'an was preserved through both public recitation and writing. It was memorized across the Muslim community, taught from teacher to student, recited in worship, and copied in written form. These overlapping channels limited the possibility of a later authority quietly replacing the text across geographically separated communities.

The Birmingham Qur'an folios preserve parts of Surahs 18–20 in an early Hijazi script. The parchment was radiocarbon dated to 568–645 CE with 95.4% probability, and the University of Birmingham places the manuscript in the mid-seventh century, within decades of Muhammad's death. Manuscript specialist Marijn van Putten has also argued that shared, highly specific spellings across numerous early Qur'an manuscripts point back to a single written archetype in the first Islamic century.

Modern critical historians likewise place Muhammad and the Qur'an firmly in the seventh century. Sean W. Anthony treats the Qur'an as the earliest and strongest literary witness to the historical Muhammad and draws on seventh-century inscriptions, documentary evidence, and non-Muslim writings that independently attest an Arabian prophetic movement associated with him. Gregor Schoeler's work supports an early culture in which reports about Muhammad circulated through both oral teaching and written notes.

Hadith transmission developed its own critical machinery. Reports circulated with named chains of transmitters, often through multiple routes. Scholars compared chains and textual variants, examined whether transmitters had met, assessed memory and honesty, compiled biographical dictionaries, and classified reports as strong, weak, or fabricated. Modern isnad-cum-matn analysis has shown that some traditions can be traced substantially earlier than the surviving canonical collections.

Muhammad's life and message are anchored in first-century scripture, manuscripts, inscriptions, outside testimony, and traceable streams of transmission. They do not first appear as a polished legend centuries later.

[Sources, transmission, and bibliography](/messenger/sources) sets out how the hadith record was preserved and tested; [Muhammad ﷺ and the Qur'an he delivered](/messenger/quran) covers the text's own preservation.

### <span class="num-roman">V.</span> The Prophetic Explanation
{: #the-prophetic-explanation }

The fraud theory conflicts with Muhammad's sacrifices, austerity, and the Qur'an's corrections of him. The delusion theory fits poorly with decades of coherent teaching, disciplined conduct, and effective leadership. The demonic theory runs against the message's sustained orientation toward God, repentance, justice, and moral reform. The legend theory is incompatible with the early documentary and transmission evidence.

Muhammad's own explanation remains: the source of the message was God.

His claim was not confined to a private experience or a brief burst of enthusiasm. He lived under it through persecution and victory, weakness and power, grief and public responsibility. His character, the content of the Qur'an, the structure of his mission, and the early preservation of the sources converge more naturally on sincere prophethood than on fraud, delusion, demonic inspiration, or later invention.

Belief in his prophethood can therefore rest on a cumulative historical and rational judgment: the prophetic explanation accounts for the evidence more coherently than its principal alternatives.

That cumulative case is laid out under [the competing explanations](/messenger/claim), with the harder-to-weigh evidence kept separate in [predictions](/messenger/predictions) and [reported miracles](/messenger/miracles). [Encounters, changed minds, and later appraisals](/messenger/encounters) collects what people who met him said.
