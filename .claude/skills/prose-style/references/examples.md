# Examples

The strongest passages on the site, with footnote markers removed. Follow their voice, not their words.

An inference drawn from a fact, so the reader sees the reasoning work:

> The liable group closely resembled the group from which a pre-modern state could demand military service. If the tax had simply been imposed on people for being non-Muslim, there would be no obvious reason to exempt women, children, monks, the elderly, the poor, and those unable to fight.

A concession stated in three words, then the turn:

> Islam permits a man to have up to four wives at one time, while a woman may have only one husband. The asymmetry is real. But it does not follow that Islam values men more, or that polygyny exists simply to satisfy male desire.

Facing the hardest form of an objection without apology:

> Nine is plainly young by modern standards. But the number does not carry the same social and developmental meaning in every age.

Confidence matched to the evidence, with each strand given its true weight:

> Good character alone does not prove revelation: a sincere person can still be mistaken. Predictions can vary in specificity; miracle reports depend on their transmission; and literary power involves judgment. No strand should be asked to prove more than it can.

A long list of specifics that earns a short verdict:

> That is why classical Islamic law surrounded the *hudud* with unusually strict restraints: demanding standards of proof, penalties for unsupported accusations, discouragement of confession, prohibitions on spying into private wrongdoing, avoidance of fixed penalties where serious doubt existed, recognition of repentance in certain cases, and attention to necessity and social conditions. The penalties were therefore difficult to impose and historically rare. Enforcing their severity while discarding these safeguards does not preserve the classical Islamic legal structure; it distorts it.

A contrast used because the reasoning depends on it:

> Conquest explains the spread of Muslim rule; it does not, by itself, explain the spread of Muslim belief.

Facts carrying the argument, with the crux last:

> It also condemned Abū Lahab, one of the Prophet's ﷺ fiercest enemies, while he was still alive. He had years in which an outward profession of faith could have been used to mock the revelation, yet he died an enemy of Islam.

Conditions set out plainly, then the turn:

> The Qur'an was delivered through public oral recitation over twenty-three years by a man who could neither read nor write and had no prior literary career. Its passages came separately, often in response to unforeseen events and outside their final order, without the ordinary process of private drafting and revision.
>
> Yet admirers and opponents alike have regarded it as the summit of Arabic eloquence, in a mode of expression that is neither prose nor poetry and was previously unknown to the language.

A witness's position doing the persuading:

> Heraclius was questioning an enemy rather than a follower, and Abu Sufyan later admitted that he would have liked to say something damaging if he could.

A concrete cost in one line:

> Military service also carried a cost that cannot be reduced to a tax rate: the risk of injury or death.
