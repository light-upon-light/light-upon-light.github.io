# Voice profile

This is the project-specific file in the skill: the voice of this site, which defends and presents Islam to people who may not share its beliefs.

## Readers

Skeptics, curious seekers, and educated generalists. Write for a reader who is intelligent and willing to follow an argument, but who:

- may not share the writer's beliefs, so never presume agreement;
- may not know Arabic, Islamic law, classical theology, hadith methodology, or the history, so explain the context briefly and define every technical term on first use in each major section, since readers arrive through links and tables of contents;
- is reading by choice and will leave if bored, confused, or condescended to.

They are not your friend, your student, or your opponent, and the prose should treat them as none of the three.

## Register: dignified and measured

Eloquent yet plain, easy to follow and digest. Write with sincerity, not manufactured enthusiasm. Plain does not mean casual. Clarity comes from sentence structure and from putting the point where the reader can find it, not from swapping precise words for everyday ones: a text "was delivered," it did not "come"; a practice "developed through stages," it did not "come in stages."

## Tone: confident and affirmative

State conclusions plainly and say where an opposing view goes wrong. The confidence comes from precision and evidence, not from emphasis. Firm is not the same as heated. Present points and examples as they are, without dramatizing them and without a trail of caveats.

Do:

- State a conclusion as a conclusion.
- Grant what is true in an objection by stating it, and answer at once: "The asymmetry is real. But it does not follow that Islam values men more."
- Name the specific flaw: an ambiguous term, a missing premise, a step that does not follow.
- Keep the temperature low and the claims exact.

Avoid:

- Hedging a claim the reasoning supports ("it may perhaps be suggested that...").
- Assurance words in place of reasons ("clearly," "obviously," "any honest person can see").
- Triumphalism ("this decisively refutes," "checkmate," "case closed").
- Mockery, sarcasm, or any comment on opponents' motives or intelligence.
- Softening a claim to seem agreeable, or apologizing for it.
- Announcing fairness or seriousness instead of showing it ("This objection deserves a careful answer," "To be fair," "Nothing here denies that...").

Calibration, for the claim that Islam was spread by the sword:

- Too soft: "While some might feel that conquest perhaps played a role, it is certainly understandable that others see the matter differently."
- Too hot: "This is a lazy slander that no serious historian should entertain."
- Right: "Conquest explains the spread of Muslim rule; it does not, by itself, explain the spread of Muslim belief."

## Person

- Use **"you"** for the reader, especially when inviting the reader to test a claim.
- Use **"we"** only inclusively, meaning the writer and reader together or people generally, and check that it includes a skeptical reader.
- Do not use **"I."**
- Do not use an **authorial "we"** ("we will argue," "we now turn to," "as we have shown"). The site has no narrating persona. State claims directly.

## Names and honorifics

- Always write ﷺ after the Prophet's name or title.
- Spell it Qur'an.
- Never invent a quotation, source, consensus, or historical fact. Never write Qur'anic Arabic or a hadith number from memory; see `_notes/guides/citations_guide.md`.

## Spelling and punctuation

- **American spelling:** analyze, behavior, defense, skeptic, center, judgment, toward, favor.
- **American quotation style:** double quotation marks; periods and commas inside them; single marks only for a quotation inside a quotation.
- **Serial comma:** "premises, inferences, and conclusions."
- **Em dashes:** unspaced and used rarely.
- **Semicolons:** allowed to join two closely related clauses. Never two in one sentence, rarely two in one paragraph.
- **Colons:** good for introducing an explanation or the thing a sentence has just promised.
- **Exclamation marks:** none.
- **Rhetorical questions:** avoid them. If one is truly the clearest form, answer it at once, and never use several in a row.

## Emphasis and formatting inside prose

- **Italics:** rarely, for stress that word order cannot supply, for words discussed as words, for transliterated terms, and for book titles.
- **Bold:** none inside running prose. Bold is reserved for lead-in labels that open a paragraph or list item (`**A note on terms.**`), and citations keep their existing `**Sūrat …**` convention inside blockquotes. No all caps.
- **Numbers:** spell out zero through one hundred and round numbers in running prose. Use numerals for dates, statistics, measurements, and citations.

## Quotations

- Introduce quotations with plain verbs: "says," "writes," "argues," "states." Do not rotate inflated substitutes ("opines," "posits," "avers") for variety.
- Fit each quotation into the grammar of the sentence, and keep it short, except that Qur'anic quotations are never cut or shortened.
