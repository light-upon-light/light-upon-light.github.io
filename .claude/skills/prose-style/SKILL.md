# Writing Style Guide

How prose on this site should read, for anyone writing it, person or model. The
guide is self-contained, so a writer who cannot open files can be given it whole.
How to build an argument and handle sources is in `_notes/guides/methodolody_guide.md`, and
markup is in `_notes/guides/formatting_guide.md`.

## The voice

You are a careful person who has looked into something closely and is telling
an intelligent stranger what you found. That stranger is sceptical, owes you
nothing, and can stop reading at any point. They are not your friend, your
student, or your opponent, and the prose should treat them as none of the three.

The register is dignified and measured: eloquent yet plain, confident and
affirmative, easy to follow and digest. Write with sincerity, not manufactured
enthusiasm. Write like a thoughtful person, not a template.

## Principles

**State the point directly.** Say the claim, the evidence, or the inference
itself. Test every sentence: is it conveying information or reasoning, or is it
framing, signalling, or landing a rhythm? If the latter, cut it.

**Make the point clear early, and let the crux land last.** A reader should know
early what a paragraph is about. Where evidence builds toward a conclusion, the
conclusion comes after it.

**Reason in the open.** Show why a fact supports a claim. Words like therefore,
thus, but, yet, despite, unlike, even, and rather than state how ideas relate.
They are part of the argument and should be used wherever the relation is real.
So is a colon that ties a claim to its evidence.

**Be concrete.** Prefer a named person, event, place, date, number, or verse to a
general description. One specific example persuades more than a list of
abstractions.

**Let the facts carry the argument.** Do not tell the reader what to think of a
fact or why it matters. Give the fact in the position where its force is plain.
Judgments about the evidence itself are substance and stay: "The source is weak."
"The requirement is unusually demanding."

**Match confidence to the evidence.** State claims strongly when the evidence
supports them, and do not weaken a clear claim to sound neutral or cautious. Use
remarkable, striking, or astonishing when the evidence earns it. Do not overstate
either: no claim should be asked to prove more than it can.

**Concede plainly, once.** When something is true and inconvenient, say so in a
short sentence and move on. Do not stack caveats, sound apologetic, or announce
how fair you are being. Keep the fairness and strip the announcement of it.

**Keep the register up.** Use ordinary English, but never at the cost of precision
or dignity. Where a formal word is the exact one, use it.

| Write | Not |
| --- | --- |
| was delivered | came |
| stated | said |
| simultaneously | at once |
| developed through stages | came in stages |

**Use one word for one idea.** The terms an argument is built on are not
interchangeable. On the Qur'an page, "convergence" is not "range", "transcends" is
not "avoids", and "piecemeal" is not "in fragments".

**Write for someone new to the subject.** Do not assume the reader knows Arabic,
Islamic law, hadith methodology, or the history. Explain the context briefly.
Define each Arabic or technical term on first use in each major section. Keep
paragraphs short, with one main idea each.

**Be concise without losing logic.** Remove verbal overhead: repetition, setup,
and explanation that adds no meaning. Do not remove premises, inferences, or the
words that join them.

**Use natural subjects.** Keep what the point is about as the subject of the
sentence, and do not contort a sentence to do so.

## What to cut

Writers, and models especially, drift toward these.

**Meta-commentary.** Sentences or tail clauses about the article, the writer, the
reader, or how convincing a point is. The tail form hides inside sentences that
otherwise read well.

| Cut | Prefer |
| --- | --- |
| This is an important point because it changes how the evidence should be understood. | The evidence changes the interpretation substantially. |
| It is worth emphasizing that the requirement is unusually demanding. | The requirement is unusually demanding. |
| The weakness of this source should be acknowledged. | The source is weak. |
| But it does show something important: the lawfulness of polygyny did not make Fatima's hurt irrelevant. | But the report still shows that the lawfulness of polygyny did not make Fatima's hurt irrelevant. |

**Self-congratulatory pointers.** "Worth reading twice", "the one line worth
keeping".

**"Not X, but Y" as a mannerism.** Keep the construction when the reasoning depends
on distinguishing X from Y. Across a page, the habit becomes a tic. The same goes
for chiastic reversals.

**Aphorisms and rhythmic cascades.** Tricolons for effect, four-beat closing
sentences, throwaway zingers.

**Writing for effect.** Invented scenes, rhetorical questions, manufactured
intimacy, drama, hype, and sentences built mainly to sound impressive.

**Added force.** Do not reach for "even", "yet", or an intensifier where the facts
do not make the contrast themselves.

## Mechanics

- Em dashes are unspaced and used rarely.
- No bold inside running prose. Bold is reserved for lead-in labels that open a
  paragraph or list item (`**A note on terms.**`), and citations keep their
  existing `**Sūrat …**` convention inside blockquotes.
- Always write ﷺ after the Prophet's name or title. Spell it Qur'an.
- Never invent a quotation, source, consensus, or historical fact. Never write
  Qur'anic Arabic or a hadith number from memory.

## Examples

The strongest passages on the site, with footnote markers removed. Follow their
voice, not their words.

An inference drawn from a fact, so the reader sees the reasoning work:

> The liable group closely resembled the group from which a pre-modern state could demand military service. If the tax had simply been imposed on people for being non-Muslim, there would be no obvious reason to exempt women, children, monks, the elderly, the poor, and those unable to fight.

A concession stated in three words, then the turn:

> Islam permits a man to have up to four wives at one time, while a woman may have only one husband. The asymmetry is real. But it does not follow that Islam values men more, or that polygyny exists simply to satisfy male desire.

Facing the hardest form of an objection without apology:

> Nine is plainly young by modern standards. But the number does not carry the same social and developmental meaning in every age.

Confidence matched to the evidence, with each strand given its true weight:

> Good character alone does not prove revelation: a sincere person can still be mistaken. Predictions can vary in specificity; miracle reports depend on their transmission; and literary power involves judgment. No strand should be asked to prove more than it can.

A long list of specifics that earns a short verdict:

> That is why classical Islamic law surrounded the *hudud* with unusually strict restraints: demanding standards of proof, penalties for unsupported accusations, discouragement of confession, prohibitions on spying into private wrongdoing, avoidance of fixed penalties where serious doubt existed, recognition of repentance in certain cases, and attention to necessity and social conditions. The penalties were therefore difficult to impose and historically rare. Enforcing their severity while discarding these safeguards does not preserve the classical Islamic legal structure; it distorts it.

A contrast used because the reasoning depends on it:

> Conquest explains the spread of Muslim rule; it does not, by itself, explain the spread of Muslim belief.

Facts carrying the argument, with the crux last:

> It also condemned Abū Lahab, one of the Prophet's ﷺ fiercest enemies, while he was still alive. He had years in which an outward profession of faith could have been used to mock the revelation, yet he died an enemy of Islam.

Conditions set out plainly, then the turn:

> The Qur'an was delivered through public oral recitation over twenty-three years by a man who could neither read nor write and had no prior literary career. Its passages came separately, often in response to unforeseen events and outside their final order, without the ordinary process of private drafting and revision.
>
> Yet admirers and opponents alike have regarded it as the summit of Arabic eloquence, in a mode of expression that is neither prose nor poetry and was previously unknown to the language.

A witness's position doing the persuading:

> Heraclius was questioning an enemy rather than a follower, and Abu Sufyan later admitted that he would have liked to say something damaging if he could.

A concrete cost in one line:

> Military service also carried a cost that cannot be reduced to a tax rate: the risk of injury or death.

## When rewriting existing text

Everything above applies, with these additions.

A rewrite is not a summary. Preserve every distinct premise, inference,
counterexample, concession, causal link, qualification, and response to an
objection. When a sentence contains both meta-commentary and reasoning, remove the
commentary and keep the inference in plain language. When unsure whether something
is substantive, keep it.

Work on a small unit at a time. Passing over a whole section at once misses most
of the problems and flattens the rest.

Change wording only where the change is an improvement. A near-synonym is not one.
Keep each claim exactly as strong as it was, and add no contrast, dash, or
intensifier the original lacks. These changes all overstated or padded:

- "including tribes that had fought hardest" became "even tribes"
- "and though they had both the skill" became "yet though"
- "Revealed over the span of 23 years" became "Revealed gradually over…"
- "unforeseen events" became "events that could not be foreseen"

Do not move a payoff to the front, or split a colon that ties a claim to its
evidence into separate sentences.

These rewrites improved the text without changing its claim:

| Before | After | What it fixed |
| --- | --- | --- |
| a text regarded by admirers and opponents alike as the summit of Arabic eloquence, by a far margin | a text that admirers and opponents alike regarded as by far the summit of Arabic eloquence | an awkward trailing phrase |
| because the civilizations involved had long since disappeared and their own records could no longer be read | because they concern civilizations that had long since disappeared and whose own records could no longer be read | a vague noun phrase |
| compare it with evidence | compare these details with evidence | an unclear "it" |
| or earlier. That date falls within a generation | or earlier, a date within a generation | a needless sentence break |
