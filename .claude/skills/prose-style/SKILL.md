# Prose Style

This skill governs how prose reads at the scale of words, sentences, and paragraphs. How to build an argument and handle sources is in `_notes/guides/methodolody_guide.md`; markup is in `_notes/guides/formatting_guide.md`. Whatever the structure, every sentence inside it should follow this guide.

**Before writing, read** `references/voice-profile.md`, which sets the readers, tone, register, and mechanics, and `references/examples.md`, which shows them at their best. **Before changing existing text, also read** `references/rewriting.md`.

A writer who cannot open files, such as an isolated model call, needs this file and those references supplied in its prompt.

## The ideal

Write as someone who has looked into something closely and is showing it to an intelligent reader who has not seen it yet. That reader is your equal in intelligence but not in background knowledge, is skeptical, owes you nothing, and can stop reading at any point. They will follow any step you actually show them, and they will notice any step you skip, blur, or inflate.

The goal is prose that is effortless to read. That takes effort to write. Almost everything a reader experiences as ease (the right word, the obvious next sentence, the point arriving where they expect it) is the product of revision.

When rules conflict, decide in this order:

1. **Accurate.** Never trade truth for rhythm or force.
2. **Clear.** The reader understands it on the first pass.
3. **Precise.** It says exactly what is meant, no more and no less.
4. **Natural.** It sounds like a thoughtful person speaking carefully, not like a document assembling itself.
5. **Graceful.** Rhythm and elegance, when they cost nothing above.

Break any rule here sooner than write something awkward. The rules describe what good prose usually does. They do not replace reading the sentence and hearing whether it works.

## Sentences

**Make the actors the subjects and the actions the verbs.** A sentence is easiest to follow when its subject is the thing doing something and its verb is what that thing does. Nouns built from verbs ("the rejection of," "the existence of") hide the action and make the reader rebuild it.

- Weak: The rejection of the premise by critics is based on a misunderstanding of its scope.
- Better: Critics reject the premise because they misunderstand its scope.

**Put familiar information first and new information last.** Open each sentence with something the reader already has, such as a term just introduced or the subject of the previous sentence. Save the new or important material for the end, where stress falls naturally. This one habit produces most of what readers call flow.

**Reach the main verb quickly.** Keep subjects short and close to their verbs. A long opening clause makes the reader hold everything in suspension.

**Prefer the active voice.** Use the passive when the actor is unknown or irrelevant, or when it lets the sentence begin with familiar information.

**Give each sentence one job.** One main claim and at most one qualification. When a sentence starts collecting "although," "which," and "while" clauses, split it.

**Vary length deliberately.** A run of same-length sentences produces a drone. Put the short sentence where the point needs to land.

**Make every sentence mean one thing.** Reread for ambiguity, especially in pronouns. A bare "this" or "it" with no clear referent is the most common failure; write "this distinction" or "the second premise."

## Words

**Use ordinary English, but never at the cost of precision or dignity.** Write "use," not "utilize"; "about," not "with regard to." Where a formal or technical word is the exact one, use it, and define a technical term the first time.

**Hold terms fixed.** Once a concept has a name, keep that name. The terms an argument is built on are not interchangeable: on the Qur'an page, "convergence" is not "range," "transcends" is not "avoids," and "piecemeal" is not "in fragments." In careful prose, repetition is a courtesy.

**Prefer concrete to abstract.** Give a named person, event, place, date, number, or verse in place of a general description. When a claim is abstract, give an instance.

**Cut what does no work.** "In order to" is "to." "The fact that" can almost always go. "Serves as" is usually "is." Ask of each word what would be lost without it.

**Let precision carry the confidence.** Intensifiers ("very," "extremely," "truly") and assurance words ("clearly," "obviously," "undeniably," "of course") weaken a claim, because they tell the reader to be convinced instead of showing why. Evaluative words are different: "remarkable," "striking," and "astonishing" are claims about the evidence, and are welcome when the evidence earns them.

**Hedge only where the uncertainty is real, and hedge once.** If the evidence is strong, say so without qualification; if it is mixed, say exactly where. Do not stack caveats.

**Avoid stock figures of speech.** Use a figure only when it is fresh and it clarifies, and use few.

## Paragraphs

**One point per paragraph.** A reader should know early what a paragraph is about. If a paragraph makes two points, split it.

**Let the crux land last.** The last sentence lands the point. Where evidence builds toward a conclusion, the conclusion comes after it.

**Link sentences through their content.** The strongest connection between two sentences is a shared term: what ends one sentence begins the next. When a connective is needed, use one that states a logical relation ("because," "so," "but," "therefore") rather than one that merely adds ("moreover," "furthermore," "additionally").

**Keep paragraphs readable.** Most paragraphs run 60 to 150 words. A one-sentence paragraph is deliberate emphasis; used often, it stops working.

**End on substance.** Do not close a paragraph by restating what it just said, or with a quotable moral it has not earned.

## Reasoning and force

**Reason in the open.** Show why a fact supports a claim. Words like therefore, thus, but, yet, despite, unlike, even, and rather than state how ideas relate. They are part of the argument and should be used wherever the relation is real. So is a colon that ties a claim to its evidence.

**Let the facts carry the argument.** Do not tell the reader what to think of a fact or why it matters. Give the fact in the position where its force is plain. Judgments about the evidence itself are substance and stay: "The source is weak." "The requirement is unusually demanding."

**Match confidence to the evidence.** State claims strongly when the evidence supports them, and do not weaken a clear claim to sound neutral or cautious. Do not overstate either: no claim should be asked to prove more than it can.

**Add no force the facts do not supply.** Do not reach for "even," "yet," or an intensifier where the facts do not make the contrast themselves.

**Test every sentence.** Is it conveying information or reasoning, or is it framing, signaling, or landing a rhythm? If the latter, cut it.

## Tone

The voice profile sets the tone. Whatever it says, these hold:

- **Start where the substance starts.** No warm-up, no announcing what the text is about to do. The first sentence of a piece or a paragraph should already be doing work.
- **Write about the subject, not about the discussion.** Sentences that step outside the subject to grade an objection ("This objection deserves a careful answer"), vouch for the writer's fairness ("Nothing here disputes that"), or rate the other side's view ("That response is not foolish") carry no information, and a skeptical reader hears them as performance. Keep the fairness and strip the announcement of it. The test is whether deleting the sentence would lose anything about the subject.
- **Concede plainly, once.** When something is true and inconvenient, state it and answer it at once, without apology.
- **Represent other views in their strongest form,** in terms their holders would accept. A reply to a weak version persuades no one who holds the strong one.
- **No contempt.** Sarcasm, mockery, and sneering cost the trust of every reader who has not yet made up their mind.

## Patterns to cut

Some habits make prose sound machine-written or formulaic, and readers now spot them quickly. Once they spot one, they discount everything around it.

- **Meta-commentary,** whole sentences or tail clauses about the article, the writer, the reader, or how convincing a point is. The tail form hides inside sentences that otherwise read well.
- **Self-congratulatory pointers:** "worth reading twice," "the one line worth keeping."
- **Staged contrast:** "This is not X; it is Y" where no one claimed X. Keep the construction when the reasoning depends on distinguishing X from Y. Across a page, the habit becomes a tic. The same goes for chiastic reversals.
- **Aphorisms and rhythmic cascades:** four-beat closing sentences, throwaway zingers, and reflexive groups of three when two or four things are what actually exist.
- **Writing for effect:** invented scenes, manufactured intimacy, drama, hype, and sentences built mainly to sound impressive.
- **Inflated vocabulary used for its sound:** testament, pivotal, underscore, delve, tapestry, landscape, multifaceted, foster, showcase, navigate (figurative), robust.
- **Dodging "is":** "serves as," "stands as," "functions as."
- **Trailing commentary:** "..., highlighting the importance of..." or "..., reflecting a broader trend."
- **Vague attribution:** "some argue," "many scholars believe," with no one named.
- **Inflated significance and summary closers:** "This raises profound questions about...," "Ultimately," "In conclusion."
- **Mushy balance:** "There are many perspectives," "It is a complex issue," used in place of a conclusion.

Cutting meta-commentary should keep any reasoning inside it:

| Cut | Prefer |
| --- | --- |
| This is an important point because it changes how the evidence should be understood. | The evidence changes the interpretation substantially. |
| It is worth emphasizing that the requirement is unusually demanding. | The requirement is unusually demanding. |
| The weakness of this source should be acknowledged. | The source is weak. |
| But it does show something important: the lawfulness of polygyny did not make Fatima's hurt irrelevant. | But the report still shows that the lawfulness of polygyny did not make Fatima's hurt irrelevant. |

## How to work

Draft for substance first, then revise in passes. Each pass looks for one kind of problem, which catches more than trying to fix everything at once.

1. **Meaning.** Does each sentence say one definite thing? Is any step missing that the reader needs?
2. **Sentences.** Are the subjects the actors and the verbs the actions? Does familiar information come first and the point last?
3. **Words.** Cut what does no work. Replace inflated words. Check that terms stay fixed.
4. **Patterns.** Scan for the patterns listed above, and make a second read for tail-clause commentary and staged contrasts, which hide in sentences that look fine.
5. **Sound.** Read it as if aloud. Where you stumble, the reader will too.

## Final check

Before delivering, confirm:

- Person, register, spelling, and punctuation follow the voice profile.
- Each paragraph makes one point and ends on substance.
- Every technical or Arabic term is defined on first use in each major section and used consistently after.
- No intensifiers, assurance words, or stacked hedges.
- Nothing from the patterns to cut.
- No sentence a reader would need to read twice.
